Thesis Conclusion

> “The ignorance regarding the growth mechanisms of scallops is complete. The scientific community has never truly taken an interest; the fishermen, inheriting no tradition since intensive exploitation is recent, know nothing of the early stages of scallops, which they only encounter as adults when dredging them up. At the beginning [,] there is therefore no direct relationship between the scallop larvae and the fishermen [, and] it is through the mediation of researchers that this link will gradually be constructed.”
>
> **“ELEMENTS FOR A SOCIOLOGY OF TRANSLATION: The Domestication of Scallops and Fishermen in the Bay of Saint-Brieuc,”  
> Michel Callon, 1986, p. 179**

This thesis addresses various issues related to our interest in money and the astonishment provoked by the monetary ambitions of the most prominent promoters of cryptocurrencies (CM). By uniquely reactivating the debate between rule and discretion, these ambitions—characterized as liberal-technocratic—are presented in the form of a syllogism whose validation or refutation of its premises and their connections constitutes the guiding thread of our chapters. This syllogism postulates that, since (i) technology is autonomous and neutral with respect to the social world and (ii) CM are purely technical monies; then (iii) they are immune to human governance and its socio-political interests, which makes them (iv) “better” monies than national currencies. The primary objective of this thesis was to study the CM Bitcoin and Ethereum to contribute to enriching an emerging research field on CM that remains underexplored, particularly within the humanities. We aimed to overcome two pitfalls. The first is the “technologism” predominantly present in analyses—whether native, grey, or academic—that portray CM as purely technical, neutral, autonomous, and stabilized entities by focusing solely on their technical and protocol characteristics. The second is “sociologism,” which, conversely, reduces CM to their libertarian origins. To this end, we approached CM as objects, indeed as socio-technical infrastructures in perpetual recomposition, requiring the restitution of their relational and socio-historical context.

### Thesis Summary

**The first chapter** provided a detailed presentation of our two objects of study, allowing us to explicate: the context and characteristics of their design, the key components and processes of their functioning, their stepwise development, the actors (human or non-human) and domains of development contributing to their infrastructure, as well as the resources and constraints of this development. It is through the restitution of the socio-historical thickness of the emergence of the pioneering Bitcoin first, certain *altcoins* thereafter, and Ethereum finally, that we were able to question the purportedly “technical” apoliticism, neutrality, and autonomy of cryptocurrencies (CM).

We highlighted that the key sociotechnical assemblages of Bitcoin and the typical scenario of its functioning refer back to the singular inspirations and practices of Nakamoto, coupled with the theoretical constraints he encountered, and emphasized that the architectural choices made—particularly its PoW-based consensus associated with the issuance of native units of account (NUA)—were hybrid, negotiated, and therefore political. This original politico-technical entanglement of the protocol became more complex with infrastructural development, since a mere protocol does not constitute a CM without *confrontation* with users. We demonstrated that Bitcoin, as a “seamless web” infrastructure, is reducible neither to the intentions and staging of its designer nor to its protocol boundaries, since it is continuously renegotiated through actors’ improvisations. Thus, we identified three phases—*proof of concept phase*, *sin phase*, *maturation phase*—in its *carnivalesque* development, which gave rise to a variety of arrangements and actors’ work, progressively more numerous, differentiated, and integrated into the monetary and financial system. The presentation of Ethereum concluded the chapter and its demonstration*: the explication of the salient points of the normativity of its assemblages and its own infrastructural development also highlighted, by contrast, the normativity of Bitcoin. For Ethereum’s design added, to Nakamoto’s critiques of the traditional monetary system, critiques of the protocol and infrastructural rigidities of Bitcoin and the *altcoin* experiments that followed it.

**Chapter Two** offers a critical contribution to the controversy surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM). From a position grounded in a monetary institutionalism interested in usages, we assert that they are indeed monies, while rejecting the orthodox monetary perspectives—of homogeneity, uniqueness, and exclusivity—shared both by *coiners* and by their academic and practitioner critics. From this standpoint, CM integrate into the monetary field as a novel form, not due to the absence (presupposed by both sides), but *a contrario* because of the presence of a singular governance with an apparently polycentric nature.

Our literature review has highlighted that two distinct families of approaches—instrumentalist and nominalist/chartalist—share a reified definition of money, reduced to its modern forms. Both approaches relegate cryptocurrencies (CM) to the status of valueless financial assets: according to these perspectives, money must perfectly fulfill the canonical monetary functions within a unitary and exclusive economic space administered by a sovereign center. In contrast, for *non-statist* nominalist approaches based on usages, within which our work is situated, money—both past and present—is neither universal nor exclusive; it is marked and circulates alongside parallel currencies, and the sole discriminating criterion conferring monetary quality is the presence of usage in accounting and payment. We emphasize these usages through the study of practices, notably highlighting that native units of account (NUA) serve a primordial type of usage that is both exclusive and sovereign within their protocol: they alone enable the nominal expression and actual payment of the debt opened by transactional demand, which transaction fees allow to settle. 

Thus, CM, as bearer claims on the issuing protocol that accepts them in payment for the services it offers, are community-type parallel currencies: their monetization within their payment community relies on a fiduciary logic similar to that of national fiat currencies, whose value and purchasing power are guaranteed by institutions and social actors. The examination of the presumed absence of governance in CM—a central issue in the controversy over their monetary quality, supposedly superior compared to fiat currencies—revealed that CM reactivate an old debate on “good” monetary governance, involving ideological stakes (the role of the state and central banks) and material stakes (the risk of erosion of regulatory capacities). 

We oppose a positive vision to the normativity of these approaches, shifting the stakes and formulating the hypothesis that the monetary singularity of CM lies in a polycentric governance revealed by the crises and conflicts agitating their community regarding the desired monetary qualities. CM constitute a novel monetary category, distinct from national and private currencies, founded not on a fiduciary logic of seal or contractual signature, but on a logic of distributed consensus, which establishes itself as a criterion of categorical clarification within the monetary field.

**Chapter Three** examines two cases of crises—the Bitcoin CVE 2018 crisis and the Ethereum *Hard Fork* crisis following the “The DAO” attack—to study the polycentric governance of cryptocurrencies (CM) and the formation of consensus within their communities. We identified that CM crises encompass two types: *vulnerability crises*, where the code contradicts community expectations, and *evolution crises*, where the code functions but the community desires to modify it. We also uncovered a twofold crisis governance, with distinct procedures and expectations: *closed-door governance*, which is routine and based on local consensus, and *public governance*, which is conflictual and based on global consensus. This analysis refutes liberal-technocratic claims that CM are regulated solely by code and thus apolitical: when the letter of the code does not respect its spirit, social consensus prevails.

The analysis of the Bitcoin CVE 2018 crisis, from crisis onset to crisis resolution, allowed us to trace the modifications to the Bitcoin protocol by explicating the contexts, mechanisms, and actors involved in the discovery, evaluation, correction, publicization of patches and bugs, as well as the network update. Placed within the history of Bitcoin crises, this case also provided an opportunity to present a comprehensive overview of the crises previously experienced by the CM. The nomenclatures and categories uncovered on this occasion reveal the existence of a crisis policy, whose examination enables the description of a governance structure. Since Bitcoin is dominated by the Bitcoin Core implementation and versions, the code administration repertoire and the members of the development team—who enjoy administrative privileges, notably the lead maintainer—*de facto* occupy a central place in governance. However, these privileges are framed to guarantee traceability of modifications, the integrity of published source codes, and the formation of consensus among participants on whether to accept the new version or not, *via* mechanisms for expressing agreement or disagreement. The examination of crisis resolution reveals that the production of the patch, its publication, and the rapid network update relied on partial information and the formation of a local consensus among a handful of actors. This case allowed us to describe a crisis governance of *closed-door, routine, and locally consensual* nature.

The case of the Ethereum *Hard Fork* crisis following the “The DAO” attack highlights the transition from *closed-door governance* to a more exceptional *public governance*, conditioned by the emergence of dissensus. Our account explicates how the exploitation of a vulnerability in a decentralized fund sparked a dispute over desirable intervention strategies, reflecting the heterogeneity of community views regarding governance: whether to act *within* the letter of the code (doing nothing or counterattacking) or *on* the letter of the code (*Soft* or *Hard Fork*). Public governance produced a majority consensus without coercion among all community components *via* the distributed development of mechanisms for expressing various agreements or disagreements: the minority of *etherists* refusing the update in the name of “*Code is Law*” sovereignly seceded.

Following this summary, we propose to highlight what we consider the main contributions of this thesis in five points.

### Decoding Crypto through the Infrastructure Studies Approach

The inscription of our research within the field of *infrastructure studies* initiated by Leigh Star and Ruhleder (2010; Star 1999) is, in our view, the source of several contributions. The transposition of this analytical framework to cryptocurrencies (CM) has made it possible to illuminate the invisible and neglected aspects underpinning their uses and monetization.

The principal challenge of infrastructure studies lies in the composite nature of the objects studied (material, immaterial, and relational) and their scale, which renders them difficult to perceive (except *in cases of failure*! One of the nine infrastructural properties, Star 1999, pp. 380–382, which our Chapter III allows us to interrogate). This characteristic raises a fundamental question regarding visibility (Edwards et al. 2009). Cryptocurrencies (CM) exemplify this low visibility, further exacerbated by the technologism (a vector of invisibilization) that surrounds them. Our approach reveals the composite sociotechnical nature of CM: they are not mere technical protocols (neutral and autonomous), but *on the contrary*, complex socio-technical infrastructures shaped from all sides by social, political, and economic stakes. Chapter I illuminates the emergence of Bitcoin and Ethereum as CM by situating them within their socio-historical context. It reveals the trade-offs, problematic issues, and hybrid compromises that have marked each stage of their development, from their initial designs to their infrastructural development phases. CM are embedded in organized practices and consist of material entities (bricks, machines, cables, servers, power plants) and abstract entities (protocols, standards, practice conventions, representations of the “good” currency and governance), underscoring the crucial importance of the relational dimension in their analysis (Star and Ruhleder, 2010). Bitcoin and Ethereum become currencies through the continuous work of various actors (Bowker 1996, p. 50; Star 1999; Edwards et al. 2009), such as hackers, engineers, gamers, traders, and venture capital investors. This work highlights key issues, such as tensions between local and global needs and the challenges of integration into the existing monetary and financial system. These tensions influence practices and organizational structures, acting both as drivers and obstacles to change. They are particularly illustrated by the paradoxes of reintermediation observed during their development. While other studies have examined CM and their governance (Kavanagh and Miscione 2017; joined by Musiani 2018; Musiani, Mallard, and Méadel 2018) through isolated case studies, our contribution has been to make visible the infrastructural development of Bitcoin by weaving these cases together to create a broader framework and reveal a *seamless web* with a *harlequin* pattern (cf. Chronology 2, Chapter I, section I.1.2). Beyond the transposition of the infrastructural development model into three successive stages (*construction/launch*, *development/success, sedimentation/stabilization*), we have illuminated the issues of integration into an existing assemblage of artifacts, habits, norms, and human roles (Edwards et al. 2009, pp. 366–367). Revealing the carnivalesque dynamics of the infrastructural development of CM, this method has enabled us to clarify infrastructural properties such as embedding, transparency, learning linked to belonging, connection to practice conventions, incorporation of standards and norms, building upon an installed base, visibility during failure, and modification through modular incremental change (Star, 1999, pp. 380–382). It also sheds light on the property of *reach*—both spatial and temporal—emphasized by Star (1999), which points to the extent of infrastructures exceeding isolated events and practices. Other studies on CM, in our view, have not fully revealed this dimension.

Finally, our work directly contributes to the segment of the *infrastructure studies* corpus dedicated to “exploring power dynamics, conflicts and contestations, meanings, and embodied social relations” within infrastructures (Musiani 2018, p. 1), which we propose to do through the study of crisis governance. Our approach demonstrates that, beyond governance *by* infrastructure, governance *over* the infrastructure of cryptocurrencies (CM) is conflictual and polycentric, revealing the heterogeneity of representations within the communities of *coiners* and the ways in which the properties of CM are renegotiated. The infrastructures of Bitcoin and Ethereum appear as loci of power where political, economic, and ideological stakes are continuously at play. The crises of *vulnerability* and *evolution* analyzed have shown that polycentric governance relies on diverse community authorities and institutionalized intervention capacities, supporting (for now) the formation of consensus among individuals with divergent interests. These crises reveal a polycentric governance combining forms of local and public governance, which takes precedence over code regulation.

These elements make CM particularly interesting, as far from being yet another case of *e-infrastructure*, they incorporate a monetary perspective that has been little studied, offering insights into issues of meaning and shared values.

### From the Apolitical Acephalism of CM to the Challenge of a Sociology of Crises

This work also contributes to the sociology of crises through the cases of Bitcoin and Ethereum. First, we add the object of CM to the wealth of case studies constituting this corpus. The desire to unveil the socio-political governance of CM, based on the study of crises experienced by Bitcoin, Ethereum, and their communities, has allowed us to reaffirm (by embracing them) the theoretical and methodological stakes, as well as the tools developed by this corpus. Chapter III demonstrates the heuristic value of analyzing “the politics of crisis” and “its government” from *crisis onset* to *crisis resolution* (Aguiton, Cabane, and Cornilleau 2019, p. 4): this approach enables us to refute liberal-technocratic claims presenting CM as acephalous and apolitical currencies, regulated solely by code, by making visible the complexity and diversity of governance mechanisms within Bitcoin and Ethereum.

Starting from the gap between the routine state and the critical phenomenon delineated by crises and their nomenclatures produced by the *coiners* themselves, we have highlighted two types of crises—*vulnerability* crises and *evolution* crises—as well as the complex processes that enable *coiners* to qualify certain situations as crises and to manage them accordingly. As each of our cases illustrates, establishing a crisis diagnosis is a decisive political act, through which it is necessary to designate, beyond the “nature” of the pathology (and its severity), the prescriber, the patient, the treatments, and the care pathway. Every crisis diagnosis refers to a framing that can be contested, as demonstrated by the Ethereum Classic schism.

This work has offered a privileged perspective on the heterogeneity of representations of CM and their desired, tested, and renegotiated properties in times of crisis. We have studied how these communities organize themselves to remedy these crises and regulate the critical activities that this entails. Consequently, we have identified the actors, institutions, arenas of debate, and mechanisms involved in the production and governance of crises. Thus, we have uncovered a twofold crisis governance—*closed-door* and *public*—reflecting differentiated and evolving relations of authority, power, and counter-power. We have shown how the polycentric governance of CM involves different modes of crisis management, engaging various stakeholder groups, debate arenas, tools, and mechanisms, corresponding to diverse community expectations and capable of supporting consensus formation among individuals with divergent interests (De Filippi and Loveluck 2016, p. 15). Taken together, these enable CM to adapt to a changing environment. The resolution of the Ethereum crisis, which led to the secession of Ethereum Classic, illustrates how the practice of *forking* appears as a guarantee that no group can impose code modifications without the community’s agreement. Ultimately, this sociological approach to crises has allowed us to demonstrate that, ultimately, in the case of CM, social consensus prevails over protocol rules, as evidenced by all crises, regardless of their type (whether *vulnerability* or *evolution*) and their governance (*closed-door* or *public*).

### A coherent integration of CM into the field of monetary theory

Chapter II contributes to monetary theory, particularly to Francophone Monetary Institutionalism (FMI), by incorporating cryptocurrencies (CM) within its framework without compromising the coherence of this corpus. We proposed a critical review of the literature on CM, presenting the main critiques and situating them within their theoretical and epistemological corpus. CM, as a monetary innovation, constitute a test of explication for monetary theory. We demonstrated that these monetary objects are more readily integrated within an institutionalist monetary approach focused on usages. This non-statist nominalist apparatus allowed us to highlight the inconsistencies and blind spots of dominant analyses of CM: neither the canonical functions of money nor state exclusivity can relegate CM outside the monetary field. The practices of the Bitcoin and Ethereum communities show that CM possess the minimal characteristics of money: they are used as units of account and means of payment.

Our empirical data constitute a contribution in themselves, as we propose a mapping of the actors and stakeholders constitutive of their payment community and highlight disparate (and potentially conflicting) monetary representations. At the core of our analyses are the recognized monetary invariants of institutionalism. We illuminated the community sovereignty that makes the native units of account (NUA) of CM bearer claims on the protocol and the trust that these debt relations imply. Methodical, hierarchical, and ethical trust, far from residing solely in algorithmic authority, is distributed among protocol codes, the Github repository, exchanges, wallets, actors, media, and so forth. This distribution of trust, which does not derive from either the seal logic of public monies or the signature logic of private assets and monies, allows us to assert the conceptual singularity of CM and to establish a typology of digital assets: their monetization proceeds from a fiduciary logic akin to public monies, but we speak of a *distributed consensus logic* to emphasize a key difference. The institutions and actors guaranteeing this purchasing power differ from national currencies, with trust ultimately resting on a network of voluntary actors and institutions forming a dual and polycentric governance.

Cryptocurrencies (CM), as radical attempts to institute a currency protected from politics and citizen deliberation, offer a paradoxical contribution: if they succeed as money, it is “*in direct contradiction with the political ideology and monetary theory that underpin them*” (Dodd 2017, p.1). Far from depoliticizing money, they repoliticize it in a singular manner: even as cryptocurrencies, money remains an institution co-produced by the members of its payment community. CM invite negotiation over their properties, as revealed by *evolution crises* governed *publicly*.

### A translation effort *attentive to* and *attended by* the actors

The positioning of this thesis within the fields of science and technology studies and the sociology of technological controversies raised formal questions in terms of “translation” for our “actor-networks.”

Society results from ongoing actions, where socio-technical objects and interactions between humans and non-humans form complex networks influencing actions and decisions. As a researcher, we started from these networks, which led to a reflexive effort prompting us to question the interactions and mutual translations between actors (human and non-human) that we helped to form around the world of CM. Chapter II demonstrated that a certain mutual disdain and misunderstanding partly underpinned the controversy between *coiners* and money professionals (economists, bankers, central bankers).

Our work, through the (evaluated and discussed) choice of an “encyclopedic” form, enables a contribution in terms of translation between different social spaces: we wished for it to facilitate dialogue between the academic world (especially the humanities) and that of CM. The fine granularity of certain expositions, the presence of abundant footnotes, and the prominence given to actors’ voices are conceived as bridges offering spaces of translation (accessing a discussion on BitcoinTalk, discovering a block explorer and the *on chain* activity it allows to observe, etc.). Despite its heaviness, this formal device has made it possible to produce a thesis rich in materials, offering researchers pathways to the arcana of the socio-technical networks of CM. Our work is not conceived as an external critique directed solely at the academic world, but also addressed to the *coiners*. The constitution of an *etic* language founded on the *emic* language of the *coiners* (translated through the construction of a glossary and rich appendices) should allow us not only to speak about them but also to engage in dialogue with them.

Like the scallop larvae and the fishing sailors of the Bay of Saint-Brieuc studied by Callon (quoted at the outset of this general conclusion), we have sought to be among those researchers who facilitate mediation between the *coiners* and money professionals.

### Cryptocurrencies (CM): Convenient Scapegoats for a Monetary System in Crisis?

We will conclude this work with an open question that has accompanied us from the beginning of our reflections on CM to the end of this thesis. This question partly explains why the reader will not find here a definitive answer regarding the “good” or “bad” quality of CM compared to national currencies, nor critiques of their alleged dangers emphasized by finance professionals.

CM are accused of being an ecological disaster (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015; Bank of International Settlements 2018; Servet and Dufrêne 2021, among others) due to their operation based on Proof of Work (PoW). They are also seen as a threat to states’ capacity to collect taxes and monitor financial transactions (Krugman 2013), as well as destabilizing forces for the economy. At Davos, President E. Macron placed Bitcoin and crypto-assets alongside *shadow banking*, labeling them as the most deregulated and deregulating forces within the global banking and financial system[^1]. “*But is this not equally true of traditional money, continuously created by central banks? By shining the spotlight on the hell of CM, are we not revealing the weaknesses of bank money?*” (Couppey-Soubeyran 2021).

From our theoretical and civic standpoint, these issues are significant but transversal to our objects of study. Cryptocurrencies (CM) are not the cause of new ills but the symptom of a financialized world and the privileges institutionalized therein. President Macron must have known that shadow banking, tax havens, and fraud existed long before CM and are integrated into the traditional banking and financial system, upon which regulators can act. Through this rhetorical device, an emerging sector whose size and assets under management remain relatively limited and contingent today was singled out, whereas, in many respects, it is conflated with others whose size, volumes, and dispersion are incomparably larger and more dangerous[^2]. It is paradoxical to want to strongly regulate an emerging sector—something that actors themselves request in the face of the legal uncertainty they encounter—while traditional finance, responsible for the 2008 crisis, continues to benefit from ever greater leeway and liquidity injections. In France, the public debate, emphasizing risks at the expense of opportunities[^3], stands in stark contrast to the more nuanced discussions taking place in other countries or within certain financial institutions (BoE[^4], FMI[^5], BIS[^6]).

Cryptocurrencies (CM) serve as a powerful contrasting agent that makes visible the financialization of our societies and its effects, such as the market regulation of energy and tax evasion. Although they participate in these phenomena, they do so with distinctive properties. Indeed, they enable tax avoidance, but these transactions are recorded in a public ledger accessible to all, unlike the closed databases of financial institutions. Perhaps, then, by being accessible to everyone, it is the scale of the fraud that becomes intolerable, highlighting that when it involves a minority, it is tolerated (a reasoning put forward by an auditor working on defense issues during a seminar). Our infrastructure studies demonstrate that CM are integrated into the monetary and financial system and can, as such, be regulated at the level of actors and services: *on-chain* payments, followed by *off-chain* exchanges, especially for large amounts, are more easily traceable than those of traditional bank money (E. Klein, cyber-specialized law enforcement officer, Participant Observation No. 12). 

The energy consumption of CM is legitimately questioned, but it must be compared to that of the traditional financial system. Recent studies show that “*in this face-off* [...] *traditional finance*” must also be held accountable: one “*study* [by Valuechain] *arrives at a total of 4981 TWh/year*” (Magali 2022), and another “*estimates it at 2,340 to 3,861 TWh per year, i.e., between 23 and 38 times more than bitcoin*” (Dumas 2023). The energy consumption of PoW systems depends on the energy source used for mining, and it is the responsibility of states to regulate and promote more virtuous energy sources. PoW CM offer the opportunity to monetize potential energy surpluses from newly created production units, making the direct resale of these surpluses profitable in the face of storage and transmission limitations (A 2020; S. Gouspillou, Interview No. 17).

Finally, following Couppey-Soubeyran (2021), one can legitimately ask: is the national currency truly *“the public good it claims to be?”* In reality, *“the dangerous privatization of money”* is not due to cryptocurrencies (CM), but rather *“to its capture by the banking and financial sector,”* from which innovations *“such as cryptocurrencies or complementary currencies”* seek to emancipate themselves (*Ibid.*). Are central banks not more technocratic than CM, through a statutory independence that removes from democratic deliberation the “constrained discretion” they exercise? By criticizing the traditional monetary and financial system, CM reflect an era of distrust toward politics, public administrations, and governments. Paradoxically, although they constitute radical attempts to institute a currency protected from political influence, they singularly repoliticize it. Not founded on the contractual logic of signature, CM appear less as private currencies than as public ones. They do not escape community deliberation; the study of their crisis governance demonstrates that they are co-produced by the members of their communities, and their polycentric governance invites each component to renegotiate their ownership. 

Nothing allows us to assert that these infrastructures and their communities will be able to weather all the crises that the future holds, or that the severity of any one of them will not ultimately undermine the trust they might inspire. However, our investigative work contributes to showing that these monetary and financial infrastructures have allowed us to use and study them without any authorization being necessary. Moreover, in many respects, a national currency also requires this entire assemblage of socio-technical devices, software, validation procedures, etc. Inevitably, there are bugs, problems, and malfeasance, as *“after discovering that a cyberattack had diverted $81 million from the Central Bank of Bangladesh, the interbank network Swift warned its users that this was not an isolated case and issued a security update for its client software, which must be installed imperatively”* (Gross 2016). Yet, in this case, the transactional database is private, not public, and it is possible to falsify *“valid accreditations to create and disseminate messages on the network”* (*Ibid.*), which cryptographic proofs prevent. In the case of Swift, the software codes are proprietary and not open source; thus, the patch is mandatory, not auditable, nor *forkable* by clients who are already fortunate to have been informed… whereas such a crisis would have been visible to all within the framework of a CM. 

Every currency is the product of an infrastructure, both national currencies and CM. Therefore, rather than reproaching them for *“what can be equally reproached to legal tender, let us question the reasons for their development”* (Couppey-Soubeyran 2021) and the infrastructural properties—such as openness and transparency—that the traditional system could profitably emulate.
