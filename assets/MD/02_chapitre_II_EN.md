II.  : Moving Beyond the Controversy over the Monetary Status of Cryptocurrencies through an Institutionalist Approach Focused on Usage

> “Bitcoin can be better understood as [...] a currency protected against inflation [...] without relying on trusted third parties. [...] Bitcoin automates the functions of a modern central bank and makes them predictable and practically immutable by programming them into decentralized code among thousands of network members, none of whom can alter the code without the consent of the others. [...] While Bitcoin is a new invention of the digital era, the problems it claims to solve—namely, providing a form of money that is under the control of its owner and likely to retain its value over the long term—are as old as human society itself. [...] Bitcoin’s resilience has not been limited to successfully repelling attacks; it has also brilliantly resisted any attempt to modify or alter its characteristics. [...] Moreover, the mode of operation of its consensus rules makes it highly resistant to alteration by individuals[,] no one controls Bitcoin and [...] the only option available is to use it as is or not to use it at all.”

But there is no conceptual obstacle to the idea that two individuals engaged in a transaction could settle by transferring wealth from one electronic account to another in real time. Pre-agreed algorithms would determine which financial assets have been sold* [...]*. And the provider of that good or service would know that incoming funds will be allocated to the appropriate combination of assets, according to another pre-established algorithm. I think that if central banks want to maintain their central position* [...] *they must meet the intellectual and technological challenges that lie ahead.”*

*“The revolution in information technologies* \[...\] *has given rise to passionate speculations* \[...\] The promise of the ‘new economy’ has excited the imagination of the young* \[and\] *among the institutions of the ‘old economy’ wondering whether they will soon be rendered obsolete, one can cite central banks, which are beginning to question whether their ability to stabilize the value of their national currency might be eroded by the development of electronic means of payment.* \[...\] *The only real question concerning this future is that of the importance of central banks’ monetary policies \[and those\] that demonstrate both the commitment and the skills necessary to maintain a stable value for their countries’ currencies should continue to play an important role in the coming century.”*

**“Monetary Policy in a World Without Money”; Michael Woodford 2000, pp. 1 and 42**

It is conceivable to imagine an “ideal” currency based on an interindividual and decentralized payment system, without banks, central banks (CB), or any intermediaries. However, to completely detach money from any instituted collective, one must assume the emergence of technological innovations that substitute for the central and collective authorities traditionally charged with administering money. Such a currency is only conceivable in optimal markets, devoid of information asymmetry and uncertainty. These ideas, although radical, are not exclusive to Nakamoto and libertarian coiners. They are shared by monetary professionals: Nobel laureates in economics and practitioners have deemed the idea desirable[^1]. King (1999, cited in the epigraph), Governor of the *Bank of England* at the advent of electronic currencies, saw no problem with the existence of a purely private settlement system. And here come the coiners, led by bitcoiners, who claim to go further. These monetary representations, describing “*a world without money*” (Woodford 2000, in the epigraph) where everything is merely securities, remain controversial. Simmel (2009, pp. 166–167) emphasizes that this ideal currency is unattainable due to uncertainty and “*the imperfections of economic technique*” which money socially supplements: monetary fiduciary trust and liquidity are inseparable from an instituted collective guaranteeing them[^2]. Since economic theory has not confirmed these assumptions, practice might provide answers. Faced with what sometimes appears as a “*dream*,” sometimes as a “*nightmare*” libertarian monetary vision (De Filippi 2013; Karlstrøm 2014), the responses may be surprising.

[^1]: See, for example, the reflections of economists such as Milton Friedman and others who have engaged with the concept of private or decentralized money systems.

[^2]: Simmel, Georg. 2009. *Philosophy of Money*. Translated edition, pp. 166–167.

This chapter contributes to the controversy surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM). We will examine two of the propositions within the “liberal-technocratic” syllogism at the core of coiners’ monetary ambitions. This syllogism posits that since (i) technology is autonomous and neutral with respect to the social world, and (ii) cryptocurrencies are purely technical monies; then (iii) they are immune to human governance and its socio-political interests, which makes them (iv) “better” monies than national currencies. The refutation of the first two premises carried out in the preceding chapter weakens the subsequent ones, but these latter, situated within the domain of economic science, require specific deconstructions. This chapter will interrogate the remaining two propositions: that cryptocurrencies are monies, and that they would be “better” than national currencies due to an allegedly apolitical nature guaranteed by the absence of human governance.  

This endeavor entails questioning the concept of money and, by extension, the issues related to its governance, which are crucial in the analysis of money. The first chapter serves as the foundation for the second. The explication of the carnivalesque development of cryptocurrencies lays the groundwork for their *monetization* (see Section II.2.2 below) and reveals signs of an extant governance (actors and domains of development, maintenance, and crisis management, etc.). In seeking to better characterize cryptocurrencies and to assess their claim to be “good” or “bad” monies, we will highlight that their singularity lies precisely in the apparent form of this governance, which is neither centralized nor “*acephalous*” (Favier and Takkal Bataille 2017), but rather “*polycephalous*.”  

We will affirm the monetary nature of Bitcoin and Ethereum, without, however, endorsing the monetary views of academic and practitioner critics or those of coiners. After establishing that the question of governance lies at the heart of the categorical clarification regarding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies, we will further examine this governance in Chapter III.

Many “technocratic liberal” monetary ambitions of coiners refer back to foundational controversies within the monetary field concerning the definition of money, the expected properties for it to be considered “good,” and the governance that guarantees it as such. These ambitions radically revive an old and recurrent debate in monetary history, encapsulated by the phrase “rules versus discretion,” at the core of which lies the question of neutral money. The orthodoxy, aligned with the rules camp, gathers proponents of the neutrality (or neutralization) of money, conceived as a commodity whose equilibrium depends on a competitive market. For this school of thought, only the stability of the unit of account matters, and everything must therefore be sacrificed to a sound money with a limited supply to guarantee its value over time. This stability, viewed as neutrality, would be “good” for the economy because it would allow agents to fix their expectations. Although historically the central administration of money is a necessary evil, this current advocates rigid rules to strictly constrain monetary policies and authorities.  

In the discretion camp, money is primarily a political framework. Its quality is measured by the capacity of the monetary system to ensure its reproduction according to the founding principles recognized by its members. Money and its qualities depend on trade-offs made by the payment community: the stability of the unit of account is a choice, and ensuring the viability of the payment system is another, no less important depending on the context. Ultimately, the payment community decides the ends and means to be implemented. Although monetary authorities must nonetheless be accountable to the community, they must have room for maneuver and discretionary powers (cf. lender of last resort). A “good” money maintains a political consensus around it. The assessment of the qualities and legitimacy of money depends on the analysis of uses, utility, and expectations of the actors.

In these controversies, the technicist liberal ambitions of coiners align with a *radicalized* rule in which any centralization is considered a compromise. Despite their differences, many coiners believe that every currency faces a “*fundamental problem*”: trust! Currencies require “*trusting the central bank not to debase the currency*” and “*trusting banks to hold our money and transfer it electronically*,” but since “*they lend it out in waves of credit bubbles with barely a fraction in reserve*,” “*the history of fiat currencies is full of breaches of this trust*” (Nakamoto 2009). In light of the issues surrounding the stability of currency value, coiners regard Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies (CM) as unprecedented solutions. Not backed by any commodity nor by liabilities issued by a third party (whether physical or legal), their unit of account (UCN) abstracts away from the seals and signatures of credible third parties, which underpin existing currencies. Through their architecture, cryptocurrencies would offer monetary and payment systems opposed to hierarchical systems. They present themselves as alternatives to a contemporary monetary order deemed incapable of providing an independent and high-quality currency and monetary policy, due to a conception based on collusion between political and banking powers. For coiners, cryptocurrencies would offer sound currencies of unparalleled “hardness” and credibility[^3], since the immutability of the coinage would guarantee temporal coherence beyond challenge. The design of cryptocurrencies would accomplish an unprecedented feat: protecting themselves once and for all from political interference, monetary manipulations, and the inflation they would invariably produce[^4]. How? Through their technical and distributed protocol automating the functions of modern central banks, making them predictable because programmed within immutable codes, running on machines whose decentralization would prevent any discretionary alteration. Some proponents see in them “better” currencies than national “fiat” currencies[^5], which should serve as the foundation for a more secure, efficient, and resilient international monetary system, following a process of “*hyper-bitcoinization*” leading to the massive adoption of Bitcoin or another cryptocurrency, which they ardently desire (“*hyper-crypto-monetization*” thereafter, Ammous 2018, p. 9).

Coiners and financial professionals, theorists and practitioners of money alike, claim allegiance to the camp of the rule. This theoretical proximity partly contributes to the intensity of the conflict opposing them regarding Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies (CM). More “*royalists than the king*,” coiners assert that they have depoliticized money through sound minting, from which financial professionals—still too dependent on political interests—are excluded. Coiners claim to replace monetary governance with an intangible and “immutable” protocol, “trustless,” without intermediaries, relying on a “mathematical” rather than political consensus. Financial professionals respond that, precisely due to this absence of governance, cryptocurrencies are not very good monies and that it is neither possible nor desirable for them to become massively adopted.

We position ourselves in the camp of discretion, and the qualification of cryptocurrencies as “good” or “bad” monies is deferred to an empirical analysis of their uses and monetary governance. Before disputing these qualities, it is essential to have determined their monetary status, which the denomination of cryptocurrencies claims in comparison to traditional monies. It is deliberately that we adopt this indigenous appellation. Our thesis defends the idea that cryptocurrencies do constitute money and that they represent a novel form by virtue of their governance: the denomination of cryptocurrencies emphasizes the centrality of cryptography, the sociotechnical nexus of their protocol and minting, as well as usage arrangements (the linkage between the individual and the collective being at stake here, cf. Chap. I). 

This monetary status is marginally recognized by certain studies (Yermack 2013; Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz 2013; Ali et al. 2013; Böhme et al. 2015; Raskin and Yermack 2016; Dodd 2017; Kindelberger 2017; André Orléan 2019), but remains predominantly contested by economists and practitioners. Affirming or denying that cryptocurrencies can be money is not an easy task, as it requires defining what money is—a question without unanimous answer, given the plural and situated approaches to money. Theoretical schools have often responded to questions related to the monies of their time [(Tutin 2009; de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=Y63CC1), such that more recent cryptocurrencies seem like unidentified monetary objects, orthogonal to the traditional conception of money (i.e., unitary, territorialized, bank-based, centralized, and ultimately guaranteed by a sovereign state).

The above elements explain that “*most analyses of Bitcoin fail to take a clear position and thus create false debates, precisely because they suffer from a theoretical void*” (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre, 2015, p. 5). Historically, monetary innovations have led to institutional and political adaptations, shaping the representations and institutional conditions of the monetary space under consideration. Complementary currencies (CCs) are formidable heuristic tools illuminating the qualitative variations of institutional monetary arrangements and what money conceptually and practically encompasses[^6]. Like credit money in the 19th century, the computerization of markets in the 1990s, or electronic currencies in the 2000s, Bitcoin and CCs do not correspond to the usual legal and academic representations. As with previous innovations, CCs provoke a renewed debate on money, its status, and its role (Tutin 2009; de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003; Kindelberger 2004). CCs, as “*institutional monsters*,” constitute a “*test of explication*” for academics and practitioners, putting “*the definition* [of money] *into crisis, or rather* [forcing one] *to clearly explicate what* [it] *is and what* [it] *is not*,” akin to the “*Electronic Communication Networks*” analyzed by Muniesa, which challenged the definition of the *market* (2017, p. 3). CCs can be related to parallel currencies, which “*economic theory rarely takes into account,* and when it does, it does so with difficulty (currency substitutions) or in connection with very specific phenomena (hyperinflation) that only represent a portion of the phenomenon*” (Blanc 1998a, p. 6). This relegation of alternative currencies is explained by the fact that “*the economic analysis of monetary phenomena* [...] *relies* [...] *on two distinct* [but] *complementary and inadequate models* [regarding] *the issue of parallel currencies*: a ‘*legal monetary approach*’ *considering that money falls solely under* [...] *the State* [and] a ‘*market monetary approach*’ *considering money as a commodity* [reducing the] *transaction costs* [of] *barter*” (*Ibid.*). The case of CCs poses the same difficulties and calls into question the capacity of existing theories to conceive money in its otherness. For us, CCs will be an opportunity to challenge dominant representations by advancing a francophone monetary institutionalism (FMI) interested in actors’ practices.

To demonstrate the monetary nature of crypto-monies (CM), this chapter is divided into three sections. The first section presents a literature review of the critiques of CM, while situating them within their theoretical and epistemological frameworks. Beneath the diversity of critiques from orthodox economists, heterodox scholars, and practitioners, two main clusters emerge that motivate the monetary relegation of CM: the instrumental approach and the nominalist/chartalist approach (II.1). The second section provides an opportunity to clarify our approach, which is situated within monetary institutionalism and draws on economic ethnography. We thus adopt a non-statist nominalism, allowing us to assert that neither the canonical functions of money (central for proponents of the instrumental approach), nor state exclusivity (essential for critics of chartalist approaches) can be used to exclude CM from the domain of money. Indeed, in light of practices, CM clearly exhibit the minimal recognized characteristics of any money (captured by the conceptual triptych: debts, trust, sovereignty) and integrate readily into the phenomenon of parallel currencies (II.2).

The third section, continuing the attempt to characterize CM within the monetary field, formulates a hypothesis contrary to that which underpins the dispute between coiners and their critics regarding the “good” or “bad” quality of these currencies: if the monetary singularity of CM is to be sought in their governance, it is not because the latter is absent, neutral, or reduced to their protocol codes. As outlined in the first chapter, CM and their payment communities are “*multifaceted*[^s], *politically contested*[^s], and *sociologically rich*[^s] in function and meaning” (Dodd, 2017, pp. 4 and 8). By focusing exclusively on the opinions of the most libertarian coiners and comparing CM to national currencies, monetary professionals deprive themselves of seeing the heterogeneity of coiner monetary representations, the community conflicts that they necessarily imply, and thus the governance in place to regulate them. Indeed, coiner communities experience controversies regarding whether or not to evolve their CM to make it “better,” revealing a dual and polycentric governance that exceeds the formal interaction framework established by their protocol codes. This dual and polycentric governance explains why CM are founded neither on the seal logic (of state monies) nor on the signature logic (of private monies), thereby offering a path toward categorical clarification within the monetary field (II.3). The duality and polycentrism of governance will thus reveal their full importance in characterizing CM as a novel monetary form, justifying a specific analysis in a dedicated Chapter III.

# “Cryptocurrencies Are Not Money!”: Arguments Against the Monetary Nature of Cryptocurrencies

Through a critical review of the economic and monetary literature, this section aims to outline the main contours of the controversy surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM). We have compiled the critiques that lead the majority of experts not to consider cryptocurrencies and Bitcoin as money, and then sought to elucidate the theoretical and epistemological foundations of these judgments, in order to clarify their limits and blind spots. This theoretical work appeared necessary for several reasons. First, these critiques are all embedded within different, sometimes antinomic, epistemological foundations, categories, definitions, and representations. Second, they often exhibit a problematic syncretism in terms of internal and external coherence: "*it must be acknowledged that a lack of clarity, if not outright confusion, most often prevails in studies on the subject.* [...] *the harshest assessment must be made within the economic discipline* [...] *where, most of the time, comments on Bitcoin are not accompanied by any theoretical illumination. Is this so surprising, given how divisive money is among economists?*" (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, p. 2). As a test of explicitation, cryptocurrencies challenge the capacity of dominant theories to incorporate them. It is unsurprising when this dispute is situated within the history of controversies that have always followed monetary innovations. While each economic school has sought to address questions related to the monies of its time, cryptocurrencies thus constitute formidable objects for contributing to monetary theory.

As a major innovation, it is understandable that cryptocurrencies (CM) provoke questioning and debate. Like previous innovations, they struggle to fit within existing monetary categories, and their specific characteristics make them difficult to apprehend—both by the general public and by economists and practitioners, including banking and financial actors, regulatory authorities, central banks, and public administrations—especially given the absence of a single, consensually accepted definition of money. Money is defined differently within the legal domain—through a complex web of various articles and codes (see section II.1.2)—and within the economic domain, where competing definitions coexist (see sections II.1.1 and II.2). Certain properties and theoretical invariants enjoy more or less consensus. Thus, following Aristotle, the theoretical identification of the “functions” of money has served as a foundational reference for all matters concerning money: the unit of account function, the medium of exchange function, and the store of value function.  

As a unit of account, money serves as a general standard of value underpinning all economic calculation: every exchange value depends on it, and this function conditions the determination of nominal magnitudes that make heterogeneous goods commensurable, comparable, and exchangeable in terms of quantity and quality. As a medium of exchange, money acts as an accepted and universally recognized intermediary: it is a general equivalent exchangeable for any commodity. Finally, as a store of value, money can constitute an asset within one’s patrimony and serve to defer purchases over time. Although these functions are common to monetary approaches, they do not carry the same meaning or importance across different theories: the priority and status assigned to each function determine the differences among competing theoretical frameworks.

In fact, commodity monies (CM) do not coincide with the dominant economic definitions and theories (J.P. Koning 2012; Selgin 2013; Selgin 2014b; Weber 2014a; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015). The monetary field is structured around two major opposing approaches. And if, as Schumpeter reminded us, there are “*only two theories of money worthy of the name… the commodity theory and the credit theory*” (Goodhart 2005, p. 817), neither makes room for CM: these are relegated either by instrumental and substantial approaches, which, at the heart of neoclassical theory, conceive money as a commodity (see Box 1), or by competing nominalist approaches (or “*legal constraint*” approaches, central to the Chartalist perspective, see Box 2; Orléan 1998, p. 11), which conceive money as a social debt to holders, a product of state-imposed restrictions. First, we will revisit the theoretical critiques within the economic field.

## Criticism of Commodity Money from Major Monetary Theory Traditions

Experts in economic science and its practitioners may be surprised by the formation of a consensual agreement among opposing schools of thought, particularly in the monetary domain, which CM nonetheless manages to produce. A majority of authors, from all affiliations, deny CM the status of money. Since analyses of money—its nature, the conditions of its emergence, and its effects—differ according to the conceptual framework employed, it is important to situate the critiques of CM within their theoretical contexts. We will begin with critiques originating from the so-called instrumental monetary approach, orthodox in economic science, where money is conceived as a commodity (“*commodity money*”). Then, we will present Chartalist critiques, grounded in the competing corpus that conceives money as a claim on the state (“*claim money*”).

### Instrumental Critiques Based on Canonical Monetary Functions

Instrumental or substantial monetary approaches rely on a complex theoretical framework grounded in a utility/rarity value theory (see Box 1 below). Nearly all economists who criticize cryptocurrencies do so from within this framework. From this perspective, the monetary object (and cryptocurrencies do not constitute an exception) must possess an “intrinsic” value, based on desirable characteristics. The canonical monetary functions derive from these sought-after properties; they justify the expression of a rational demand for money, according to the utility provided by its “consumption.” Just as with non-fully convertible paper monies (fiat currencies), integrating cryptocurrencies into this framework is not straightforward: their “*digital metallism*” (Maurer et al., 2014; Mallard et al., 2018) does not suffice to fully identify them with metallic monies (gold and silver), the typical ideal representatives of commodity money for this tradition. Metallic monies are naturally attributed an intrinsic value, stemming from their backing by socioeconomically precious physical materials: the value of money is supported by their relative scarcity and singular, sought-after physical properties (malleability, resistance to oxidation, etc.), which confer upon them a market value not exclusively monetary, opening up a direct and inherent consumption utility (Simmel’s “*substance value*” 2009). Cryptocurrencies certainly exhibit, like precious metals, a limited supply and the recognized properties of good monetary commodities—divisibility, transportability, and preservation over time (Menger 1892, p. 247)—but the immateriality of their unique cryptographic numbers (UCNs) prevents these economists from attributing to them an intrinsic value linked to the utility of direct consumption.

Regarding the unit of account function, there is a consensus that cryptocurrencies (CCs) cannot claim to fulfill this function (Ali et al. 2013, p. 5; Yermack 2013, pp. 3 and 7; Weber 2014a, p. 16). Their units of account (UoAs) are indeed not a general standard, as they do not (or only minimally) serve to denominate the value of goods and services. Although prices are currently quoted in cryptocurrency units* such as BTC or ETH, this should not be understood as a use *per se*: according to these authors, prices displayed in cryptocurrency units conceal a nominal pricing in national currency (here, the primary fiat currency), whose real-time adjustment to market exchange rates is carried out via gateway services that provide merchants with an instant conversion service of the cryptocurrency units received as payment (cf. Chap. I.2.1; Ali et al. 2013; Brito and Castillo 2013). This lack of use as a unit of account is particularly evident in the fact that the costs borne by node operators are denominated in fiat currency, whereas their revenues are denominated in cryptocurrency units. Here, cryptocurrency units function solely as a payment vehicle, not as money.

Opinions regarding the capacity of cryptocurrencies (CM) to fulfill the function of exchange instruments are more nuanced. Some authors acknowledge this capacity (Yermack 2013, p. 7; Brito and Castillo 2013, p. 34), but the majority qualify its scope (Weber 2014b, p. 2; Weber 2014a, p. 16): their transactional spheres would be too limited. The relatively small number of merchants accepting cryptocurrencies corresponds to an equally limited number of transactions (Ali et al. 2013, p. 4; Roubini 2018; Bank of International Settlements 2018). Moreover, this function would be confined to illegal activities (Weber 2014b, p. 2; Krugman 2013; Krugman 2018a; Krugman 2018b; Stiglitz 2017; Tirole 2017). Cryptocurrencies also prove to be *“heavy, slow, and costly means of payment”* (Krugman 2018b) and would be inefficient compared to existing payment methods (payment cards, PayPal, etc.). Ultimately, their acceptance as payment would be reducible to a corporate communication strategy whereby firms use them more as *“signals—look at me, I am at the cutting edge of progress!—than for their actual utility”* (Krugman 2018a). Due to this lack of use, they would be quite incapable of fulfilling the function of a store of value. All commentators have emphasized this functional incapacity, notably because of the high volatility of their price. This volatility would render them, by definition, unfit to provide a store of value function (Ali et al. 2013, p. 4; Yermack 2013, pp. 2 and 7; Weber 2014a, p. 16; Weber 2014b, p. 3; Kubát 2015), since anticipating price declines discourages holding them. Conversely, anticipating sharp price increases discourages spending and circulation. It is thus understandable that, through the lens of an instrumental approach where, *“to succeed, money must be both a medium of exchange and a reasonably stable store of value”* (Krugman 2013), cryptocurrencies fall outside the framework.

Ultimately, within the dominant neoclassical corpus, cryptocurrencies are recognized neither intrinsic value nor monetary functions essential to their monetary qualification. From this perspective, N. Roubini offers a succinct conclusion: *“Bitcoin is NOT a currency: it is not a unit of account, it is not a unique means of cash, it is not an evolving means of payment, it is not backed by any asset, it has no legal tender status, its price is heavily manipulated, and its partial store of value function therefore rests on nothing.”*[^7] His addition of a reference to the law, inscribed within the competing nominalist corpus, illustrates the syncretism he displays (like many others) and anticipates that the same disqualification awaits cryptocurrencies there, albeit based on different arguments.

### A “creature of the state” currency: nominalist and chartalist critiques

In the field of the humanities, the history of the origins of money and the key concepts of orthodox theory are contested. Neither historians, anthropologists, nor certain economists recognize any historical or empirical veracity in these “myths” of a purely commodity money, which, however, is not originally so[^8] (Aglietta and Orléan 1998; Alary 2009; Wray 2010, p. 39; Cartelier 2013). The “nominalist” corpus[^9] challenges what its authors consider an *aprioristic*, *ahistorical*, and *asocial* economistic *sophistry*, which retrospectively (and anachronistically) projects erroneous categories onto economic and monetary phenomena (Chavance 2011, p. xi). The multiple heterodox monetary approaches constituting this corpus (chartalism, neo-chartalism, post-Keynesianism, institutionalism, conventionalism, etc.) share a vision of non-commodity money (Ingham 2004, p. 24; Aglietta and Orléan 1998), and each school establishes (more or less strong) links between money, the state, and the normative framework that the latter establishes and enforces (see Box 2 below).

Many critiques of cryptocurrencies (CCs) rely on arguments drawn from this corpus, particularly those stemming from state-centric theories of money. If, like fiat currencies, CCs cannot claim any “intrinsic value,” their independence from national political sovereignty and monetary authorities would render them unfit to be recognized as money. Money is conceived as a “*money–institution*,” and fiat currencies, although devoid of “intrinsic value,” are in this corpus claims on an issuer enjoying exorbitant privileges: the State, which, through the formal framework (normative and fiscal) imposed by force and violence, ensures the circulation of the issued currency units and thereby the macroeconomic closure (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, pp. 12–13). Considering that money, as a particular form of debt, implies state political sovereignty (reduced to formal legalism), one is led to exclude CCs from the status of money. Indeed, CCs would be “*not a claim on anyone*” (Ali et al. 2013, p. 3). Since they are backed neither by a commodity nor by liabilities issued by a third party (natural or legal person), their voluntary acceptance, absent any coercion, would offer no discount guarantee to their holders. These critiques therefore consider that under such conditions CCs cannot fulfill either the role of unit of account or that of means of payment and exchange enabling the valuation and settlement of obligations, notably those of miners perceived as issuers. The use as a unit of account, essential for this school of thought, marks that money refers to a higher collective order: monetary sovereignty refers to the issuer’s capacity to impose its money as unit of account and in payment. 

What can be said in the face of CCs, for which the expression of production costs and revenues of mining activities is differentiated (the former expressed in national currency, the latter in CCs)? “*Most, if not all, costs, even for producers in the bitcoin economy, are denominated in different currencies* (...) [Insofar as] *bitcoin revenues have a volatile value in terms of the currencies needed to pay inputs, it would not be economical to adopt bitcoins as a unit of account, that is, to display prices in bitcoins*” (Weber 2014a, p. 16). This argument, already mobilized by proponents of an instrumental approach, is here interpreted as a sign of the absence of proper monetary sovereignty, depriving CCs of bearer claim status, and thus of money. Since “*a thing can only be issued as money if it is capable of extinguishing any debt contracted by the issuer*” (Ingham 2004, p. 25), and considering (wrongly, in our view) that the issuance of CCs is the act of miners and not of the protocol, for these authors CCs are not money.

The authors of this school of thought emphasize that the legal constraints guaranteeing the demand for and circulation of money are no longer limited solely to fiscal regulations. They are supplemented by regulatory actions undertaken by monetary authorities and, more broadly, by public authorities. Historically, monetary authorities have occupied a central position within the hierarchical system and played an essential role both in maintaining the stability of the unit of account and in preserving the payment system[^10] (Velde 2013, p. 3; Weber 2014b, p. 3; Krugman 2018a; J.P. Koning 2018f). By providing credit and liquidity to society as a lender of last resort (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 19), the minting of money has become a crucial instrument in the economic sphere, serving as a tool for countercyclical policies (Tirole 2017; Varoufakis 2013; Varoufakis 2020). Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies (CM) would not offer such levers, given that their minting is predetermined and independent of actual economic conditions. In summary, the critique from this perspective explains that

> "*Bitcoin relies neither on a hierarchical banking system overseen by a central bank, nor on a clearing system ensuring the sustainability of payments. Completely decentralized, it is unable to provide the liquidity necessary for the economic circuit of goods and services production through advances for financing the real economy. In the event of an economic downturn, it cannot enable public stimulus actions. The hypervolatility of its price renders it a monetary instrument poorly suited to anchoring expectations and sustaining payments.*" (Ponsot 2021, p. 3).

Ultimately, cryptocurrencies are in no way comparable to national currencies, as they do not offer

> "*the necessary buffers to prevent capitalist crises*" (Varoufakis 2020).

From the standpoint of this comparison (which we argue is not valid, cf. section II.3.2), the relative autonomy of cryptocurrencies with respect to the state—particularly the inelasticity of the issuance of their unit of account (UCN)—ultimately relegates them outside the domain of money.

Thus, regardless of the theoretical framework underpinning these critiques, all concur that cryptocurrencies cannot be considered money. We will now present the criticisms advanced by monetary authorities who, combining various arguments already identified, arrive at the same categorical rejection.

## Cryptocurrencies as a Test of the Explication of Money: “Monetary Monsters” Difficult to Classify

Money is not merely the subject of abstract theoretical debates; it is also a highly concrete practical construction: the monetary and financial order is primarily defined formally by law and by the practices of monetary authorities. With crypto-assets (CAs), legislators and regulators must first, as with any monetary and financial innovation, question their legal status. However, their singularity renders legal qualification challenging insofar as they correspond neither to existing legal definitions nor to those empirically developed by monetary authorities and their administrations. CAs subject money to a “*test of explicitation*” akin to that provoked by computerization and the emergence of “*Electronic Communication Networks*” in the 1990s. These networks, labeled “*institutional monsters*,” disrupted “*the definition of the market*,” compelling practitioners to clarify “*what a market is* (...) *and what it is not*” (Muniesa 2007, p. 3). Similarly, CAs resemble “monetary monsters,” forcing practitioners to reconsider their categories and contents and to specify what is or is not money. The following quotation from E. Assouan (2018) of the Banque de France provides a succinct summary of the relegation of CAs by monetary professionals: “*crypto-assets, sometimes mistakenly called cryptocurrencies or virtual currencies, refer to Bitcoin and other virtual tokens used for speculation and for certain purchases [...]. The Banque de France prefers to speak of crypto-assets rather than money. A true currency, like the euro, is a universal unit of account and is accepted by all merchants because its value is guaranteed. Conversely, no one guarantees the value of Bitcoin, which constantly fluctuates, and no one is obliged to accept it as payment. Crypto-assets are no more illegal than casino chips, for example, but because they offer total anonymity to their holders, they can be preferentially used to finance illegal activities***”** (Assouan 2018).

### Objects Not Covered by the Legal and Empirical Categories of Money

National monetary spaces today constitute sovereign jurisdictional domains that have been structured over the long term, both hierarchically and territorially, concomitantly with the development of nation-states (Cohen 1998; Gilbert and Helleiner 1999a; Helleiner 2003). The regulatory categories of money and its forms (fiduciary, scriptural, electronic, public/private, etc.) are explicit and formal, and fall under this sovereignty. The articulation of foreign currencies at the international level and the question of relations between monetary authorities depend on a highly regulated activity that has become internationalized (for the historical construction of *International Central Banking*, see Feiertag and Margairaz 2012). This activity is based on multi- or bilateral cooperation agreements and mechanisms (such as *swap* arrangements) and aims to strengthen their relations and expertise in order to contribute to global monetary and financial stability. This complex and hierarchical legal and administrative framework simultaneously establishes the scope of objects, actors, and behaviors that monetary authorities seek to regulate, as well as the ends and means of such regulation. Here, Bitcoin, Ethereum, and their associated UCN* definitively fall outside the framework adopted by the majority of states and their administrations.

Within the Eurosystem to which France belongs, *money in the narrow sense* corresponds to the monetary base (or *High-Powered Money*) issued by the central bank, to which are added the demand deposits issued by second-tier banks (European Central Bank 2015a). The monetary base includes so-called fiduciary money (coins and banknotes[^11]), which for us constitute manual means of payment[^12], as well as scriptural forms (cf. reserves held by financial and monetary institutions—FMIs—at the central bank). Manual monies, issued by the sovereign authority and its representatives (the central bank and the network of national central banks within the Economic and Monetary Union), enjoy a special status benefiting from legal tender and compulsory acceptance: they cannot be refused[^13]. To this monetary base are added scriptural money and electronic money, the latter forms having been recognized most recently[^14]. Scriptural monies recorded in banking ledgers rely on recognized private issuers, who are monitored and regulated. Their circulation is ensured by means of payment such as transfers or checks. These scriptural and electronic monies do not benefit from legal tender and compulsory acceptance and are accepted voluntarily. Taken together, these forms formally constitute the euro[^15]. Legal tender and compulsory acceptance is the privilege of monies issued by the sovereign public issuer and its representatives; scriptural and electronic monies pertain to private entities with specific statuses. These entities are legally recognized, regulated, and supervised by the competent monetary authorities. National monies rest upon a hierarchical banking and financial architecture, at the apex of which stands the central bank, following a slow and uneven institutionalization process (Cohen 1998; Gilbert and Helleiner 1999a; Helleiner 2003; Feiertag and Margairaz 2012). Through the lens of these definitions, Commodity Monies (CM) initially appear as monetary monstrosities. Indeed, their *Non-Circulating Units* (NCUs) are neither national money nor foreign currencies: they are neither deposits nor claims held on a third party (natural or legal person)[^16]. Consequently, there is no central banker with whom to negotiate.

Cryptocurrencies (CCs) are difficult to integrate within such a framework. Within it, the units of account (UoAs) of CCs are considered as securities issued by private entities and in no way as money issued by a sovereign public issuer. Yet, on the other hand, their UoAs enjoy a degree of liquidity far greater than many financial products: they can be used directly for “quick” payments among economic agents who accept them (in a manner similar to the means of payment included in M1) or indirectly, by being converted into national currency, at the cost of short delays and relatively modest transaction fees, on trading platforms open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year (thus approaching the means of payment included in M2). Moreover, by their design, CCs appear to be the antithesis of the management ordinarily exercised by actors within the hierarchical system. Beyond the fact that the programmatically inelastic issuance leaves little room for the daily activities carried out by central banks to adjust their monetary policies, Kubát (2015, p. 411) highlights other peculiarities: although the money supply in circulation is easily auditable, this does not mean that all UoAs visible in the ledger are active and spendable, since a certain number of them are irretrievably lost[^17] (and thus out of circulation) due to loss of access (destruction/loss of private keys). Such a situation is inconceivable in the monetary system where the central bank is supposed to have control over monetary and financial channels. In this regard, central bankers are responsible for renewing lost and deteriorated physical currency, and access to a current account can be claimed (for example, in the event of the account holder’s death); thus, the money held is never truly inactive (*Ibid.*). But to this difference is added another: whereas “*one cannot say how many bitcoins are actually usable in the economy*” (*Ibid.*), the transparency of the ledgers facilitates the assessment of both the amounts of lost UoAs* and the velocity of circulation of those that are active, whereas for legal tender these assessments are more difficult to perform.

The difficulties in integrating cryptocurrencies (CM) into existing definitions and categories were early recognized by monetary, banking, and financial authorities—particularly central banks—as well as by certain administrations. Their characteristics raise the question: "*if it seems accepted that these are not currencies within the meaning of the Monetary and Financial Code, are they: goods (like gold)? services? In that case, are they regulated services, such as payment services or electronic money, or investment services?*" (Mariani and Marc 2014, p. 28). As infrastructures and boundary objects, they induce different viewpoints depending on the observer’s relative position (Star 1999; Trompette and Vinck 2009), explaining the visibility of the explicitation challenge they impose on various monetary authorities. Depending on the actors involved (central banks, market regulatory authorities, tax administrations, etc.) and "*in the absence of more precise current regulation*," "*the qualification of virtual currencies may be considered with regard to different branches of law that may come into conflict*" (Mariani and Marc 2014, p. 28). Cryptocurrencies compel these authorities to establish *ad hoc* qualifications and denominations (not necessarily congruent) in order to adapt their regulations.  

Early interested in the emergence of cryptocurrencies, these authorities developed a grey literature in the form of reports or information notes[^18]. It was only belatedly that they initiated a process (still ongoing) of regulatory requalification of these objects. Initially, their positions oscillated between, on the one hand, a lack of qualification (highlighting their uncertainties and difficulties in relating them to existing monetary categories) and, on the other hand, outright disqualification (due to incongruence with existing categories). At first, publications by monetary authorities were limited to providing general information on Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies, emphasizing their risks. These publications served as occasions for advice and calls for caution directed at users—both current and potential. This information was followed by an explicit acknowledgment that cryptocurrencies did not correspond (except in legal exceptions) "*to any qualification under current banking and financial regulations*": "*they are not payment instruments within the meaning of c) of Article L. 133-4 of the Monetary and Financial Code; similarly, the qualification of electronic money cannot be retained, as virtual currencies do not represent a claim on the issuer and are not issued against the delivery of funds, within the meaning of Article L. 315-1 of the Monetary and Financial Code; finally, these virtual currencies do not fall within the category of financial instruments whose list is defined in Article L. 211-1 of the Monetary and Financial Code (notably, it should be observed that Germany has* (...) *classified virtual currencies among financial instruments; to our knowledge, it is the only jurisdiction to have done so).*" (*Ibid.*, p. 29).  

Today, more than ten years after their emergence, cryptocurrencies "*still do not have an explicit legal status and their oversight by public authorities remains embryonic*" (Bercy Infos 2020) and sometimes contradictory, as illustrated by the German case. At the European level, the European Central Bank initially qualified cryptocurrencies as a "*Virtual Currency Scheme*" (VCS, European Central Bank 2012, 2015) and defined them negatively as "*representations of value not issued by a central bank, a banking institution, or an electronic money institution, which, under certain circumstances, may be used as alternatives to money*" (European Central Bank, 2015). This qualification is neither unique nor shared by the various administrations involved in the institutional fabric of the traditional monetary system. They may be grouped under the denominations of "crypto-asset," "digital currency," or "virtual currency," and more recently "cyber-currency" (a term recommended by the French State through the Commission for the Enrichment of the French Language in 2017). These disparate denominations correspond to heterogeneous and contradictory classifications. After initially considering them as goods, monetary authorities now treat them as financial assets, even though the Court of Justice of the European Union (2015) has recognized their status as a means of payment[^19].  

From a fiscal perspective, although cryptocurrencies fall within the scope of declaration and taxation of speculative capital gains, administrations struggle to establish a precise, homogeneous, and stabilized framework (see Directorate General of Public Finances 2014; Ministry of Economy and Finance 2019). Since 2019, cryptocurrencies no longer fall under the regime for the disposal of movable property (Article 150 UA; Légifrance 2018) and are now subject to a specific regime (Article 150 VH bis; Légifrance 2019). The development of these legal frameworks is a dynamic and negotiated process, not yet completed[^20]. Competent authorities still need to clarify the status they assign to these objects and, in so doing, the regulations and regulatory measures that would apply to them, as well as to the actors who use them[^21].

The theoretical corpora underpinning the critiques surveyed, despite their differences, share a common foundation: money and currency are conceived as homogeneous, unitary, and exclusive. Our thesis is that it is possible to relax one or the other of these conventional characteristics (which pertain to situated hypostases) while retaining the qualification of money. In this respect, we will assert, contrary to these views, that CM are money.

# “Yet, they function as money!” through the lens of a non-statist nominalism attentive to practices

From our perspective, one of the obstacles to recognizing the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM) lies in "*the inability of analysts to abstract themselves from what they already know: bank money, unitary and centralized, ultimately guaranteed by the State. Yet, the Internet and cryptocurrencies challenge this traditional conception of money and call into question the theory’s capacity to apprehend their specificity.*" (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, pp. 2–3). In this sense, neither the instrumental nor the substantial theory of money, nor the "chartalist" approach, fully capture the phenomenon of cryptocurrencies. They share a common bias that takes the characteristics of modern money as the unsurpassable foundation of their definition of money. They commonly conceive money through the lens of its capacity to perfectly fulfill the canonical monetary functions, and this within an economic space conceived as unitary and exclusive, thanks to a reified sovereign center. However, these approaches are not insurmountable and continue to be contested within monetary studies. Starting from the uniqueness and exclusivity of monetary phenomena rests on two intertwined hypostases: one conceives money from the viewpoint of contemporary monetary systems and the central role played by central banks (and the State), and the other postulates that this automatically produces uniqueness and homogeneity. These hypostases, which underlie the critiques of cryptocurrencies, stem from an ahistorical, counterfactual presentism that disregards both past and present history: this contemporary situation, when situated in the longue durée, is not so much the rule as the exception (Cohen 1998; Gilbert and Helleiner 1999a; Helleiner 2003; Blanc 1998a; Zelizer 1999; Steiner 2007; Polanyi 2011; Doepke and Schneider 2017). No instrument, past or present, perfectly fulfills the canonical functions of money, and exclusivity always conceals a plurality of monies and payment communities/monetary groups which, through these monies, can connect and exchange. There also exist monies outside the State’s purview. The difficulties encountered in qualifying cryptocurrencies recall those raised by parallel currencies (Blanc 1998a). To the instrumental and chartalist approaches, one can oppose more heterodox theoretical and methodological frameworks that allow cryptocurrencies to be recognized as possessing a distinctly monetary dimension. More liberal currents (libertarianism, *free banking*, etc.), despite their adherence to the market and instrumental vision of money, theorize that the presence of the State is less necessary than superfluous (when it is not the primary monetary problem). Other "non-state" nominalist currents, such as the Francophone monetary institutionalism within which we situate ourselves[^22], thus enable a different conceptualization of money. This latter current retains the vision of money as founded in rule, by nature collective and political, but does not consider the State as always essential, nor does it assume that money has everywhere and always perfectly fulfilled the canonical monetary functions. It is thus possible to see cryptocurrencies as a monetary chimera, half commodity money, half debt money (J.P. Koning 2012; Selgin 2013; Andri Olafsson 2014; Weber 2014b), and to consider that this hybridity is no longer a disqualifying factor for their qualification as money: on the contrary, it allows for the conceptualization of a new category.

## Novel Monetary Chimeras, Relegated to Speculative Uses

Authors from theoretically competing schools and corpora have highlighted the hybrid nature of cryptocurrencies (Koning 2012; Ali et al. 2013; Selgin 2013; Andolfatto 2013; J.P. Koning 2019c). Rather than attempting to subsume them under existing categories ill-suited to them, these approaches have led to uncovering the genuinely monetary dimensions of cryptocurrencies and to delineating the contours of a novel monetary category. Nevertheless, for these authors, cryptocurrencies are nonetheless conceived as “*bad*” monies[^23] (Andolfatto 2013; Selgin 2014b; Koning 2018a). These analyses nonetheless mark a step forward toward recognizing the monetary status of cryptocurrencies, since “*it must be money in order to be bad money*”[^24] (Knapp 1924, p. 1). However, they join the chorus of critics who ultimately see them only as speculative financial assets.

### Cryptocurrencies: Novel Monetary Chimeras… of Poor Quality

Contemporary free banking theorists G. Selgin (2013; 2014) and L. H. White (White 2018; White 2020) are among the liberal authors who have taken an interest in cryptocurrencies. We have already encountered these authors, as they participated in the mailing list *“Libtech-1”*, created by Szabo in 1994 (McCormack and Szabo 2019; Lars 2020; cf. Chronology no. 1, Chap. I.1.1). Selgin starts from the observation of the unprecedented hybridity of cryptocurrencies: these, indeed, “*involve characteristics both of commodity money and fiduciary money, as they are generally defined, without fitting the conventional definition of either type*”[^25]. According to him, cryptocurrencies borrow from the former their “absolute scarcity,” but differ from them by their lack of non-monetary use and their lower costs relative to metallic monies (Selgin 2012, p. 4). This latter characteristic brings them closer to fiat monies, even though their absolute scarcity sets them apart from the latter (Selgin 2014b, pp. 5–6). These hybrid characteristics define a new monetary category: that of “*synthetic commodity money*”[^26].

Despite this recognition, commodity monies (CM) are judged as not being “*ideal*” because they are too imperfect. To the great dismay of coiners, who made *free banking* their theoretical banner and who saw Selgin as “*the perfect gateway drug to Bitcoin*” (Farrington 2021), the latter, like all other commentators, regards the inelasticity of CM money supply, such as Bitcoin, as fundamentally problematic. While he suggests “*that a synthetic commodity monetary regime might outperform existing fiat money regimes*,” this refers to a system where the money supply remains elastic (Selgin 2014b). The error of bitcoiners is to “*compare Bitcoin to gold as an investment asset*,” whereas “*economists, by contrast, are more interested in comparing a Bitcoin-based monetary system with a gold standard monetary system*” (White 2018). Regarding the “*similarities and* [*differences between the Bitcoin system and the gold standard*],” these authors emphasize primarily the “*differences in supply mechanisms*” which, like bitcoiners, are poorly regarded because they imply a problematic deflationary dynamic (*Ibid.*; Selgin 2014b). If CM were to establish themselves as a monetary system, their limited supply dynamics, coupled with a highly volatile demand and velocity of circulation of unbacked crypto-notes (UCN), would lead to instability in both the general price level and the nominal expenditure level, with a hoarding phenomenon incentivized by the appreciation of their price (Selgin 2014b; Selgin 2014a; White 2018). Ultimately, from their perspective and contrary to that of the Coiners, CM will struggle to play the role of a store of value instrument due to their instability: CM will “*not be likely* [*to replace the dollar*],” because “*a Bitcoin-based monetary system could prove incompatible with macroeconomic stability*” and it is “*unlikely*” “*that Bitcoin will ever become a generally accepted and widely used medium for everyday payments, that is, ‘money’*” or “*a ‘currency’*” (Selgin 2013, pp. 23–24)[^27].

### Paradoxical and Syncretic Critiques, Confined CM to the Role of Financial Asset

[^27]: See Selgin, G. (2013). *Synthetic Commodity Money*. Cato Journal, 33(2), 221–238.

It must be acknowledged that the preceding critiques *“lack clarity”*, and even foster *“a genuine confusion”* regarding commodity monies (CM), particularly *“within the economic discipline”* (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, p. 2). The paradoxical nature of these prior critiques, in our view, highlights the limitations of the theoretical frameworks underpinning them. In the absence of “intrinsic” value—since they are not backed by any commodity—CM would not qualify as money, akin to paper currencies which, at the time of their emergence, aroused the suspicion of many analysts who regarded them as a pure *“fraud since they are based on nothing”* (J. Adams cited by John Kenneth Galbraith 1976, p. 58). However, these reasonings, grounded in “substantialist” conceptions of money, are unsatisfactory because they risk excluding modern credit monies, which are not convertible into metal. Moreover, it is doubtful that the authors employing this critique would be comfortable aligning themselves with “gold bugs,” who condemn the advent of fiat currencies and the abandonment of gold convertibility of national currencies in the nineteenth century or following the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in the 1970s.

At the cost of a problematic syncretism, some actors, such as Krugman (2018b), discuss fiat currencies by invoking the concept of intrinsic value, which for them encompasses state regulations and guarantees, whereas historically, substantialist views used this concept to oppose in essence “commodity monies” to “debt monies,” contrary to nominalist approaches that defended the monetary status of the latter (de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003). In a theoretical stretch, this argument aligns with “nominalist” arguments in their “chartalist” version. According to their “digital metallism” (Maurer et al., 2014; Mallard et al., 2018), which relies on a coinage disconnected from credit and states, CM would not be able to claim monetary status.

This argument must confront the case of metallic systems: can one indeed maintain that perfectly convertible monies were not money, since they were not directly linked to the issuance of pure credit[^28]? Or that, outside the monopolization of monetary issuance by a national central bank, there is no money? Numerous monetary experiments outside the framework of the nation-state and its elected representative have existed historically (central banks and nation-states are only modern constructs), and also exist presently (the euro with a central bank independent of member states, the phenomenon of parallel currencies, dollarization in Latin America, the Iraqi Swiss dinar[^29], etc.). Should one therefore conclude that the means of payment in circulation are not money? Again, it is doubtful that the authors who have mobilized these chartalist arguments would accept such consequences.

These various critical authors, coming from opposing schools of thought, nevertheless agree on one argument: cryptocurrencies (CM) constitute a new class of high-risk and highly speculative financial assets. According to them, a speculative euphoria akin to the seventeenth-century “Tulipomania”[^30] is replayed with cryptocurrencies. CM are nothing but “*Bubble, fraud, and turmoil*” (Krugman 2018b), “*a natural Ponzi scheme*” (Shiller, cited by Krugman 2018b), whose “*price will fall to zero if confidence disappears*” (a view also shared by Shiller, 2018). Chapter I demonstrated how the speculative use of cryptocurrencies was essential to stimulate their infrastructural development. It is certain that cryptocurrencies are perfect “*objects of speculation*” (Kindelberger 2004, p. 50): innovative objects; floating exchange rates subject solely to market forces; limited supply, both due to protocol-based issuance and their high degree of hoarding and concentration; and a constantly growing demand[^31]. All the ingredients are present for them to become the object of mimetic polarization within the purely self-referential logic of a “*Keynesian beauty contest*” (André Orléan 1989; Koning 2018b). But are these arguments disqualifying with respect to their qualification as money? Even the critic Tirole (2017) acknowledges that no currency is exempt from either a speculative dimension (hence the existence of “forex” markets), or a self-referential dimension (money can be conceived as the quintessential self-referential object; see André Orléan 1998; Ingham 2007), or volatility (the degree of which serves as a criterion for characterizing economic and monetary crises, cf. inflation, or even hyperinflation)[^32].

Starting from the speculative and playful dimension, an element from which a significant part of their demand derives, Koning (2018b; 2018d; 2018c; 2018a; 2019c; 2019a; 2020b) offers a more refined and nuanced qualification: cryptocurrencies, Bitcoin foremost among them, would partly constitute a new "type of financial betting game," an "early bird game" benefiting the first entrants (Koning 2018d). Cryptocurrencies stand in contrast to traditional financial securities or monetary means of payment, which he classifies within the family of positive-sum games ("win-win opportunity") recognized as having a productive dimension: the concerned assets enable issuing companies to generate more resources than those initially contributed by the security holders, hence the fact that they yield income (dividends or interest). Conversely, cryptocurrencies are declared non-productive and are placed in "the category of bets and hedges," zero-sum games (for every winner, there is a loser) akin to "insurance, futures and options contracts, as well as various games of chance such as lotteries" (Koning 2019c). Nevertheless, such speculative demand is not likely to abate soon, since there exists "a constant demand for ponzis from willing and informed individuals" (Koning 2018d), as evidenced by the enthusiasm for all kinds of gambling. This author nonetheless highlights notable differences with Ponzi schemes and "chain letters"[^33]: the absence of centralization characteristic of Ponzi schemes and the impossibility of exploiting the scheme through falsification, which is found in chain letters, with their users enjoying a transparency regarding their rules and functioning far superior to these two systems (*Ibid*).

Considering cryptocurrencies (CM) as financial assets is certainly not erroneous: a currency can serve as a foreign exchange currency. Its monetary essence then evaporates, and it becomes a portfolio asset like any other. However, this usage is reductive and confines them to a status that is neither primary nor exclusive. This issue brings us back to the consideration of the degree of liquidity at the heart of the empirical definition of the money supply adopted by authorities. An asset that experiences successive phases of euphoria and panic rarely retains its liquidity, as evidenced by the tulip episode. In this respect, cryptocurrencies appear as singular assets. The question remains open: does the inadequacy of cryptocurrencies with respect to dominant monetary theories prove that they cannot legitimately claim to be money? Or does their very existence call into question the capacity of these theories to analyze monetary phenomena in all their dimensions? Like other authors who call for a renewal of the approach, we consider the second hypothesis to be true: "*bitcoin challenges* [the] *popular monetary theories*" that "*treat money as a noun, and not as an adjective*" (Koning 2012). In this regard, the works of Koning (2012; 2013; 2018f; 2018a; 2018b; 2018e; 2019c; 2019a; 2020b) are interesting for their ambivalence. On the one hand, they illustrate the monetary positions we critique; on the other, they develop an approach in terms of “monetization” akin to ours: money is conceived less as a thing referring to objects than as a state qualifying them according to their degree of monetization, that is, liquidity[^34]. This interest in “monetization” opens onto our own research questions since it lies at the heart of the francophone monetary institutionalism that we mobilize. Presented in the introduction, it is now incumbent upon us to specify its contours and to explicate the two argumentative frameworks which, when transposed to cryptocurrencies, allow us, in our view, to overcome the previous critiques.

## Francophone Monetary Institutionalism: a Non-State Nominalism Capable of Encompassing Cryptocurrencies

Beyond their differing theoretical foundations, the critiques surveyed share the perspective of conceiving money and currency (in all their forms) as homogeneous, unitary, centralized, and exclusive. In this respect, they reveal a common "*incapacity* [...] *to abstract from* [...] *bank money, unitary and centralized, ultimately guaranteed by the State*" (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, pp. 2–3). The positions of E. Assouan (2018) and Selgin (2014a, p. 2) are representative: for the former, "*a true currency, like the euro, is a universal unit of account and is accepted by all merchants because its value is guaranteed*," whereas the latter requires that money be a "*generally accepted means of payment*" without specifying the scope of this universal/general acceptance, nor how, nor by whom it is defined. Authors who start from the hybridity of cryptocurrencies, although granting them a "*certain degree of money*," nevertheless regard them as "*poor means of exchange and* [of] *store of value*" (Koning 2018e), according to classical monetary functions. These critiques, through their functionalism, reiterate instrumental and market-based representations of money, as evidenced by recurrent references to monetary functions and the primacy given to "*the function of an exchange instrument* [...] *guided by the vision of commodity money*" (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 13). This is reflected in Selgin’s term "*synthetic commodity money*." These critiques converge on reifying, *aprioristic*, *ahistorical*, and *asocial* visions, within which the qualities of "good" or "bad" money are exogenously prejudged, above the actors.  

However, in our view, addressing the question of "*how to think about money*"[^35] requires decentering from the classical conception of money and the study of the functional and market characteristics of monetary media, which are neither primary nor sufficient. Following Knapp (1924), we hold that focusing on the materiality of the money object constitutes "*a grave error*," and those who do so, like the "*numismatist cannot learn much about* [...] *money, because* [they deal only with] *its corpse*" (Knapp 1924, p. 1)[^36]. This "forensic" interest, shared by the aforementioned critiques, is incomplete, partial, and biased from the perspective of the IMF and the pure nominalism from which it originates (cf. Box 2). On the other hand, we prefer to Knapp’s approach those of Simmel[^37] (2009) or Polanyi, according to whom neither the (formal) law nor the State (which establishes it) constitutes the unique and exclusive germ of money.  

In the manner of Koning, the term money (and liquidity, of which it is the ultimate form) is for us less a noun referring to a thing than an adjective qualifying it: hence the use of the term monetization. Through this prism, how can one fail to see that "*bitcoins have always been money. A certain degree of money was attached to them from the moment the first bitcoin user realized that their bitcoins could be exchanged with another user for something else.* [...] *If you treat money as an adjective, the regression theorem and the theory of charts are not relevant. Whether money is attached to a token issued by the government to pay taxes, or whether it is attached to a precious object for religious or industrial purposes, is irrelevant. The degree of liquidity—or monetization—is the variable of interest*" (Koning 2012). No object is monetary in itself, and any object can, in an appropriate situation, play the role of money: the material characteristics of the money object matter less than the situations in which it is used as such and why it is used as such.

### Beyond Money, Cash: Questioning Monetization in Light of Uses

This decentering marks the programmatic renewal of the 1980s in the monetary field, involving sociologists, historians, anthropologists, ethnographers, and institutionalist economists of the IMF school. This renewal, "*concomitant with the decline of economic theories of value that claimed to explain the formation of economic values by non-monetary criteria,*" emerges from the desire to overcome the limits of this classical approach to money (Blanc 2009a, p. 3) and its "narrow meaning"[^38]. Based on a shared ethnographic foundation interested in uses and practices, these works seek to understand "*the social meanings of money, the variations in the modes and extent of its uses, as well as those of the boundaries and forms of the currency through which money exists*" as a "*social bond*" (Blanc 2009a, p. 1). This theoretical approach replaces the orthodox theory of utility/rarity value with a theory of monetary value, where money is essential to decentralized economic coordination, since no intrinsic value preexists exchanges: monetary mediation is consubstantial with both, and all value is monetary (and extrinsic). Money becomes crucial for understanding the market, linking decentralized transactions (micro-imbalances) and their interdependencies (macro-equilibrium) in a context of radical uncertainty and default risk (absent from the theory of value). Thus, the IMF treats money as a "*total social fact*"[^39], the product of a shifting institutional entanglement, "*not only of money itself, but also of individual relations mediated by*" it (Blanc 2009a, pp. 3–4).

[^38]: See Blanc 2009a for a detailed critique of classical monetary theory.
[^39]: The concept of "total social fact" is borrowed from Marcel Mauss’s seminal work on gift exchange.

Understanding money in all its dimensions requires going beyond the study of the generic characteristics of mediums, for it is less a “*material and physical fact*” than “*a social fact*,” “*an institution, a faith*” (Mauss 1914, pp. 3–4). The IFM, following the “*unifying approach proposed by Dufy and Weber (2007)*” and through the analysis of money’s uses, aims to account for the social meanings of money, the plurality of monetary instruments, and the modalities of their uses and interrelations (Blanc 2009a, p. 2). Priority must be given to understanding the practices and uses of money, recognizing the importance of both (individual) representations and (collective) institutional arrangements that are articulated in any monetary phenomenon. Through this prism, and since money first appears as a “*specific set of relations taking various forms—symbolic (unit of account, seal, signature), material (means of payment: coins, banknotes, entries), institutional (rules of accounting, payment, issuance, exchange)*” (Théret 2008, p. 7), money cannot be reduced “*to currency*” conceived as “*a socially, if not economically, neutral medium capable of measuring, mediating, and preserving wealth*” (Blanc 2009a, p. 1). From this follows a conceptual distinction between money, as the social abstraction of value, and currency, referring to the “*manual means of payment*” on which this value rests through monetization (*ibid.*). Several pitfalls must thus be avoided. First, that of integrating currency into the sphere of goods, to which it is opposed by definition (Simmel 2009); second, that of conflating the absence of a monetary medium with the existence of a non-monetary society; and finally, that of mobilizing the hypostasis of currency in all uses, ideationally bearing the canonical monetary functions (cf. section I.2.3 below).

Contrary to the economic myth of barter, we concur with others that no society was ever “*completely devoid*” of the notion of money, even in the absence of a market system. If money serves a function, it is primitively that of being an operator of social belonging. It constitutes a constructed relationship to the always situated social totality, within which the values of the society in question are expressed and affirmed (or not). From this perspective, it is futile to seek, as Menger (1892) did, the “origin” of money in the characteristics of mediums and solely individual interests. To think about money requires focusing less on *what* *circulates* than on *how* it circulates. The materiality of means of payment matters less than the “monetary state”[^40] attributed to them, by *fusion*, according to usages taking place within a situated economic, social, and political environment. Hence the fact that, while the economists’ conception of money “*primarily refers to a problem of quantity, money* \[primarily refers to\] *a set of qualities*” (Blanc 2009a, p. 1). Our research object is less money than *money* conceived as a process of *monetization*, dynamic and multidimensional, articulating the two. Passing “*the test of ‘monetization’ depends on the satisfaction of two conditions*” that transfigure money into an institution, following an assignment process that is less economic than socio-political: to establish itself “*as a measure of abstract value (unit of account) (Keynes 1930; Grierson 1977; Hicks 1989; Hoover 1996); and as a means of storing and transporting this abstract value*” (Ingham 2004, p. 70). Money as a social relation and a measure thereof constructs a “unified” institutional space, a community. Within it, the commensurable, the comparable, and the payable (and, negatively, the incommensurable, the non-comparable, and the non-payable) are collectively defined, according to institutional devices and arrangements.

### Money as a Payment System: Monetization and Liquidity of Heterogeneous Debts

From the foregoing, the IMF acknowledges that money cannot be reduced to *a single institution,* since, empirically, it is “*simultaneously a system of signs (language), a system of objects (materiality), and a system of rules (institution)*” (Théret 2008, p. 2). To grasp the complexity of the monetization phenomenon, the IMF has developed a conceptual framework centered on the concepts of debt, trust, and sovereignty (shared with monetary heterodoxy, cf. Box 3). First, three simultaneous modes of money’s presence in the world (termed states), whose articulation it is imperative to analyze, are distinguished. Previous critiques, anchored in their functionalist, market-based, and unitary primer of money, say nothing about this articulation. These critiques focus solely on the “*objectified state,*” “*identifiable in monetary instruments serving as means of payment,*” thereby obscuring the essential: the “*incorporated state* [...] *where it appears as a standard of value and trust* [and the] *institutionalized state, that is, the various rules and regulations that unify a monetary space governed by an accounting system and constituting a payment community*” (*Ibid.*, p. 16). Moreover, money is more commonly conceived as a payment system, whose presence is sufficiently guaranteed by three characteristics (Cartelier, 1996, pp. 76–77): first, the establishment of a nominal unit of account; second, the existence of a “coinage,” that is, a set of rules determining the manner in which the nominal units of account are put into circulation (cf. modalities of access to means of payment outside receipts from others); and finally, the presence of a framework for settling balances, since the existence of nonzero balances implies restructurings of the coinage medium capable of restoring payment equilibrium.

At the core of this apparatus lies a nominal unit of account that enables the preliminary measurement of economic value, without which exchanges would lack terms to be honored. The primacy (both historical and logical[^41]) of this usage as *money of account* is asserted: from it derives the usage in payment/exchange. These usages (and not functions), considered as “*primary concepts in a theory of money*” (Keynes cited in *Ibid.*, p. 8), constitute the “*generic (and functional) properties*” of money, distinct from “*non-monetary uses*,” such as the store of value function (*Ibid.*, p. 2). The unit of account integrates money into metrology, presenting itself as a “*semantic system, similar* [...] *to language, writing, or weights and measures*” (Polanyi 2011, pp. 163–164), structuring the language of economic value and allowing for the quantitative evaluation of heterogeneous debt and credit relations, forged among members participating in the division of labor within the society under consideration (Cartelier 1996; Aglietta and Cartelier 1998). This mediating language of diverse debt and credit relations[^42] irreducibly refers to a collective and holistic dimension (the establishment of a language as a system of rules and signs recognized by a community cannot be reduced to an individual logic). The socially recognized space of the commensurable, the exchangeable, and the payable is founded in money and its nominal unit of account, which underlie our relation to ourselves, as economic subjects, and to others. In this respect, and since no linguist would claim that a language exists solely by virtue of the presence of institutions formally setting its usage framework, money lives primarily through those who employ it, sometimes outside of this framework. This touches upon the “incorporated state,” the most individual and micro level of analysis, where money “*is first and foremost a mental activity*” structuring “*the very person of its users*, [making] *part of their habitus,* [which] *is inscribed in their system of dispositions*” (Théret 2008, p. 16). Money is a collective belief, the result of an agreement among group members, reflecting the perceived or real ties of individuals with society, each having to see in it a legitimate expression of value (Orléan 2005).

The diversity of debt relationships mediated by money highlights the issue of the quality of claims and uncertainties (cf.* payment default and systemic risk, Théret 2008, p. 16), and thus of liquidity. This raises the question of institutional palliatives such as regulations, hierarchy, and authority (which may become power[^43]), serving as vectors of trust, security, and stability (pointing to our other monetary states). The signature of money pertains to a *signified* and requires *signifying* objects to be invested, as a tangible symbol (“*objectified state*”). Regardless of the means of payment, their monetization depends on the assessment of their liquidity by various actors, liquidity conceived as “*desirability (and thus acceptability) in the eyes of others*” (Dequech 2013, p. 255). It is supported by the trust that all have in this future acceptance and in the perceived qualities that guarantee it[^44]. It is under these conditions that money becomes the operator of “*the accounting and* \[the\] *settlement of debts*” and that the payment instruments backed by it are allowed to behave *de facto* as such (Ingham 2004, p. 25). The symbols of political authority cherished by chartalists, which are affixed to it, or the acceptance of money in the settlement of fiscal obligations, attest to this trust to be built among all and to the “institutionalized state” where money presents itself as the political form of a community. Yet nothing dictates that any human community—and thereby any currency—cannot be instituted outside the exclusive domain of nation-states, a proposition as reifying as it is anachronistic and counterfactual.

### Money Put to the Test: When Debts, Trust, and Sovereignty Confront Each Other

Our conception of money, first as a set of rules shaping a unit of account and its material representations, gives rise to a series of issues concerning the authorities who formulate these rules, verify their application, sanction breaches, and issue the material representations of the unit of account, as well as the trust and legitimacy granted to the authorities, the rules, and the money itself. We advocate a “non-state” nominalism whose conventionalism opposes chartalism (Dequech 2013, pp. 252–253), leading us to analyses demonstrating “*sometimes from an empirical point of view* \[cf. section I.2.3\]*, sometimes from a doctrinal point of view* \[this section\]*, that the state dimension of sovereignty cannot annex the notion of monetary sovereignty.*” (Blanc 2009a, p. 6). Certainly, “*money must be issued. And something can only be issued as money if it is capable of canceling any debt contracted by the issuer*” (Ingham 2004, p. 25). The institutional space encompassed by money contains an entanglement of diverse forms of claims, issued by heterogeneous issuers and circulating in various spheres, which calls into question the trust placed in them according to the quality of the signatures or seals affixed, their intertwinement, and the defaults implied by repudiation of some claims. From this, and depending on the personality of the issuers and the quality of their claims, public monies are distinguished from private monies and securities, and, as explained, why each cannot ensure the same level of trust (Orléan 1998, cited by Scialom 2003).

Private monies, like financial securities, rest on a logic of signature, based on contractual (private law) foundations, which by nature renders them fragile: the quality of the signature and thus the trust in the money it guarantees refers to “*a local evaluation of the specific risks attached to such and such a debt*,” that is, to the credibility and solvency—real or anticipated—of issuers, which can be abruptly called into question (cf. default; André Orléan 1998, p. 8). Conversely, public monies rest on a logic of seals, purely fiduciary in nature, endowing them with superior qualities and liquidity: the issuer is a sovereign representative (under public law) whose signature quality is unmatched. Trust in the enduring validity of this claim relies on collective, public, and institutional guarantees and, ultimately, on the permanence of states and their fiscal systems, which maintain the desirability of the money issued by the state or its representatives. This logic of the seal is found at the foundation of central money, so-called first-tier money in contemporary hierarchical systems.

We ourselves recognize the state’s key role in modern monetary phenomena, its administrations establishing and regulating the national currency and the modalities of conversion with other currencies (Gilbert and Helleiner 1999a, p. 11). However, the monetary phenomenon is not reducible to this alone. Money is “*less decreed than elected*” (Jean Michel Servet, Théret and Yildirim 2016, p. 30), since monetization is less a royal act than “*a collective process of election by which a group of producers and exchangers of goods constructs its cohesion by bringing forth a common representation of value unanimously accepted* [...] ; *it is the unanimous agreement of the group that underpins the monetary institution.*” (André Orléan 2019, p. 1). It is thus understandable that any embodied sovereignty must always face “*in one way or another* \[the\] *sovereignty of money users*” (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, invoking J. Rueff’s concept, p. 23). The legitimacy of the state and its laws is tested and contested, endogenously (through renegotiation of the rules of coinage and settlement regulations) or exogenously (as with the phenomenon of parallel currencies). Money appears as *ambivalent*: a dialectic unfolds between individuals and the collective, irreducibly political. Proof that “*money does not belong to the state*”: even the most radical coercion (the death penalty) fails to guarantee the effective legal and forced tender status of the “*revolutionary assignats*” (Orléan 2002, p. 27).

Starting from the trust inherent in the monetary fact, as the IMF does, allows us to understand that no trust is ever imposed from above. It originates from below, and those who grant it entrust it only temporarily, retaining the power to withdraw it as swiftly as it was given: the negative counterpart, distrust, thus emerges. Trust (and its opposite) pertains to a multidimensional phenomenon based on a diversity of actors, institutional arrangements, relationships, and social representations. This idea of an institutional web producing or failing to produce trust lies at the heart of the IMF’s work. Simmel (2009) articulated this through his theorization of an infinite monetary *continuum* (evolving but not univocal, ranging from “barter” to “pure” monetary exchange), grounded in trust whose underlying mechanisms undergo shifts that move it from more individual guarantees (so-called “centrifugal” trust) to others that are more collective and institutional (“centripetal” trust)[^45]. 

If money is a collective construct, trust is first enacted within each individual: there lies a self-referential and mimetic desire for liquidity, a crystallization of tension between individuals and the collective(s) in which they are embedded (André Orléan 1998; Aglietta and Orléan 2002). This self-referentiality underpins the status of ultimate liquidity and bearer claim on the entire social body. The holding of money as a social promise, while always referring to the collective, simultaneously transforms into an individual power over economic circulation (or hoarding), outlining an “ambivalence” of money (and the liquidity it embodies[^46], Aglietta 1988). 

This multidimensionality of the trust phenomenon is also captured by the distinction of three levels, of different orders, which articulate in a singular manner within any monetary system (Aglietta and Orléan 1998; Théret 2008; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015; Blanc and Fare 2017). The most individual level pertains to what is called *methodical* trust, referring to conditions of use and the mimetic nature of individual behaviors, constituting a form of routine belief in the collective acceptance of means of payment. *Hierarchical* trust refers to the fact that money is always guaranteed by one or more sovereign authorities, hence hierarchies between issuers and means of payment. Finally, *ethical* trust activates the authority of the normative system (norms and values) and implies that monetary regulation ensures the reproduction of society in accordance with its constitutive values. Previous analyses appear myopic to this last, yet essential, dimension. For faith in the stability of money as a unit of account to exist, these three dimensions must be hierarchically and coherently articulated in the eyes not only of the state and its representatives but also of the citizens (Aglietta and Orléan, 1998; Théret, 2008; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015). Without this, distrust prevails.

Questioning the liquidity of means of payment, regardless of their forms, reveals their social nature and connection to political sovereignty, as well as the dialectical relationship between individual and collective logics, and between authorities and the legitimacy granted by members of society, which plays out in the process of monetization. In his *Treatise on Money* of 1360, Nicolas Oresme warned against fully attributing monetary sovereignty to the prince and the person who embodies it: for “*although, for the common good*,” it is incumbent upon him to affix “*his mark on the coin*,” he does not thereby become “*the master or owner*”! As the “*standard of the exchange of natural riches; it is therefore the possession of those to whom these riches belong*,” those very individuals whom the prince merely represents (Oresme, p. 54 cited by Desmedt and Piégay 2007, p. 130). Oresme interrogates this tension and the content of monetary governance: for him, while the debasement of coinage was legitimate, it raises the question of whether such power should reasonably be entrusted solely to the prince (Tutin 2009, p. 10).  

Considering money as “a social bond” demands consistency, since it presupposes the existence of a relational space (non-univocal) that money helps to constitute, “*as is forcefully evidenced by the fact that it always presents itself backed by a payment community*” (André Orléan 2019, p. 1). Conceptualized by Knapp as early as 1905, a payment community designates the entirety of actors who identify with the national monetary system, insofar as they share the same unit of account, the same principles of coinage, and debt settlement (Blanc 1998a, p. 7). Attached to every payment community is a “*symbolic universe*,” a “*homogeneous space of representations characterized by a hierarchy of values and norms*,” which “*imposes itself on actors through the definition of the framework within which their practices take place and monetary forms acquire meaning*” (Blanc 2009a, p. 12). This payment community pertains to a “*proper sovereignty, vested in specific persons or institutions*,” which is never given a priori, since it is constructed, developed, and dynamically tested, as it is traversed by differing interests (*Ibid.*).  

Thus, one can distinguish “monetary groups” conceived as “*subsets hierarchically embedded within the payment community*” which, while “*respecting its common principles,*” nonetheless reject some of them, hence the fact that their members may “*refuse to use certain monetary instruments in favor of others specific to them*” (*Ibid.*). Each monetary group belongs to its own symbolic universe, which defines limits and guides the social uses of money for itself and its members. This plurality simultaneously raises questions regarding the conditions for articulating systems with one another *via* the establishment of conversion procedures (*Ibid.*).

The preceding framework, with a universal scope, encompasses all currencies, whether metallic or fiat, without opposing them. It allows, through the analysis of their coinage and the regulations in place, to describe and analyze the diversity of media as well as the evolution of monetary norms and institutional arrangements. Once situated within the IMF, we shall see that the two main bodies of arguments mobilized against cryptocurrencies—the synchronic uniqueness of canonical functions and the jurisdictional exclusivity of money—do not hold.

## Beyond Instrumentalist and Chartalist Critiques: Cryptocurrencies Neither Unitary nor Exclusive

Thus, we challenge the definitions of money “*by what it does*” employed by critics of cryptocurrencies, arguing that “*money must a priori be considered as an entity* *to be defined not by functions relative to an externality, but by its own constitutive properties*” (Théret 2008, p. 6). Being simultaneously a signifier and a fundamental signified that underpins a payment community, it is only through the lens of the respective properties of Bitcoin and Ethereum, that is, from the perspective of their users, that one may legitimately assess their monetary dimensions and the qualities attributed to them. This is also what ethnographic inquiry allows us to assert: analyzing money as a system that is both ideational and material requires the study of situated uses, where a complex intertwining of actors, groups, institutional devices and arrangements, values, and representations—more or less homogeneous—occurs (Dufy and Weber 2007). This is consistent with one of the central theses of Chapter I: the phenomenon of cryptocurrencies can only be truncated if it is reduced solely to their protocol *without taking into account the relational and carnivalesque dynamics inherent to their monetization*. Materially, such a reduction would amount to overlooking the composite ensemble of sociotechnical arrangements beyond protocols that sustain their use and the actors involved therein. Ideationally, it amounts to imposing analysts’ representations against those of the very actors themselves, thereby disregarding their own monetary expectations.  

The controversy permeating the monetary field does not require a crisis to emerge: systematic analysis of practices demonstrates that all societies experience the synchronous presence of usages and, consequently, of particular monies, both within and alongside traditional payment systems (Zelizer 1989; Zelizer 2005; Blanc 1998a; Blanc 2009a). A “practical” conflictuality expresses itself in the (more or less formal) forms of differentiation or indifferentiation *of monies* in circulation, which contribute to imposing (or not) modalities of conversion. At stake are the very conditions of circulation, as well as the reproduction of shared trust in the stability and durability of the payment system. This plurality of practices demonstrates the diversity of symbolic universes around which disparate monetary groups may be constituted.

Ultimately, the heterogeneity of monetary practices contrasts with the characteristics of uniqueness, exclusivity, and full synchronicity of canonical monetary functions, which are today axiomatized in both theory and practice. By liberating money from reifying constraints, it becomes possible to interrogate the institutional, collective, and political conditions that govern (or fail to govern) the dynamic maintenance of the uniqueness and stability of the monetary system under consideration. Contrary to critiques focused on the synchronic presence of monetary functions, we will observe that national currencies themselves are less homogeneous than they appear when, simultaneously and alongside them, a diversity of parallel currencies exists. From these observations and the practices of the “coiners,” it becomes possible to delineate usages in accounting, payment, and store of value.

### The Irreducible Heterogeneity of Currencies Past and Present

When one focuses on the actual uses of money—both past and present—money appears as “*fragmentary in terms of instruments*,” appearing homogeneous and unitary only “*in terms of the system*” (Blanc 2008, p. 11): the only universally valid monetary rule is that the uses of money mobilize, everywhere and always, a multiplicity of objects (not perfectly homogeneous nor fungible among themselves), pertaining to differentiated uses and functions. The centrality accorded to monetization is thus reaffirmed, since systemic uniqueness among a multiplicity of monies is not given but constructed and institutionalized, without it being possible to exclude the phenomenon of parallel currencies (CM) therein.

Polanyi (2011) is credited with systematically challenging the hypostatization of money as a universally and perpetually “*All Purpose Money*” (APM), perfectly embodying the canonical monetary functions (*Ibid.*, p. 9). He distinguished so-called “*primitive and archaic*” monetary systems from “*modern*” ones. The former corresponded to “*Special Purpose Money*” (SPM), characterized by the coexistence of a diversity of monetary objects fulfilling differentiated functions, structuring plural and compartmentalized economic spaces. The work of historians, anthropologists, and ethnographers confirms this: there are numerous “*examples where the functions of medium of exchange and unit of account do not coincide* [, such as] *in medieval Europe,* [where] *the separation of the different functions of money was the rule rather than the exception*” (cf. Spufford (1988) and Kindleberger (1993), Doepke and Schneider 2017, pp. 1–2). Historically, monetary functions were only partially carried by a heterogeneous variety of media, more or less convertible among themselves, pertaining to specific monetary uses, actors, norms, and channels of monetary circulation (cf. the separation between “treasure” and “commodity money,” Polanyi 2011, Chap. 9). Within the national space, strong competition existed between the national currency and other types of money in circulation, such as sub-national currencies (issued by private or infra-state entities) and foreign currencies. It was less easy to eliminate this competition than to accommodate it; thus, political authorities had an interest “*in attracting as many foreign currencies as possible onto their territory*,” “*tolerating these specie, or even* [in] *giving them legal tender status*,” since “*the prohibition of illicit* [and] *foreign specie was illusory*,” while “*local currency remained insufficient in quantity*.” [Moreover,] “*even when state structures are relatively strong, the monopoly cannot be established over monetary circulation* (Favier 1981, p. 177) [when the] *sovereign did not possess the privilege of taxation, because numerous non-state para-fiscal circuits existed*” (Desmedt and Piégay 2007, p. 124).

Nevertheless, for Polanyi (1944; 2011), “modern” currencies, following the “Great Transformation,” would have become pure Market Price Mechanisms (MPMs), unitary and integrated, perfectly fulfilling the canonical monetary functions, unlike the Subsidiary Price Mechanisms (SPMs), which are conceived as costly and inefficient. This distinction between SPMs and MPMs already demonstrates the partial and situated nature of definitions of money, given the historical prevalence of SPM systems. These considerations thus make it impossible to exclude “imperfect” monetary forms from the monetary field based on historically situated MPMs, which represent only a small fraction thereof. Polanyi postulated that the heterogeneity and non-synchronicity of monetary functions belong to a bygone past, and that SPMs had definitively given way to MPMs. It is from this MPM standard that Contemporary Monies (CM) diverge too far. However, contemporary research demonstrates that the MPM framework also involves a hypostasis: as modern as they may be, our contemporary fiat monies are less unitary than they appear (Melitz [1970] cited in Blanc 2009, p. 11; Zelizer 1989; 1999; 2000; 2005). Despite their high degree of uniqueness and integration, modern monies are subject to “social markings” emanating from three sources, which depend on the identity of the holder (organization or individual, gender, activity, and community of belonging) and occur at different moments in the sequence of monetary operations (Blanc 2009a, pp. 13–15) [^47]. To this endogenous heterogeneity within the national currency is added the phenomenon of parallel currencies (cf. next subsection). Money is heterogeneous from all sides, which renews the question of the modalities of conversion and the actors who perform it (official approval in the case of banks and financial institutions, mint authorities in metallic systems, but also street money changers).

Finally, as in the past, the dollar and the euro are assigned in their uses to controls and restrictions that limit their liquidity and fungibility. The qualitative differentiations produced by these markings call into question their full capacity to synchronically perform the canonical functions. Hence, money, or more precisely monies (Zelizer’s “*monies*,” 1989), are everywhere and always *SPM* (Socially Produced Monies). It thus becomes impossible to assert, as a criticism against the CM, that the “*euro is universally accepted*,” as E. Assouan (2018) claims. The universality of the euro is relative, as with any currency. Already, the European Union is not the universe, and the euro, like the dollar, does not have the status of an international currency, since its European circulation is confined only to countries engaged in the monetary union. And even within the monetary union, can one truly say that every euro is “*accepted by all*”? As we have seen in Section 1, legal tender and forced circulation are subject to exceptions: such as the obligation to provide exact change or the possibility of merchants refusing payment under the principle of “good faith” (Article L. 112-5 of the French Monetary and Financial Code). Legally, the “*euro*” includes private scriptural monies, whose acceptance, as we have seen, is far from universal since they do not enjoy the legal tender and forced circulation that manual monies issued by the central authority benefit from. Thus, money, whether dollar or euro, indeed “has a smell,” particularly when it is “dirty,” marked with the seal of “illegality” (for example, the ink-staining of stolen banknotes in an ATM, Koning 2018d), requiring “laundering” through specific procedures and devices in order not to lose its liberatory power (Blanc 2008, p. 19).

### National currencies and parallel monies: an always contested monetary space

The exclusivity of their circulation within the state jurisdictional space is neither full nor absolute. The challenge to *SPM* did not occur overnight but rather reflects a difficult, long, tumultuous, and never completed process of homogenization: consubstantial with the formation of nation-states, it stems from political conflicts and negotiations whose legitimacy is tested (consider the “*National Banking Act*” of 1863 and the controversial emergence of the “*greenbacks*,” Zelizer 1989). At the beginning of the U.S. process, nearly 5,000 currencies circulated (excluding counterfeits) issued by different entities, and even today, although this number has decreased, heterogeneity persists: as illustrated by the diversity of banknotes and “*promises*” they bear (Koning 2017)[^48], or by the fact that not every legal currency has forced circulation (such as bank cards, checks, certain large denominations; cf. Section III.1.2).

[^48]: See Koning, R. (2017). *Title of the referenced work*. Publisher.

National spaces still today witness the coexistence of numerous parallel monetary instruments, often during crises but also in other contexts (Blanc 1998, 2006, 2009b). Blanc (1998b, p. 4) enumerates 465 such instruments worldwide over the period 1988–1996. This generic term encompasses a polymorphic reality. The phenomenon refers to the use, in payment and/or accounting, of units of account and means of payment different from those of the national monetary system, and indeed raises typological issues due to situated experiences that follow a dual movement of extension and diversification (Blanc and Fare 2013, pp. 54–56). These parallel currencies can be classified according to the identity of the issuers (Blanc 1998b), territorial embedding or localism (Blanc 2002), the objectives of the project (Blanc 2011), or chronological criteria (Blanc and Fare 2013)[^49].

In all cases, behind this great diversity of *ad hoc* monetary systems, each with its own unit of account and constructed around specific actors, communities, and objects, lies a shared intent to question national coinage. These monetary systems emerge in response to situated challenges, part of whose solutions precisely reside in instituting, through money, partitions and/or openings between persons, goods, services, and territories. The organizational constraints and resources of these monetary systems pertain to questions of scale and targeted objectives, which can be explained by four distinct rationales articulated in various ways: the capture of seigniorage income for the benefit of the issuing entity; the protection of the social space against income leakage and external interferences; the stimulation of local economic activity; and the transformation of the nature of exchanges. Interrelated, these rationales form the basis of a multitude of currencies that, to varying degrees, contest and seek to improve the existing monetary system. And as with complementary currencies (CM), the monetary media employed often inadequately fulfill the three canonical monetary functions, either by design choice (as with demurrage currencies à la Gesell, for example) or due to the characteristics of their design and organizational constraints (voluntarily restricted circulation sphere, management of *double spending* in the presence of counterfeits, etc.; Blanc 2009b; Ali et al. 2013, p. 3). These parallel currencies, of which CMs appear as a new iteration, underscore that the structuring of the monetary space results from a diversity of instruments that do not fully embody all the canonical functions of money.

When one focuses on the institutional conditions that enable the articulation of this diversity, the stakes and conflicts that invariably permeate any monetary space, its payment community, and its monetary groups become starkly apparent. These currencies are the practical expression of a tension in value, trust, and sovereignty. The heterogeneity they entail and assert stands in contrast to the characteristics of monetary uniqueness and exclusivity that are today axiomatized, both in theory and in practice. The constraint imposed by the synchronicity of functions in the qualification of money is relaxed, while maintaining both internal and external coherence. Through the lens of the foregoing, we consider that the phenomenon of complementary currencies (CCs) fits perfectly within the broader movement of parallel currencies, which has been particularly dynamic in recent decades (Blanc, 1998; Lietaer, 2009). Let us now transpose the above to the case of Bitcoin and Ethereum.

### Parallel Cryptocurrencies: Monetary Uses of the BTC and ETH Units of Account

The preceding discussion has led us to specify the characteristics we consider essential: the uses of units of account and payment in complementary currencies. If readers have already perceived how this theoretical framework contextualizes the specifically monetary dimension of Bitcoin and Ethereum, what remains is to undertake a more systematic transposition. Everything that constitutes money and participates in monetization is present in complementary currencies. To grasp this, one must start from the uses. The administration of evidence relies on our field investigation: the uses (observed and/or practiced), in unit of account and payment, within communities bearing the same name, are beyond doubt—particularly regarding a type of transaction that previous critiques have feigned to ignore. It immediately appears that Bitcoin and Ethereum correspond to money conceived as a payment system, since they exhibit all the *typical characteristics*: issuance and regulation of the circulation of a unit of account according to the establishment of minting rules (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, pp. 3 and 6). We will first revisit the generic and functional dimension of the unit of account, where money simultaneously acts as an abstract operator (“incorporated state”) concretely embodied (cf. means of payment as “objectified state”), institutionalizing a space of commensurability and exchangeability within the payment community (“institutionalized state”).

#### The Imperative Structuring of the BTC and ETH Units of Account

As we have seen, money is an abstraction and simultaneously presents itself as a *signified*, which requires *signifying* objects and tangible symbols to be invested (“*objectified state*”). Despite their singular appearances, complementary monies (CM) fall within our monetary universals. Historically, the “*choice of forms of unit of account*” leads to their existence “*in the form of a name*,” following two configurations “*depending on whether* [this name] *coincides with that of the means of payment or not*” (Servet, Courbis, and Froment 1991, p. 333): if there is a dissociation, the social and abstract ontology of money as a signified becomes evident, which is captured by the concept of “*imaginary monies*” or “*phantom money*”[^50]. As for the “*choice of names*,” the “*limited variety of terms employed*” responds to the “*limited capacity for innovation of states*”: this “*quasi-stability* [...] *despite political changes or revolutions*” underscores, if further proof were needed, the incapacity of “*power* [to] *abstract from civil society*,” the importance of the weight of habits and routines vis-à-vis a signified embodying social cohesion, and thus the risks involved in challenging the name ([Servet, Courbis, and Froment 1991, pp. 333–334)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=RkztUX). This is why the terms employed often refer to names of old currencies—the dollar, the crown, the franc, the florin, etc.—or to units of weight—the pound, the shilling, or the peseta—as if they participated in a “*common memory*” “*from which* *peoples draw*” their monetary representations. On the side of the signifier, minting materially reinforces the objectification of this abstract measure, by affixing symbols representing the issuing authority or any other symbol of belonging to society and its values [(A. Orléan 2002; Aglietta and Orléan 1998)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=pGE8bL). Thus, regarding national currencies, the pursuit of stability dominates, explaining why “*a differentiation by the name of the unit of account is rare*” ([Servet, Courbis, and Froment 1991, p. 334)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=RkztUX). The same does not hold true for complementary monies, which nevertheless remain ruptures within continuity.

[^50]: See the discussion on “imaginary monies” and “phantom money” in the referenced literature.

Situated within a "community localism" rather than an "state" or "sub-state" framework, Bitcoin and Ethereum exemplify how, despite the digital and intangible nature of their crypto-assets (UCN), the choice of their names and representations reflects a deliberate effort to create and reinforce a collective identity and sense of belonging, drawing upon historical, cultural, or political references specific to their communities. The name *bitcoin* is formed from the English words “bit” (the basic unit of binary information) and “coin” (a piece of currency), following the precedent of Szabo’s Bitgold and Dai’s B-money projects, which Nakamoto cited as references (cf. Chap. I, sect. I). For Ethereum’s *ether*, Buterin sought inspiration from science fiction. The name *ether* was chosen with reference to the medieval scientific conception of ether as a hypothetical fluid thought to fill the universe and enable the propagation of light and electromagnetic waves (Russo 2020, p. 55): through ether, the crypto-asset situates itself within the history of science, suggesting its essential and universal role in the operation of the “world computer” envisioned by Ethereum’s designers.  

These crypto-assets must also be endowed with distinct and easily recognizable symbols and be integrated into an accounting system. This entails the establishment of (i) a logo, initially fixed but capable of evolving through community proposals (as with Bitcoin[^51], Milano 2020); (ii) a stock ticker symbol (BTC or XBT for Bitcoin and ETH for Ethereum); (iii) a symbol inspired by the imagery of national currencies (“₿” for BTC; “Ξ” for ETH)[^52]; and (iv) an accounting system (see Appendix II.1 for Bitcoin and III.1 for Ether). Decimalization[^53] has been adopted, aligning these crypto-assets’ units of account more closely with those of current fiat currencies. However, they differ markedly in the range of payment scales they aim to cover: Bitcoin allows for 8 decimal places after the decimal point (10^-8 BTC), and Ether for 18 decimal places (10^-18 ETH). This high divisibility compensates for the limited monetary supply, which “*may seem insufficient* for a global population of 7 billion people”: for Bitcoin, since there are “*100 million satoshis in one bitcoin,* [...] *the maximum supply of 21 million BTC* equals *2.1 quadrillion satoshis or* [...] *2,100 trillion satoshis*” (Champagne 2014, p. 2). Moreover, this provides significant granularity in payments. Large sums can circulate without any ceiling other than the supply limit itself, while the floor established by fine decimalization facilitates micropayments that national currencies would render difficult (Nakamoto 2008, epigraph Chap. I). Nevertheless, this floor is established protocol-wise but, above all, economically, *via* transaction fees (cf. Chap. I, sect. I.2.1).  

Given the continuous increase in the unit price of the BTC and ETH crypto-assets and the prevalence of fractional transactions (indicating predominant use in fiat currency accounts; cf. change implemented since the conceptual phase, Chap. I, sect. 2.1), specific fractional denominations have been established (see Appendices II.1 and III.1 for Bitcoin and Ethereum, respectively). These denominations are as crucial as those for national currencies: beyond the granularity of payments afforded by their high divisibility, these fractions facilitate mental accounting. The objective is to establish “*a Schelling point on what to use for small denominations so that people can easily talk about variable amounts of Ether, whether the price* [...] *is $0.01, $10, or $100,000*” [(Buterin 2016)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=jjPFVV). The choice of a name is delicate: “*with Bitcoin, the community struggles to agree on a smaller denomination* [...] *‘Millibitcoin’ is difficult to pronounce in a way that* ‘finney’ *is not (also, do you really want to tell a bank teller you want to buy* ‘five hundred mETH’*?)” (Ibid.). Thus, for Bitcoin even more than for Ether, the chosen names—the Satoshi, the Finney, the Szabo, the Lovelace, etc.—refer to key figures and symbols within their community (notably cypherpunk and crypto-anarchist figures; cf. Chap. I).  

BTC and ETH crypto-assets possess all the formal characteristics of units of account and are even more divisible. But do they thereby fulfill the *role* of money as a unit of account and medium of payment?

#### Account and Payment Usage

Let us respond to the criticisms according to which cryptocurrencies are not units of account in themselves, on the grounds that their usage would not be proper: nominal prices would always be originally established in fiat currencies, for goods and services accessible in payment that are too few in number, especially those that are useless or illegal.

One might respond that the existence of a usage exclusively as an accounting unit, as described by critics, is not prohibitive: the phenomenon of imaginary currencies has illustrated how a monetary system can contain different units of account (UC), some of which exist materially only through exchange rates, recorded in formal or informal conversion tables. From an ethnographic perspective, it matters little whether payment involves a conversion of the BTC or ETH units of account (UCN) received (deferred or in real time), since traces remain of an accounting use of the UCN *alongside* fiat currencies. These traces, both *on-chain* and *off-chain*, imposed by transfers of UCN between users, are of interest not only to these users but also to numerous organizations and administrations (gateway operators subject to regulations concerning *Know Your Customer* and *Anti-Money Laundering*, or tax authorities expecting such accounting to be declared).  

In this sense, our historicized review of the infrastructural development of Bitcoin and Ethereum, conducted in Chapter I, helps demonstrate how Bitcoin and Ether have each, in their own way, played roles as units of account and means of payment. These infrastructures and their UCN have found users and uses, whether illegal or speculative. Although it is difficult to assess the total number of vendors, their locations, or the types of goods and services involved, it can nevertheless be affirmed that there has been a significant and sustained increase in the amount of requested recording space, the number of transactions (see Appendices II.4, II.5 for Bitcoin and III.4, III.5 for Ethereum), and the number of users on one of the largest exchanges, Coinbase (Appendix I.3).  

Regarding the payment range of Bitcoin and Ether UCN, while the uses targeted by their decimalizations (large amounts and micropayments) are indeed found, the increase in transaction fees tends to favor large-amount operations (whereas the development of layer 2 solutions is expected to absorb micropayment demand): this is observable in the average and median size (as well as the gap between the two) of daily transfers in UCN and USD (Appendices II.7 and II.8 for Bitcoin and III.7 and III.8 for Ethereum). Furthermore, some companies accept them as payment, such as Wikipedia, Microsoft, AT&T, Subway, Dell, Bloomberg, Google, PayPal, etc.[^54].  

The infrastructural development has even led to a situation where users no longer need to wait for merchants to accept these currencies, since payment services and gateways now allow spending cryptocurrencies *via* payment cards. Our field observations confirm these uses: although “coiners”* like to hoard their cryptocurrencies, they also use them on many occasions, whether ceremonially and festively, or out of necessity within a “subsistence” crypto-finance framework (Allard 2018)[^55]. The undeniable increase in their use explains why their communities face structural scaling problems (cf. Chap. I), which led Bitcoin to a community dispute revealing governance issues over the infrastructure with highly political stakes (the “*Scaling Debate*,” section II.3 below).  

Thus, when analysts criticize cryptocurrencies as inefficient due to “excessive” transaction fees, they are effectively saying nothing other than *Bitcoin and Ethereum would be unusable... because they are too used*! This proposition is paradoxical for economists, who should know that transaction fees implicitly indicate users’ willingness to pay and the utility they derive from the system—thereby contradicting the claimed uselessness of these infrastructures posited by these economists.  

Finally, some states and administrations, such as Japan (McCombie 2018) or Switzerland (Métille 2014), have legally recognized the status of these UCN as means of payment: an irony for chartalists, since some local governments accept them as payment for local taxes[^56]!

To assert that cryptocurrencies (CM) are not currencies because they would be the general equivalent of too few things compared to national currencies in circulation makes no sense to us. Grounded in a *community localism*, the scope of their circulation is more limited, and cryptocurrencies are integrated into fewer daily activities relative to those of state localism. However, neither the status of currency nor the quality of being a “good” or “bad” currency depends on the size of their sphere of use, nor on the quantity and/or quality of persons or goods and services accessible through them. Analyzing monetization requires, for each payment system, focusing on its own sphere of circulation as a singular space for expressing the value of things in a unit of account (UCN) chosen by the members of a payment community. It is up to monetary professionals to consider that cigarettes or cans of fish are not money, let alone “good” money! This viewpoint highlights the externality from which it is asserted: from the perspective of prisoners who use them, these currencies can only be relatively “good” compared to other forms that are difficult for them to access due to heavy regulation. Consequently, these currencies aim less to substitute legal tender than to complement it. In this respect, it suffices that a good or service be expressible and payable solely in their UCN for BTC and ETH to play a role as units of account and means of payment without this role being considered derivative or secondary. Previous analysts seem to have ignored the primary use of these UCNs, described in our first chapter: to be the endogenous currencies of their protocol, akin to railway company tokens or currencies issued by local authorities, whose legal tender power is limited to the space of goods and services offered by their issuers (Blanc 1998a; Blanc 1998b). Yes, in a purely nominalist logic, currency is issued and thereby represents a claim or credit against the issuer who accepts it in settlement (Goodhart 2005, p. 818). Here, the previous critiques that view miners as issuers are mistaken: the only issuers are the Bitcoin and Ethereum protocols. Interactions *within the chain*, whether monetary or not, recognize only one unit of account: their respective UCNs. Using these protocols practically requires using the UCNs they have issued, which alone can evaluate and extinguish debts contracted toward their miner representatives. BTC and ETH are the sole units of account and means of payment linking together the supply and demand for recording: they enable the expression and settlement of transaction fees related to the processing performed (cf. Chap. I, computational and storage costs paid in satoshi/bit for Bitcoin and in gwei/units of “gas” used for Ethereum). Moreover, these two protocols have seen an increase in the number of transactions processed (Appendices II.5 and III.5), as well as in the amount—total, average, and median, in daily UCN and USD—of fees paid per transaction (Appendices II.9, II.10 for Bitcoin and III.9, III.10 for Ethereum). Beyond the upward trend since the respective launches of the two protocols, nearly 240,346 BTC (valued at approximately 1,654,410,052 USD) have been paid cumulatively to Bitcoin miners; nearly 2,833,184 ETH (valued at approximately 1,914,945,993 USD) to Ethereum miners (Appendices II.11 and III.11). These transaction fees constitute costs for users and income for transaction processing operators. These fees add to the issuance rewards received and amount in cumulative value to nearly 23,711,736,998 USD for Bitcoin and nearly 11,018,696,543 USD for Ethereum. While mining costs are expressed in national currency, mining revenues (issuance rewards and transaction fees) are denominated and paid in UCN. These must be sold to cover the former, all recorded in an accounting system that must accommodate the different units of account present. Furthermore, the increase in average mining difficulty for Bitcoin and Ethereum, as well as in cumulative hash rates*, underscores their capacity to recruit operators to ensure their functioning and security (Appendices II.12 and III.12). The overall electricity consumption estimated for the Bitcoin network (currently ranging from 40.68 TWh to 447.17 TWh, depending on the scenario considered) is also continuously increasing (Appendices II.13), as is that of the Ethereum network, which is much more difficult to estimate[^57].

Bitcoin and Ethereum each possess their own distinct sphere of activity, within which their native crypto units (NCUs) provide exclusive monetary payment and settlement services, dynamically evolving through the renegotiations among actors. These account-based and payment uses, whether intrinsic or “derived,” confer upon crypto monies (CM) the status of money, since, as Knapp emphasizes, “*it is not issuance but acceptance that is decisive*” (Desmedt and Piégay 2007, p. 119). Money represents a “*‘claim’ or a ‘credit’* [...] *constituted by social relations* [which] *exist independently of the production and exchange of commodities*” (Ingham 2004, p. 25). What matters is the acceptance in payment by the coiners, whether numerous or not. This voluntary (and non-coerced) acceptance, although conferring a more “limited” liberating power than national currencies, brings them closer to the experiences of parallel currencies. It remains then to examine the store of value function.

#### On the (Non-Monetary) Use as a Store of Value

Our interest in this third function is ambivalent. On the one hand, it is excluded from our definition of money, as well as from that of many economists. As Wicksell and Hicks have pointed out, it is secondary to money (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 11): at the strict “*definitional level, the store of value function can be set aside, since* [it is] *a non-autonomous function of the two fundamental functions, namely accounting and payment, and not specifically monetary* [:] *any good can constitute a store of wealth, but does not thereby constitute a monetary instrument*” (Blanc 1998a, pp. 4 and 17). On the other hand, the monetary beliefs, representations, and expectations of the payment community remain essential to our conception of money.

Already, the content of this reserve function is not self-evident and remains debated (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 12; Andolfatto 2013): should it be understood as a certainty regarding the preservation of purchasing power over time, or as a certainty concerning the nominal magnitudes of monetary instruments[^58]? It is the former interpretation that the majority of economists and practitioners adopt against commodity money (CM), following the volatility of their prices (Appendices No. II.14 and No. III.13). But should the status of money then be denied to many modern monies during monetary disturbances? In cases of inflation or hyperinflation, does money disappear? Do fiat monies always and everywhere allow for a perfect maintenance of their purchasing power, depending on whether their economy experiences consistently zero inflation rates[^59]? The answers are, of course, negative, and even the dollar here would no longer be money (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 12). The second meaning is also problematic, as it leads to “*identifying a whole series of financial assets that are safe (with respect to their future nominal value), but only some of which can serve as means of payment* \[which entails the risk\] *of losing sight of Money, of confusing money and liquid assets*” (*Ibid.*). This perspective of preservation is inessential to money and relative to different temporal horizons. It matters little that “*a monetary instrument preserves its value over long periods*”; money is expected “*to preserve its value over short periods* [;] *after all,* [it] *is not supposed to be a long-term store of value. Once you have received your salary, you are free to buy gold, bitcoins, or any other good you wish*” (Andolfatto 2013). Within the framework of this reserve function, BTC and ETH nevertheless distinguish themselves from financial securities by their high liquidity and the speed this affords in conversions into national currency (the Fiat → CM → Fiat sequence can be executed at any moment). Moreover, the second meaning opposes the first by centering its analysis on the settlement sphere proper to UCN (unit of account number) in BTC and ETH. Evaluating the preservation of the value of monetary instruments vis-à-vis those of other payment communities places the analysis in the realm of securities. From the standpoint of relative prices, money is currency, an asset within portfolios. And where money is exchanged for money (loan or exchange), money acquires the relativity of particular things, thus losing its essence as a unit of account: “*relativity, [money] is destined to be and not to have*” (Simmel 2009, chap. 1, section III). This second meaning, in which it matters that monetary instruments be “*defined stably in unit of account* [...] *offering certainty as to their nominal values*” (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 12), is also grasped by bitcoiners, with perfect irony. They repeat to those who speak to them of volatility that the “*price of the coin does not really matter*” because, everywhere and always, “1 BTC = 1 BTC” (Hajric 2022)!

Note, however, that this function of store of value, in its primary sense, is invoked by the actors themselves, who consider it one of the desirable properties of their native cryptocurrencies (NCs). Among coiners, this function occupies a particular place in their representations and is linked to narratives surrounding the immutability of their coinage, their limited and decreasing monetary issuance, which would lead to an appreciation of their NCs, thereby encouraging holding and, ultimately, monetization. For many coiners, cryptocurrencies are bets on the future[^60], or even “safe havens” to which one rushes “*in times of economic downturn or uncertainty*” (Andolfatto 2016; example of the Cypriot crisis cited by Banque de France 2013, pp. 3–4). There is no doubt that cryptocurrencies constitute a particular class of investment or speculative assets. Where this speculative activity matters for their qualification as money is that it provides evidence of the account and payment uses mentioned and contributes to significant market demand and liquidity. As noted, one of the criticisms leveled against cryptocurrencies is that they are purely speculative objects; yet, this use necessarily implies commensurability: currencies and assets are exchanged against one another according to the establishment of “trading pairs.” Bilateral exchange rates are thus established, raising the question of dominant NCs chosen as pivots in the definition of relative prices (BTC/USD, ETH/BTC, etc.). The available trading pairs vary from one exchange to another and remain at the discretion of exchange operators (P. Noizat, Interview no. 24). It follows that “*coiners*” more readily refer to “uncertain” quotations than to “certain” ones[^61], indicating a mental accounting performed in NCs (BTC or ETH) in their evaluation of asset portfolios. Our own experience attests to this, and the case of NFTs is a paradigmatic example: *on-chain* objects whose production and consumption necessarily involve the expenditure of NCs, their prices, as well as gains and losses, are predominantly calculated in NCs. As seen in Chapter I, the earliest exchanges only allowed CM/CM trades, with the NC BTC playing the role of pivot NC (Carter 2020). With the introduction of “stablecoins,” the situation evolved, and an increasing number of exchanges and trading pairs are expressed in “USD tokens.” This “*dollarization of Bitcoin*” conceals a “*Tetherization*” of cryptocurrencies, where “*USDT* [issued by the company Tether]” establishes itself as the “*main trading pair*” and “*principal settlement asset*” replacing “Bitcoin in this function” (*ibid.*). Nevertheless, for these Tether tokens (or any *stablecoin*) to exist and circulate within these distributed ledger protocols, they must pay transaction fees expressed in NCs[^62]. Moreover, not all stablecoins follow the same issuance logic. While Tether or USDC (a *stablecoin* issued by the company Circle, whose market share is rapidly increasing) are issued centrally and backed by “fiat currencies” of the traditional financial system, this is not automatically the case within the Ethereum ecosystem. With the development of the “open finance”/“DeFi” sector, the NC ETH has acquired a plurality of roles, one of the main ones being to serve as collateral for the issuance of ad hoc “stablecoin” credit (as is the case with DAI, where nearly 55% of the 2,535,286,576 USD of DAI in circulation are backed by the native cryptocurrency Ether[^63]). Furthermore, coiners view the volatility of NC BTC and ETH less as a problem than as a sought-after property, especially since their price trends have historically been upward. Coiners speculate in the primary sense of the term, anticipating that the NCs held will continue to appreciate; they do not use them for payment on every occasion: they value hoarding, as evidenced by slogans such as “Hodl”[^64], “staking Sat,” or “buy the dip” (aiming to accumulate ever more NCs), or the implementation of ritual practices such as “Proof of Keys Day” for Bitcoiners[^65].

That these actors prefer BTC or ETH over fiat currency as a wealth asset is not, for us, crucial to the qualification of cryptocurrencies (CM) as money. This leads us less to consider them irrational than to explicate the rationality that is theirs. Chapter I recalled: according to many coiners, CM protocols would be immune to human governance due to their technical nature, hence their capacity to offer money that is "*protected against inflation*," "*under the control of* [their] *owner and likely to preserve its value in the long term*" (Ammous 2018, p. 202). For coiners, the question of whether cryptocurrencies (CM) are "good" or "bad" money is central. They are not alone in this. To the affirmative answer they give, detractors of CM respond negatively: they are bad money. According to our conception of money (and monetization), anything used as a unit of account and means of payment qualifies as money, and these qualifications of "good" or "bad" immediately reveal a situated normative judgment. Criticizing CM for their limited capacity to fully carry the canonical functions is to forget that the apparent uniqueness of money is neither spontaneous nor given once and for all, but is constructed and maintained dynamically through the existence of institutional arrangements and the actors who produce them. These always situated uses govern the fact that every monetary instrument presents itself as *Special Purpose Money*: as Blanc (1998a) makes explicit, from the perspective of monetary instruments, few monetary objects are simultaneously "*employed as unit of account, as means of payment, and as a store of wealth*," but "*understood as a system,* [money] *necessarily brings together the principle of account and payment, and the principle of wealth preservation is also appended to it as soon as this system is viable and endures*" (*Ibid.*, p. 17). For Bitcoin and Ethereum, these different uses exist and are articulated with the norms and values of their community members. Some clarifications remain necessary to determine whether they are good or bad money, and these will ultimately open the question of their governance.

# Beyond the Claim of an Absence of Governance!

This final section continues our attempt to characterize cryptocurrencies (CM), specifically Bitcoin and Ethereum, within the domain of money. Serving as the conclusion to Chapter II, it will open onto the central issues of the forthcoming Chapter III. In seeking to identify what distinguishes cryptocurrencies from other known forms of money, we are led to the hypothesis that it is their governance that constitutes their monetary singularity. If, for us, cryptocurrencies *do* indeed function as money, this is neither in the sense of monetary experts nor in that of vocal coiners! Moving beyond the previous reifying frameworks implies not only that cryptocurrencies can (and must) be integrated into the field of money without being reduced to existing monetary categories—ill-suited to them, even if dominant. Since our starting point is not to axiomatize abstract, “pure,” and disembodied functions supposedly carried more or less naturally by certain commodities, there are no other disagreements. We challenge the perspective adopted regarding the existence of a “good” money in itself, whose “essence” would be to serve as a unitary, homogeneous, and exclusive merchant instrument: this refers to an absolutization of presupposed qualities, normatively founded externally and hierarchically above the actors who use it. Similarly, we will question related propositions that attempt to grasp money and cryptocurrencies through the prism of a single *competition* between “good” and “bad,” conceived as a mere substitution phenomenon. All these propositions refer back to the same epistemological foundation we have already criticized and which some coiners share with their critics.

First, we will revisit the polysemy and controversies elicited by the concept of governance within the academic field, and specify the positive meaning we will employ (I.3.1). Next, we will examine how the technicist liberal ambitions of coiners—according to which an absence of human governance characterizes cryptocurrencies—reactivate an old monetary controversy concerning the question of good money and the governance that guarantees it (I.3.2). Then, we will show that the characterization of cryptocurrencies’ governance involves crucial practical stakes—since it enables a categorical clarification within the field of digital assets and money—as well as theoretical ones. It seems to us that only an endogenous empirical analysis should allow for the assessment of the quality of a currency for the members of its payment community (I.3.3). Ultimately, this analysis will highlight the originality of cryptocurrencies’ governance, which we will explore in Chapter III.

## From a Problematic Concept of Governance to the Problematic of Cryptocurrencies’ Governance

The concept of governance, central to this thesis and to the critiques of CM that remain to be presented, appears problematic in its academic usages (plural and contradictory). Its semantic content, epistemological foundations, as well as its applications are subject to debate and depend on the actors and the field in which they employ it. The notion of governance is longstanding, deriving “*from the Latin ‘gubernare’*” meaning to govern, to steer a ship (Baron, 2003, p. 330). In France, the usage long equivalent to “government” (Paye, 2005, p. 13) has seen its meaning shift in favor of the liberal turn of the 1980s–1990s: originating from the Anglo-Saxon world, it draws on economic science but primarily pertains to political practices (Baron 2003; Paye 2005; Coutrot and Rebérioux 2005). Hence, a polysemy and the appearance of a “*catch-all concept*” (Paye, 2005, p. 29), composed of interwoven scientific and ideological usages that make the establishment of a single, rigorous, and stabilized definition difficult, and which continues to generate confusion, ambiguity, and controversy both in the academic field (Rhode 1996; Baron 2003) and in that of CM. At the academic level, while its broad meaning “*refers to the modalities of organization and exercise of power when collective action is at stake*” (*Ibid.*, p. 2), this concept is mobilized within sometimes contradictory epistemological and theoretical frameworks, from which two major families of usage can be distinguished[^66]: the first, original, is normative and prescriptive; the other, which we will adopt, is more positive and empirical (Boyer and Dehove 2001; Baron, 2003; Paye, 2005).

### On the Concept of Governance and Its Polysemy: A Primordial Normative Concept

The first set of uses was promoted by political rather than scientific actors, with the concept of governance serving as a framework for reforming the ends and means of public action in recent decades: it was the World Bank that imposed this concept as a vehicle for a “neo-liberal” ideology (Rodrik 2002, Baron 2003, Paye 2005). The initial particularized use of the concept of governance dates back to a 1989 World Bank Development Report, which served to support "*the concept in order to rationalize, deploy, and re-legitimize [...] its practices vis-à-vis states requesting financial aid for their development*" through a series of subsequent reports (in 1992, 1994, and 2000; Paye 2005, p. 23). This strategic use allowed the Bank to circumvent a mandate prohibiting it from interfering in the political decisions of states at a time when development aid and its prescribers were contested. The principles of the “*Washington Consensus*”[^67], a doctrine that formulates prescriptions taking the “*proverbial form of a ‘laundry list’*” (Rodrik 2002, p. 8), governed international development policies and structural adjustment plans implemented by the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund in the early 1980s, and were subject to criticism (Rodrik 2002, p. 1; Baron 2003). This first, highly political use gave rise to variations such as the “minimal state,” “corporate governance”[^68], or New Public Management, which share an ideological and programmatic foundation. These uses rely on and are supported by the axioms and methods of orthodox economics previously discussed. The diffusion of the notion of governance originates from economic science. In political science, it is the book by J. Campbell, R. Hollingsworth, and L. Lindberg (eds.), *Governance of the American Economy, Cambridge, Cambridge University Press, 1991*, “*straddling economic science and political science*,” that testifies to “*this transfer*” (Paye 2005, p. 22). In the economic field, the period witnessed a “*renewed interest in both governance and institutional economics*” and “*an emphasis on the role of institutions as modes of coordination of activities* [alternative] *to the market*” (Baron 2003, p. 341). This “*idea that the market as a mechanism is not the only mode of coordination*” is not new and lies at the core of R. H. Coase’s *The Nature of the Firm* as early as 1937 (Favereau 2010). In the presence of market imperfections (transaction costs, incomplete contracts, moral hazard, adverse selection), the market coordination mode experiences “failures” that are compensated for by others, such as hierarchical coordination within firms. In the 1970s, Williamson placed these issues at the heart of his “*New Institutional Economics*,” close to the research program of the “Extended Standard Theory” (EST), which consists of “*modeling institutions as rules in a formal game*,” analyzed strategically (Favereau 1997, citing Binmore 1988), itself derived from “Standard” economic theory.

Within this family of usages, the concept of governance serves less to relativize the role of the market than to absolutize it, since it “*refers to coordination mechanisms that are essentially economic in nature, aimed at reducing coordination costs. The analysis of institutions remains confined within the economic framework of efficiency, with the contract serving as the unifying object of coordination modalities*” (Baron 2003, echoed by Favereau 2010). The central analytical categories are similar: the contract for the New Institutional Economics (NIE) and transactions for Williamson. Opportunism and individual self-interest explain behaviors and endogenize the market. The Coasian new institutionalism gave rise to a theory of “*property rights as the foundation of the economic order, where private property is seen as the necessary basis of the market order*” (Weinstein 2013, p. 3). Hardin (1968) and his “tragedy of the commons,” as well as the “*more sophisticated works* [...] *of Alchian and Demsetz (1973)*,” establish themselves as “*economic doxa [postulating] that social organizations not based on private appropriation of resources implement inefficient forms of resource exploitation*” (Coriat and Broca 2015, p. 269). This doxa leads to intellectual property reforms and, by extension, to the software freedom claims presented in Chapter I. Governance translates into less government and prescribes importing private management methods into the core of public sectors (explicit norms and performance measures, result constraints, resource optimization). Governance entails the relegation of the political, power, and conflict; and if the state and its administrations have a role, it is as substitutes for the market in well-defined “failure” situations: it mandates thinking *policies* (*policy*) outside of *politics* (*politics*) (Baron, 2003).

### Positive reversal of the concept: reintegrating power and politics

This family of usages does not exhaust the concept of governance, and a myriad of approaches have made it a heuristic tool at the heart of more positive and scientific endeavors (in political science, sociology, urban planning, spatial economics; see Rhodes 1996, Baron 2003, Paye 2005). Observing increasing permeability between public and private *via* new modes of public action, these approaches interrogate the coordination modalities at work, composed of a multiplicity of actors and levels of intervention, by decentering from the sole market or hierarchical schemas opposing state and market (Baron 2003).

Common denominators and a “*semantic core*” as well as “*heuristic ambitions*” emerge from this (Paye, 2005, p. 15): within the framework of collective actions, the concept of governance serves to analyze (beyond their solely formal institutional aspects) the *governing processes*, their stakeholders, the arrangements and mechanisms implemented, as well as their aims and justifications. These approaches conceive governance processes as singular social constructions that must be documented and analyzed, pertaining to particular local situations as well as the interactions that take place therein. Actor logics are not given, enclosed in axioms, but analyzed through the objectives pursued, as well as the decision-making and cognitive processes employed to achieve them[^69]. Within this family, governance continues to designate the “*art of governing*,” but its meaning and scope are enriched in directions that allow engagement with crucial issues surrounding our object: it is not a vector for the erasure of the political in favor of mere market coordination and enables the revitalization of the political question and the debate among state, market, and civil society. Governance here lies at the center of a conceptual framework aimed at understanding the evolution of coordination processes over time, encompassing a “*new mode of managing public affairs based on the participation of civil society at all levels*” (Baron, 2003). This framework allows for the inscription and analysis of historically original management modes within environments marked by a plurality of actors who possess, to varying degrees, decision-making power (*Ibid.*, p. 330, echoed by Paye, 2005). It permits a clear distinction from *government*, which refers to its formal institutions, representatives, and modes of coordination and coercion (Boyer and Dehove, 2001; Baron, 2003; Paye, 2005). The state, however important it remains, is no longer by its own right “*an actor among others*” (Le Galès, cited by Baron, 2003, p. 334). Its actions are contained within a broader set of governing processes resulting from the multi-level participation of a composite ensemble of heterogeneous actors, from which collectives can “*govern without government*” (title of Rhodes, 1996). The differentiation of the political system and the interweaving of private and public activities have given rise to a “*society without a center*,” “*a polycentric state characterized by multiple centers*,” where “*central government is no longer supreme*” (Rhodes, 1996, p. 657). While this concept asserts the idea that there “*no longer exists a single sovereign authority*” (*Ibid.*), the state and civil society are not thereby conceived as mere auxiliaries of the market. This concept enables one to transcend the hierarchy, always presupposed within the framework of “good” governance theories, between state, market, and civil society: empirical evidence precludes the articulation among these three poles, composed of variable and dynamic arrangements, from being *“thought a priori”* (Baron, 2003, p. 338).

Our interest in the work of E. Ostrom and the Bloomington school is precisely explained by the fact that they developed, within the *Institutional Analysis and Development framework* (IAD, expanded into the SES framework—*Social Ecological System*; Chanteau and Labrousse 2013, p. 6), a conceptual apparatus whose advantage lies in explicitly addressing these governance issues. This analytical framework, developed around common resources, “Common Pool Resources” (CPR)—whether physical or immaterial—is entirely oriented towards analyzing the diversity of governance modes exercised by “self-managed” and “polycentric” communities (Ostrom 1990; Hess and Ostrom 2003; Hess and Ostrom 2007; Hess 2008; Chanteau and Labrousse 2013). Designed to decipher—describe and analyze—both institutional diversity and its modalities of evolution, this framework appears particularly well-suited. Composed of a collection of disparate empirical analyses covering diverse institutional arrangements, it does not presuppose any hierarchy among (reified) types of coordination or property regimes. It constitutes a non-normative and agnostic framework, insofar as governance is conceived therein without presupposing the forms and functions it ought to assume. Governance refers to a contextually situated configuration of a set of rules and interactions—and associated “bundles of rights”—enabling one to “escape the simple and Manichean dilemma” between public and private property (Coriat and Broca 2015, p. 270).  

The malleability of this toolbox (structural components of a rule system, property regime, and bundle of rights) corresponds to its systematic nature: regardless of the degree of decentralization of the governance system of the community under study, it is possible to analyze the framework of interactions and to define who holds which rights and under what terms. This is a rigorous, systematic framework open to empirical and qualitative data (Chanteau and Labrousse 2013; Allaire 2013), which can be asserted to be fully embedded within historical institutionalism (Chanteau and Labrousse 2013). Constructed in opposition to previous normative approaches, which it enables one to critique, it is situated within the struggle against the *irresistible* rise of intellectual property in the 1990s, a development criticized by jurists involved in the free software counter-offensive such as Lessig (cf. Chap. I; Coriat and Broca 2015, p. 272).

Governance thus allows for the reintroduction of political issues, power, and conflict into economic analysis, without being limited to public coercion. It differs from the notion of *“government, which presupposes a central, dominant actor”* by distributing authority, power, responsibilities, and accountability among a myriad of actors with different statuses (*Ibid.*, p. 334). The development of collective consensus requires taking into account the capacity of actors to create, modify, and interpret coordination rules within a given environment. With the replacement of an overarching power by more horizontal forms of authority, questions of legitimacy become central—whether those surrounding collective decisions and their outcomes, or those attributed to the mechanisms and processes that led to them (forms of participatory democracy, for example). Starting from the significant transformations of our societies, characterized by a disengagement of the state at the national level and a process of globalization at the international level, this concept emphasizes the issues surrounding interactions between local and global dimensions, and the interplay of actors that constitute their framework. Space is endogenized as an explanatory factor of certain economic and social processes (Baron 2003), such as the erosion of the full sovereignty of states. Collective compromises are opposed to conflicts of interest, the foundations of which are no longer solely legal, since the environment in which the various stakeholders operate in the consensus-building process can be local and subnational or, conversely, supranational, or straddling different jurisdictional spaces, implying that these stakeholders retain, to varying degrees, the capacity to define and interpret the framework of their coordination (*Ibid.*).

Reappropriated positively, the concept of governance analytically resurrects authority, power, conflict—in a word: the political. Applied to complementary currencies (CM), it empirically invites us to go beyond the assumptions shared by both proponents and critics, namely the absence of governance other than that provided by protocol codes. These assumptions lie at the heart of the controversy (belonging to the family of normative usages) opposing them on the qualification of CMs as “good” or “bad” money.

## When Complementary Currencies Reactivate an Old Monetary Debate: Symbolic and Material Conflict over the “Good” Governance of Complementary Currencies

The context surrounding the revival of the concept of governance is significant. It helps to understand how, in addition to the explanations already advanced underpinning the critiques formulated by monetary experts regarding cryptocurrencies (CM), another explanation emerges: Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies find meaning and justification within heterodox epistemological and symbolic frameworks that are situated. This situation contributes to the complicated relationship between monetary experts and “coiners\*,” characterized by ideological conflicts and material risks. Nakamoto’s critiques of traditional money and its centralization, as we have seen, refer to various lineages. While the cypherpunk and crypto-anarchist thoughts discussed in the first chapter may be unfamiliar to us, the references to the metallist system, drawing on Austrian theory, the proponents of *free banking*, and the “*gold bugs*” who advocate a return to the gold standard are not. Whether coiners like it or not, these other lineages of cryptocurrencies mean that many of them partly share the monetary visions of their critics. With their technicist liberalism, some coiners embrace the market and competitive framework that serves as common ground for academic critiques: market forces and competition “naturally” lead to the selection of *the* most *efficient* currency as a medium of exchange (the one that minimizes transaction costs), hence the properties of homogeneity, uniqueness, and exclusivity associated with it are conceived abstractly and universally as “good” in themselves. Whether academics like it or not, they share with coiners similar views by questioning cryptocurrencies through the lens of *competition* between “good” and “bad” monies. This explains why they take at face value some coiners and their “*hyper-cryptomonetization*” (or “hyper-bitcoinization,” as wished for by some bitcoiners, cf. Ammous’s epigraph) without further critical distance. In this sense, coiners and critics of cryptocurrencies share the question of whether it is possible and even desirable for cryptocurrencies to replace “fiat monies” and the traditional monetary system, postulating that cryptocurrencies have no socio-political governance. It is this absence of governance, concealing the qualities each expects from a “good” currency, that fundamentally constitutes the object of their disagreement: for the critics, this mass adoption of cryptocurrencies is neither possible nor desirable, in opposition to the latter.

This question of a “good” currency and, correlatively, that of the governance that makes it possible, refers to a structuring controversy within the monetary field. The revival of the concept of governance has fueled recent debates on this matter.

### From *the good* currency to *the good* governance: a normative approach shared by coiners and their detractors

The emergence of central banks, as a test of explicitation, has led to the reactivation of an old and foundational debate in monetary history, that of the “rule versus discretion”[^70], a history that “*can perhaps be reconstructed from the gradual emergence of the concept of ‘neutral money,’ whose practical realization has always been the exclusive objective of orthodox monetary policies*” (Tutin 2009, p. 10). Neutrality, “*intrinsically beneficial for the economy*” (Davies [2002], p. 171, cited by Théret 2008, p. 12), prompts reflection on the advisability of binding, inviolable rules designed to protect money from political interferences that seek to divert it. The innovation of credit money (paper-based, non-convertible) detached from its ties to precious metals had, in its time, provided a vigorous opportunity to open the debate on the “proper management” of money by banks and central banks (de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003; Kindelberger 2004; Tutin 2009) and to lay the foundations of contemporary *Central Banking* activities (Feiertag and Margairaz 2012).

#### From the “rule versus discretion” to “discretion-constraint”

Advocates of the neutrality of money—or its neutralization—align themselves with the instrumental and substantialist approaches previously discussed. Money, like any commodity good, attains its “equilibrium” quantity through a market price. For a currency to be considered sound (“Sound money”), only the stability of the unit of account must be ensured, since the store of value function is, in their view, the most important. To them, fiat currencies are not genuine money. The link to metal should not have been questioned, as it protected stability by imposing exogenous constraints on monetary policy, particularly on money creation. From their perspective, the “proper management” of money by banks and central banks thus entails the establishment of strict rules and constraints governing monetary policy and the authorities responsible for it (de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003; Kindelberger 2004; Tutin 2009).  

For the opposing camp (to which we belong), there is no inherently “good” money: its qualities are assessed according to a different, even opposite logic—namely, that of the reproduction of the monetary system itself, in accordance with the foundational principles recognized by citizens. From this standpoint, it is *also* a matter of ensuring the viability of exchanges and the community’s payment system, by safeguarding it against the risk of illiquidity (the classical version of the lender of last resort), or even, today, insolvency (the contemporary “too big to fail” phenomenon; de Boyer des Roches and Rosales, 2003). Monetary policy is a matter of relative trade-offs, and good governance here refers to the capacity of monetary authorities to undertake legitimate discretionary actions aimed at preventing systemic collapse: the function of the lender of last resort, popularized by Bagehot in 1873, crowns the debates of the nineteenth century, which never before so thoroughly reflected on the roles and functions of nascent central banks (Kindelberger 2004; de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003).

The aforementioned fault lines are recurrent throughout the history of monetary thought: while already present in pre-classical debates, they reappear in the controversies between the Currency School and the Banking School, as well as between monetarists and Keynesians (Kindelberger, 2004; Tutin, 2009). The period from 1970 to the present is often characterized as the triumph of the proponents of rules and the “*quantitative vision*” (Galbraith, 2008). The era of political governance of money is over; it has been replaced by its “good governance.” Contemporary monetary theory and its practitioners agree on the long-term inefficacy of discretionary policies and their impact on price stability[^71]. These *aprioristic*, *ahistorical*, and *asocial* approaches, by presupposing the qualities of “good” or “bad” money, lead to the establishment of a set of normative prescriptions, which evolve alongside theoretical refinements. Indeed, while particular attention is paid to the “right” quantity of money, this quantity—considered exogenous—is still created solely by the central bank. Since monetary and financial closure requires a final issuer who is both regulator and supervisor, this intensifies the question of the rules constraining it, which must be precise, immutable, and publicized. It is under the aegis of the victory of this conception that financial liberalization policies[^72] have emerged, both theoretically and practically, promoted by national and international organizations and the *New Monetary Consensus*, a successor to monetarism[^73], prescribing independence, transparency, and credibility for monetary policies and the institutions that implement them. Practically, central banks, their members, objectives, and instruments have been legally “autonomized” from the Treasury Department and related political pressures (B. Friedman 2008; King 1999; Feiertag and Margairaz (eds.) 2012). Following *Kydland and Prescott* [1977] and *Barro and Gordon* [1983a,b], monetary literature “*is primarily situated within the new classical economics*,” whose central hypotheses of rational expectations and natural rates lead to a focus mainly on “*issues of time inconsistency and the credibility of announcements by economic policy makers, particularly monetary policy*” (Salle, 2013, p. 703).

Within academic and political spheres, this monetary orthodoxy, often labeled neoliberal (Rodrik, 2002), is accompanied by conceptual refinements demonstrating that the rule alone is insufficient. Indeed, the “discretionary” monetary regime, under which the central bank may revise its monetary policy at will, is not *a priori* satisfactory. However, a regime associated with a commitment mechanism to a rule is no more satisfactory: the rule would not suffice to overcome the risk of time inconsistency, insofar as the central bank retains the possibility of deviating from its objective or strategy over time, at the risk of its credibility and the trust agents place in it. To render credible the statements of policymakers and the monetary policies they conduct, the rules implemented must produce consistent results over time and be sanctionable, hence evaluable by agents. Inflation targeting thus appears in this context “*as a solution to the inflationary bias*,” with inflation targeting considered “*as a rule*,” whereby the central bank commits “*to conduct monetary policy according to the optimal rule, thanks to the transparency imposed by this regime*.” To this end, it is essential that the private information it holds about its real and monetary objectives be transmitted to the public, transparency allowing “*to reveal this information to agents and* [...] *to evaluate the performance of monetary policy. Based on these performances, agents grant credibility to the central bank, which is thereby held accountable with respect to its objectives (accountability)*” (Salle, 2013, p. 705). This “inflation targeting” actually corresponds to a third regime, termed “*constrained discretion*,” which amounts “*to constrain the central bank through transparency to act as if it could commit to the optimal rule*” (Bernanke et al. [1999], cited by *Ibid.*).

#### Central Banks: A Theoretical and Practical Radicalization of the Rule?

Few proponents of political and civic discretion in monetary policy (who might justify abrupt changes in rules) claim allegiance to this regime of “constrained discretion,” which currently dominates central banks. Coiners also reject this regime: by advocating a radicalized rule, they demonstrate that this “*constant battle*” opposing “*under one name or another, since the 17th century and probably earlier*” these two monetary factions “*is far from over*” (Kindelberger, p. 62). Central banks reactivate this conflict in a singular manner, presenting themselves as a radical transcendence of the most rigorous monetary ambitions, such as those of the monetarist M. Friedman, the return to the gold standard[^74], or the *Free Banking* advocated by Hayek and his followers. Friedman had accepted, as a necessary evil, that a strictly regulated central bank should have the exclusive responsibility for monetary issuance and regulation. Yet his “good” money remains absent. Optimistically, he envisions it as “*soon to be developed, a reliable electronic currency—a method by which, on the Internet, you can transfer funds from A to B without A knowing B or B knowing A. The way I can take a twenty-dollar bill and hand it to you without any trace of where it came from. And you can receive it without knowing who I am*.” An “*ecash*” which, although facilitating illegal activities, will reduce governments’ capacity to levy taxes, which he views very positively (Friedman 1999). This desired erasure of central banks and governments, represented by the CM, also echoes the Austrian liberal current and the theory of *Free Banking*, to which vocal promoters of Bitcoin explicitly refer and which Nakamoto himself invokes.

This intellectual convergence, frequently emphasized (De Filippi 2013; Dréan 2013; De Filippi and Loveluck 2016; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015; Rolland and Slim 2017; Dodd 2017), goes beyond mere indirect reference. It was practically constructed. In 1994, N. Szabo, “*after becoming involved in the cypherpunk movement,* \[went on to\] *create his own private mailing list called libtech-l*” (Lars 2020). The list served as a forum for exchange between cypherpunk figures and economists renowned for their liberal monetary positions, whose common denominator was an interest in the idea of “*denationalizing*” money. According to Szabo, this “*was certainly a marginal idea at the time when* \[he\] *was working on it and there were only a handful of people in the world with whom* \[he\] *could discuss it and who even had an inkling* \[...\]*. We were on a mailing list, the cypherpunk mailing list, and then Wei Dai, Hal Finney, Larry White, George Selgin, and a few others and I were on a mailing list I created called libtech, and it was there that I had the idea for Bitgold and Wei Dai had the idea for B-money, and we had extensive discussions about it.*” (McCormack and Szabo 2019). Thus, the distrust of central authorities, the pursuit of a currency emancipated from state legal frameworks, and more generally, the belief in the primacy of interindividual relations naturally mediated by the market enable the convergence of cypherpunk/crypto-anarchist monetary views with those of the Austrian liberal school, of which Selgin and White are heirs. Starting from a point close to orthodoxy, through its conception of commodity money, this school becomes heterodox by its radical stance on the issue of governance: following Hayek, the state holds an “illegitimate” right regarding coinage due to its monopoly over currency issuance (and the definition of rates and rules of use), which should be abolished in favor of competition among private issuers. The unimpeded market appears as the mechanism by which bad actors are sanctioned and virtuous actors rewarded. The self-regulation of the money supply is thus ensured without intervention by a government or central bank (Hayek, 1976).

The privileged relationship fostered by Szabo’s mailing list between actors as diverse as cryptographers and academic economists has endured and continues to be reproduced, as many coiners and some economists maintain it. The predominance of references to the metallist system, the gold standard, and the tribulations of credit money in these discussions stems from the fact that Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies develop a “*digital metallism*” (Maurer et al., 2014; Mallard et al., 2018) that mimics metallic monies to varying degrees: the term “mining” and its counterpart reward mechanism, which enables the issuance of unforgeable cryptographic numbers (UCN), reinforce the analogy. The “coiners\*,” much like their “*old cousins the gold bugs*” (J.P. Koning 2019b), present themselves as defenders against the proponents of “fiat” currencies—numerous, powerful, and coalitionized[^75]—advocating for a “digital gold,” a “sound money” superior to all other forms due to the absence of governance… which, for academics, is conversely a decisive drawback.

### A Dispute Concerning the “Proper” Governance of Cryptocurrencies

Coiners and their critics confront each other from epistemologically related positions. At the heart of their disputes over cryptocurrencies lie normative questions regarding the essential characteristics that monetary governance of quality must possess. The coiners, “*more royalist than the king*,” reproach the latter for what they perceive as compromises and failures; monetary authorities have demonstrated an excessive dependence on political interests from which they were supposed to have become autonomous. While the victory of the rule was total in minds[^76], it was not so materially. The facts of recent decades disavow the doctrine of “constrained discretion”: on the one hand, its pursuit has resulted in a resurgence of economic instability and monetary and financial crises; on the other hand, it has led to increased recourse to “unconventional” monetary policies, which this theoretical corpus condemns. It is against the backdrop of this longstanding conflict and the context following the 2007–2008 crisis—which “*had the merit of reviving discussions around the construction of a new monetary and financial order, much like the Bretton Woods conference in its time*” (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 16)—that the opposition between proponents and detractors of cryptocurrencies must be understood.

It is through a virulent form of “*fiatsplaning*”[^77] that many coiners criticize the traditional monetary system, challenging monetary experts on the relevance of their analyses of Bitcoin, as well as their monetary and financial expertise. Coiners assert the superiority of cryptocurrencies (CM), which they claim are uniquely capable of guaranteeing transparency, credibility, and thus temporal coherence, since they replace human governance with “immutable” and neutral computer code. Conversely, this absence of governance—conceived as absolutely desirable by certain coiners—is, for academics, the decisive mark that cryptocurrencies are merely poor substitutes for money.

#### For coiners: an absence of governance as the hallmark of an absolutely “good” currency

Saifedean Ammous, an economist who identifies with the Austrian school[^78] and a recognized maximalist Bitcoiner*, perfectly embodies the figure of the radical liberal-technologist unambiguously mobilized to represent the entirety of coiners. In his book *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018), he explains how Bitcoin would be a better currency than national currencies and how it could serve to re-found the international monetary system (Ammous, 2018, p. 15). According to him, Bitcoin would no less than automate “*the functions of a modern central bank*” by making them “*predictable and practically immutable*” due to their decentralization, thereby establishing Bitcoin as the “*first operational and reliable example of digital cash and digital hard money*” (“*digital cash and digital hard money*,” *Ibid.*, p. 15). Like perfectly convertible currencies, Bitcoin is conceived as a critique of the contemporary fractional and non-convertible monetary system. Cryptocurrencies would allow users to have at their disposal a currency fully controlled by themselves and capable of maintaining its value over the long term (here, the idea of monetary neutrality is understood in its sense of preserving purchasing power over time, rather than as certainty regarding the nominal magnitudes of monetary instruments). According to the author, Bitcoin would remedy problems as old as human society itself, and do so more effectively than the gold standard of the past.

Native to the Internet, transcending national borders and eluding governmental controls, cryptocurrencies (CM) are perceived as effective and superior solutions to the problems of monetary manipulation and inflation, while enabling secure value transfers without the need to trust third parties. Historically, gold has been regarded as *“the great winner of this race”* toward a “sound” currency, which explains the recurrent references made to it by coiners. This reference serves Bitcoin and its native cryptocurrencies (UCN) to highlight, by contrast, their unprecedented advantages (Ammous, 2018, p. 34). Cryptocurrencies *“combine the best elements of physical monetary media, without any of the physical drawbacks related to their movement and transport”* [allowing them to] *“claim to be the best savings technology ever invented”*: they offer *“effectively the liquidity of gold over time and the liquidity of fiat money across space, within an apolitical, immutable, and open-source framework”*[^79] (Ammous, 2018, pp. 198–199). And unlike the *“historical version of sound money, gold,”* they would *“offer the modern individual the possibility to escape totalitarian, managerial, Keynesian, and socialist states”* (Ammous, p. 202).

For coiners who share such views, our problems stem solely from “fiat currencies” and their unilateral management by governments. These currencies and their governance are portrayed as the root of all evils: fiat currencies, besides being fragile (a widely disseminated “false information” claims that they have *“an average lifespan of 27 years”*[^80]), are said to explain why contemporary art and music have become *“tasteless rubbish”* due to the replacement of the aesthetic patrons of yesteryear by short-sighted governments[^81]; they are also blamed for wars, police and state violence[^82], and even the *“collapse of family values”*—attributed to the *“hedonism”* and *“pedophilia”* of Keynes, conceived as the sole demiurge[^83] (Ammous, 2018, pp. 103–109; for a critical review of the work, see Coppola, 2018, who argues that the quality of the explanations on Bitcoin is matched only by the “delirious” nature of its analyses). This type of position calls for a phenomenon of “hyper-cryptomonetization”—a massive adoption of cryptocurrencies—which they conceive as salutary. This, of course, is opposed by critics of cryptocurrencies.

The filiations and the preceding *fiatsplaining*, which often yield a fallacious and caricatural portrayal of economists and the monetary and financial system, contribute to prominent figures in economics (Nobel laureates foremost among them) adopting a defensive stance in mainstream media, even before an academic literature on the subject has emerged. The dimension claimed as positive in these positions, based on the mobilization of work conducted by others (rarely cited), serves primarily to support normative reasoning[^84]. These authorities throw their expertise and reputation into the arena to counter what, for them, is nothing more than a "*techno-mysticism inside a libertarian ideological cocoon*" (Krugman 2018b), a new form of "*right-wing extremism*," "*used by populist plutocrats, such as Trump, to destroy democracy and create an authoritarian state*" (Roubini[^85]). For these economists and practitioners (bankers or financiers), a cryptocurrency like Bitcoin "*raises two distinct questions. Is it sustainable? And assuming it is, does it contribute to the common good?*". They answer in unison "*probably not (the jury is still out) and certainly not*": regarding "*the question of sustainability, bitcoin is a pure bubble, an asset without intrinsic value—its price will fall to zero if confidence disappears*." Moreover, even if "*it could become the new gold,*" \[Tirole would\] "*not bet* \[his\] *savings on it, and* \[he\] *would not want regulated banks to speculate on its value.*" (Tirole 2017). Furthermore, "*the social value of bitcoin* [...] *escapes* *these critiques*" (*Ibid.*). These luminaries therefore conclude that the cryptocurrency phenomenon cannot and must not endure, as it is at best economically imperfect and technically limited; at worst, it is a pure scam, a Ponzi scheme doomed to collapse. They align with certain computer science researchers who see in it only "*totally dysfunctional payment systems,* \[or even\] *a technological fraud*" (J. Stolfi, cited by Colomé 2022, cf. Chap. I, section I.1.1).

At the core of their argumentation, regardless of their affiliation, all critics concur on a hypothesis borrowed uncritically from coiners: cryptocurrencies (CM) are conceived as lacking governance (or that governance is reduced solely to canonical protocol rules). Although these financial professionals, as an epistemic community (Feiertag and Margairaz 2012, chap. 10), share with coiners the idea that the objectives and means of monetary policy must be strictly framed, their practical conclusions diverge. This is particularly true concerning the much-vaunted immutability of protocol codes and the minting of cryptocurrencies. Mervyn King perfectly illustrates this opposition by asserting that "*it is both undesirable and impossible for a government to bind its successors to an immutable monetary regime*," since "*collective decisions today cannot bind future collective decisions... monetary arrangements can always be changed* [depending on whether a] *very bad government simply reclaims discretionary power (Ibid., pp. 3–4)*" (King (2004), cited by Selgin 2014b, p. 24). From this original sin of governance absence, which would make them dangerous and whimsical "apolitical" currencies (the title of Varoufakis’s 2013 blog post), they deduce a whole series of monetary defects, leading to conclusions opposite to those of the coiners. These critiques, which primarily target "*certain bitcoin aficionados* [who] *have seen in it* an *alternative to overcome international monetary disorder and currency wars*" (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 16), are less directed at the cryptocurrencies themselves than at the ambitions that some of the most radical coiners imperiously ascribe to them. On this point, their critics’ conclusion is straightforward: cryptocurrencies are poor currencies due to their minting being utterly disconnected from the real economy, which renders them an ontologically inefficient and deflationary monetary system (Varoufakis 2013; Krugman 2018a), "*incapable of stabilizing and balancing the international monetary system or exchanges between national systems*" and whose individual and collective utility is questionable (Tirole 2017). None of them (nor ourselves, for that matter) is "*impressed by bitcoin as an alternative to fiat money*," a hypothesis that is neither "*probable* [nor] *desirable, in our current capitalist situation*," for two reasons according to Varoufakis (2020): a cryptocurrency "*would not have the necessary mechanism to prevent capitalist crises from leading to depressions benefiting only the far right; and* [...] *its community-based democratic protocols would hardly contribute to the democratization of economic life*" (*Ibid.*). Starting from "*the example of seigniorage,*" Tirole (2017) considers that, while "*an increase in the money supply traditionally provides additional resources to the government* [since logically] *the proceeds from issuance must be returned to the community,*" cryptocurrencies do the opposite by *eliminating* them, since "*the first coins minted went into private hands* [and those] *newly minted create the equivalent of a wasteful race*" (Tirole 2017). Yet, as we have seen, seigniorage has not disappeared. The minting of cryptocurrencies is less connected to the real economy of economists than to the constraints and resources of their infrastructure and the actors involved (cf. Chap. I, section I.1.2). We have also observed that the status and qualities of national currencies were not established overnight: their high degree of integration into our lives is the result of a long process.

However, cryptocurrencies cannot claim such a high degree of integration, nor can they offer the same levers of collective intervention to the various regulatory bodies. If they are not designed for this purpose, cryptocurrencies cannot either “*force nations to* \[thereby\] *renounce it*” (Golumbia 2015, p. 121), whereas “*the promoters of bitcoin aim to destroy the state, the minimal foundation for the recognition of community rules against force*” (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 18). The expansion of their use would entail risks, particularly for monetary authorities.

### Monetary Authorities and the Endangerment of Their Regulatory Capacities?

Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies (CM) are accused of a form of "*cyber libertarianism*" grounded in an "*anti-government*" and "*anti-central bank and/or commercial bank*" rhetoric (Golumbia 2015, pp. 119–120). Based on this ideological charge, Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies are criticized by the vast majority of commentators, as they also appear to be a "*real headache for all those who consider public policies as a necessary complement to market economies*": "*too often used* for tax evasion or money laundering, [they call into question the ability of] *central banks* to conduct countercyclical policies in a world of private cryptocurrencies*" (Tirole 2017). The problem here is no longer that they are considered useless, but rather that they are perceived as dangerous and toxic. They are already regarded as "*ecological disasters*" (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015; Bank of International Settlements 2018; Servet and Dufrêne 2021, among others) due to their use of Proof of Work (PoW). Politically, they are also seen "*as a weapon intended to harm central banks and issuing banks* [and thereby] *the capacity of states to collect taxes and monitor the financial transactions of their citizens*" (Krugman 2013). These commentators reduce them to mere tools for circumventing regulations, facilitating illegal activities such as terrorism financing, capital control evasion, money laundering, tax fraud, and the exchange of illegal goods and services (*ibid.*; Stiglitz 2017; Tirole 2017; Krugman 2018b; Krugman 2018a; Roubini 2018; Green 2021). Yet illegal activities did not wait for the advent of cryptocurrencies to exist. Cryptocurrencies are even more easily traceable due to the public nature of their ledgers (a noteworthy property according to Captain Edouard Klein, PhD in Artificial Intelligence and cyber-specialized gendarme, Participant Observation No. 12), especially when compared to "fiat currencies" and cash, which is genuinely anonymous. What can be said about the coherence of critiques that, in one paragraph, reduce cryptocurrencies to pure speculative assets, and in the next explain their social danger by pointing to terrorist groups financing themselves with these very financial assets?

Moreover, because cryptocurrencies (CM) possess an automatic minting mechanism that allows them to issue their unit of account (UCN) independently of the state apparatus, they would be unable to adapt to macroeconomic conditions (Varoufakis 2020) and would contribute to depriving states and monetary authorities of their intervention capacities (Tirole 2017). These practical risks had already been raised prior to the advent of cryptocurrencies. As early as the 2000s, new information and communication technologies, particularly the emergence of "electronic money," had given rise to similar debates. Of particular relevance to our subject, these debates concerned both the status of these monetary innovations and the potential upheavals to be expected from them (Bounie 2001; Aglietta and Scialom 2002; Bounie and Lavoisier 2003). Electronic money is not a recent innovation, not least because electronic impulses serve as the medium for scriptural money. Preceding the appearance of cryptocurrencies by about a decade, these debates established that, depending on their architecture, electronic monies do not all have the same impact on the preexisting monetary systems at their inception.

Starting from the distinction between payment systems and settlement systems, established by Shackle in 1971, Bounie (2001) distinguishes three types of electronic payment systems: the first remains centered around a bank account; the second is anchored around a non-bank account; while the third corresponds to a new form of money, "pure electronic money." It is important to differentiate these types according to the depth of the modifications they induce on the preexisting hierarchical structure. Significant qualitative differences exist, and only systems belonging to the third type are considered potentially problematic (Bounie, 2001). Aglietta and Scialom (2002) specify that only the settlement circuit mobilized is decisive in judging a currency’s capacity to become autonomous from the monetary system from which it originates. Thus, the development of the first two types, qualified as "first-generation electronic money," is not conceived as problematic, which is not the case for the third type, encompassing what are called second-generation electronic monies (Bounie 2001; Aglietta and Scialom 2002; Sitruk 2008).

The innocuousness of the first group is attributed to the fact that these systems still operate within the space controlled by the central bank: payments circulate within a closed circuit controlled by banks, which remain a mandatory intermediary. Their monies remain denominated in the national unit of account and use the stock of traditional money for settlements. This is not the case for second-generation electronic monies: constructed around *ad hoc* clearing systems (Cohen 2002), they interface with different settlement systems and units of account, which themselves may be *ad hoc.* These electronic monies, operating within open circuits, contribute to three risks that erode the control capacity of monetary authorities: the reduction in demand for bank money, the proliferation of non-bank credit, and the emergence of private clearinghouses. Banks lose control over payment security rules and become dependent on a multiplicity of new actors who are not subject to the same regulatory and prudential constraints. Should these new monies persist and become widespread, they would have consequences for banks (loss of competitiveness, loss of control over the value chain, loss of clientele) and for monetary authorities (reduced capacity to control the money supply in circulation, inadequacy of monetary policy instruments).

Perceived risks depend on the representations held regarding money and the transmission channels employed by the Central Bank to influence prices and production. Two camps confront each other concerning the effectiveness of these risks. For the first camp, the empowerment of a portion of the monetary base engendered by second-generation electronic monies will call into question the effective capacity of central authorities to steer the real economy, by weakening the scope of central money supply, over which they hold a monopoly and whose demand depends on compliance with controlled standards (King 1999; Friedman 1999; Cohen 2002). This would then affect the very core of monetary policy, since monetary authorities would be left with only a mere power of influence *via* announcements of their preferential rates[^86] (B. Friedman 1999). It would thus be a foregone losing battle for public authorities; regulatory attempts would change nothing (Cohen, 2002): the advent of these new private monies should be seen as the closing of the monetary interlude, opened in the twentieth century, of currencies controlled by central banks (King, 1999, p. 14).  

Conversely, for the other camp, national currencies and their hierarchical system are not about to disappear. Monetary authorities should first retain influence over interest rates due to their prerogatives in *open market* operations or rediscounting (Woodford 2000; Goodhart 2000). Secondly, national currencies will maintain competitive advantages owing to their specific properties (anonymity, legal guarantee, long-standing usage routines), foremost among which is their role as carriers of trust in the State and its capacity to honor its commitments. Ultimately, the symbolic dimension of the unit of account will always be materialized and expressed as a liability of a central bank because of the trust placed in the social totality it legitimately embodies. There is thus no risk of this type of currency completely substituting traditional monies.

Thus, stablecoins (SCs) raise regulatory questions similar to those posed by second-generation electronic currencies. In addition to features such as bidirectionality, the use of clearinghouses, and *ad hoc* units of account (Aglietta, Ponsot, and Ould-Ahmed 2014), they introduce issues and uncertainties regarding the identity of the actors involved. The risks induced by the large-scale development of SCs will depend not only on their ability to attract new users but also on the capacity of national currencies to retain their user base. It is therefore unsurprising that national authorities are considering evolving the monetary forms they issue. It is against the backdrop of these debates that current announcements of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) must be understood. While monetary authorities regard the risk posed by SCs as still marginal, due to relatively low volumes compared to reserve currencies and other assets (ECB, 2015; Pfister 2017), they acknowledge the significant interest these currencies garner from the general public[^87]. To prepare for any eventuality, monetary authorities have developed scenarios and containment strategies, at the core of which lies the issuance of CBDCs: the choice of their architecture and characteristics will depend, within a defensive logic, on the private monetary initiatives that public authorities will face and to which they will adapt in order to maintain influence over money and the economy (Broadbent 2016; Pfister 2017)[^88].

CM and coiners thus participate, by virtue of their alterity, in reactivating an old and structuring conflict within the monetary field, opposing currents whose understanding of money is diametrically different. Worse still, the coiners “*come to trespass on* \[the\] *turf*” (J.P. Koning 2012) of reputed experts whom they criticize as *outsiders*. The latter question both orthodox theories conceiving money as a commodity (“*commodity money*”) and those prevailing within heterodoxy, which conceive it in a “*chartalist*” manner as a claim on the state (“*claim money*”) (*Ibid.*; Selgin 2013; Weber 2014b; Selgin 2014b). As for us, due to the singular positioning we have adopted, we do not adhere to any of these positions. The question posed in terms of substitution/competition between national currencies and CMs is, in our view, not the correct one. First, it is false to believe that CMs are immutable, that they do not adapt to their environment and therefore have no governance, as we already pointed out in our first chapter. Second, characterizing this governance becomes a decisive criterion of distinction within the field of money and crypto-assets. Consequently, it is imperative to analyze it positively, since this money and its governance are only “good” from the perspective of the actors and the reproduction of the payment community, and not from a normative, overarching standpoint.

## Characterizing the Governance of CMs Beyond Overarching Normativity: Theoretical and Practical Stakes of a Crucial Categorical Clarification

Coiners and monetary professionals disagree regarding the status and monetary qualities they ascribe to cryptocurrencies (CM), while nonetheless sharing certain aspects of a common monetary epistemology. They question whether Bitcoin, Ethereum, and cryptocurrencies could or should replace national currencies through a process of competition. Our analysis diverges on several important points. Although the statements of actors are of interest to us, we do not take them at face value. Moreover, the statements presented thus far, in accordance with our problematization and narrative choices, do not capture the heterogeneity of views held within coiner communities. Critiques of cryptocurrencies often simply reiterate, without further analytical distance, the assertions of certain coiners alone. However, other, more nuanced perspectives exist, thereby highlighting that Bitcoin and any cryptocurrency refer to diverse communities and are therefore objects of debate. Why then reinforce the ideology that confines them by participating in the visibility of only certain vocal ideologues? As a trace of a monetary nature shared with all money, cryptocurrencies belong to payment communities that are “*multifaceted*, *politically contested*, and *sociologically rich* in function and meaning. There is not one Bitcoin, [one Ethereum], but several” (Dodd, 2017, pp. 4 and 8), as our first chapter has outlined.

To obscure this diversity is to deprive oneself of recognizing governance precisely where it manifests—in the conflicts it inherently entails. To postulate the absence of governance is to relinquish one’s capacity to discern the relational networks at play, their supports, and the relevant spaces of their entanglement. It is simultaneously to forfeit one’s full ability to regulate effectively. Our research asserts the contrary. This governance, enigmatic and singular as it may still appear, exists and is structured around particular issues linked to the constraints inherent in the use of cryptocurrencies (CM). This governance involves a plurality of actors, activities, and emerging economic sectors, through socio-technical arrangements that blend *off-protocol* and *within-protocol* logics. It is through their detailed analysis that one can discern the relative powers of the various actors vis-à-vis one another, and thus the risks, responsibilities, and duties that might legally attach to each.

If, as Camus stated, “*to misname an object is to add to the misfortune of the world*”[^89], for us, relegating cryptocurrencies to the status of speculative financial assets is a problem in itself, one that raises more questions than it resolves. Far from closing the question of their governance, this stance opens it wide. Neither the contractual logic of signature nor that of the seal—which logically allow for an easy differentiation between public money and private securities/currencies, according to the status of the centralized issuing entity—seem to apply to cryptocurrencies. Hence our hypothesis of a monetary singularity residing within a new type of governance, the characterization of which constitutes a crucial practical and theoretical challenge.

The practical challenge is to enable a categorical clarification between cryptocurrencies, crypto-assets, and other digital currencies, without which it is impossible to establish and enforce the much-demanded regulations. The theoretical challenge concerns the necessity of not relying on normative and exogenous criteria to prejudge, beyond even the attribution of monetary status, what constitutes “good” money and/or “good” governance. Such “qualities,” we argue, must be assessed according to their respective capacities to ensure their reproduction in compliance with the norms and values upheld by themselves and their payment community. Chapter III will specifically aim to understand how cryptocurrencies and their communities are constructed and maintained dynamically, following the (more or less coordinated) involvement of a plurality of actors participating in their production, use, and evolution.

### Heterogeneous Communities, Controversies, Dual and Polycentric Governance

As payment communities, Bitcoin and Ethereum are less homogeneous than financial professionals claim. They consist of monetary groups differentiated both in practice and in value, reflecting the existence of a plurality of competing symbolic universes. Nakamoto initially sought to avoid intermediation by employing a network of nodes with minimal, if any, coordination (2008, p. 8). He did not address the concept of governance in his writings, but his critiques of hierarchical payment systems and governments shed light on his implicit ambitions. Nakamoto’s writings, which hold an almost sacred status (cf. Chap. I), are frequently cited, although their exegesis often gives rise to sometimes opposing gnoses. The Bitcoin initiator conceives the immutability of protocol rules as essential to producing decentralized consensus, a guarantor of “sound” money and resistance to censorship. The protocol is supposed to be self-sufficient due to consensus by Proof of Work (PoW): “*all rules and incentives necessary can be enforced through this consensus mechanism*” (Nakamoto 2008, p. 8). Sufficiency and autonomy are claimed under the slogan “Code is Law,” according to the rigorist interpretation given by numerous coiners (cf. Chap. III). V. Zamfir, from the Ethereum community, calls this conception the “Szabo law,” referring to the libertarian cypherpunk Szabo, who popularized this interpretation. The “*Szabo law*,” stipulating that computer code is sovereign in decentralized systems, has influenced “*a great number of discussions* [...] *on the fact that the blockchain is immutable and must remain so*,” justifying developers’ refusal “*to make protocol changes* [...] *when engaged in governance conflicts* [such as during the] *Bitcoin block size debate*” known as the “Scaling Debate” (see box [No. 4 below]), or during “*The DAO hard fork*” [which constituted] “*a clear violation of the Szabo law*” (Zamfir 2019, cf. Chap. III.3). Zamfir criticizes the “Szabo law,” which he considers “*deeply and radically anti-legal and anti-political*” (*Ibid.*). Szabo favors *on-chain* governance by code involving only non-human actors (and their operators). Zamfir proposes founding a “crypto law” or “*crypto law*” that integrates all human community components, even if their access to the network is intermediated. This conception privileges *off-chain* governance mechanisms and proposes reflecting on decentralized institutions enabling the reconstruction of a more democratic and participatory political system.

In the field of cryptocurrencies (CM), Szabo and Zamfir represent the two ideal-typical poles of the spectrum of conceptions of “good” CM, well-governed according to the coiners. Lustig and Nardi (2015, p. 1) qualitatively analyzed this diversity of views through the concept of “algorithmic authority,” understood as the trust placed in algorithms to direct human actions and validate information: they demonstrated that not all bitcoiners believe in Bitcoin’s incorruptibility and that they acknowledge the necessity of social and human judgments and mediations, even regarding cryptocurrencies. Similar research by DuPont (2018) on the Ethereum community and the “The DAO” crisis (case study in Chapter III) reveals the same type of heterogeneous ideals and imaginaries, especially concerning governance issues.

The “Scaling Debate” exposed to the general public the heterogeneity of Bitcoin and its payment community. This controversy over protocol changes highlighted the “*invisible politics*” of its codes and the community disputes surrounding their evolution, forming a dual governance (Rolland & Slim 2015; De Filippi & Loveluck 2016). Governance *by* the infrastructure (protocol codes, according to “Szabo’s law”) is superimposed by a broader governance *over* the infrastructure, concerning the cryptocurrency, its properties, and potential modifications. This governance *over* brings into conflict community components with divergent interests. These conflicts stem from mutually exclusive “*visions of Bitcoin*” (in terms of “ecash,” a “*low-cost P2P payment network*,” “*digital gold resistant to censorship*,” etc.) (Carter and Hasufly 2018), which this governance is supposed to reconcile. This raises questions about the definition of “community consensus” (Harper 2019) that prevails: the groups of legitimate actors entitled to participate in decisions (miners as operators in Szabo’s conception, or the largest number of users in Zamfir’s conception) and the types of mechanisms ensuring the expression of disagreements are at issue. The absence of resolution of the “Scaling Debate” at the time of De Filippi and Loveluck’s (2016) analysis leads them to conclude that Bitcoin’s governance is in “crisis”: although an open-source project involving diverse groups, it would rest on *“a small core of highly skilled developers*” (p. 1; the protocol developers or “Core devs,” cf. Chap. I), leading to a “*domination based on charismatic authority*.” This would reveal the “*limits of consensus formation among individuals driven by different political and commercial interests*” and points to the existence of “*divergences between the overall project goals* [...] *and the overly centralized and technocratic elites*” in charge of it (*Ibid.*).

Analyses such as Golumbia’s (2015), which regard Bitcoin as “*a manifestation of ‘distributed right-wing extremism’* [reflect only] *the politics of certain of its advocates*,” are unilateral (Dodd 2017, pp. 6–7). The heterogeneity of representations of a “good” cryptocurrency (CM) and the “good” governance that ensures it, as well as the conflicts it engenders, exhibit distinctive characteristics requiring complex multi-level and multi-actor interactions with a multiplicity of decision-making centers: the dual governance of cryptocurrencies appears polycentric, referring—as in Ostrom’s framework—to different territorial/community scales and to various sectors and actors. This polycentrism was evident in Chapter I through the monetization of cryptocurrencies and the various domains of infrastructural development that contributed to it, each constituting differentiated resource systems (with units of distinct resources) that support the resource system constituted by the CM protocol. This polycentrism corresponds to the more or less coordinated work of a multiplicity of actors, groups, and community components that have contributed to tracing the infrastructural development pathway of Bitcoin and Ethereum.

From the work already conducted, we can delineate two groups of stakeholders in the governance of cryptocurrencies (CM): the non-human actors of governance *by* the infrastructure (in gray) and the human actors of governance *over* the infrastructure, encompassing our eight domains of development (cf. Chap. I, with the same color coding). Contrary to the assertions of Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet (2015), a CM such as Bitcoin formally and informally defines groups of actors with different statuses and roles who are stakeholders in this governance[^90]. The non-human actors are the protocol clients. According to Szabo’s law, there exists a hierarchy between nodes considered passive (lightweight nodes of non-mining users) and active nodes (mining nodes), which participate in the entirety of consensus processes (verification, production, and validation of records). Full nodes, although absent from the analysis of De Filippi and Loveluck (2016), are active in governance *by* the infrastructure[^91] (cf. their crucial role during the “Scaling Debate” *via* the UASF proposal). Consequently, we include full nodes, because even though they rank below mining nodes—only verifying the validity of information they do not participate in producing—they are indeed active in governance *by* the infrastructure, as demonstrated by the important role they assumed *via* the UASF threat during the “Scaling Debate” (Lopp 2014; Rolland and Slim 2017; Janssens 2017). We include lightweight nodes, in contradiction to Szabo’s law, to show that any “inactive” user can “actively” change service providers to sanction governance choices they disapprove of (a behavior observable during the “Scaling Debate”). The particular status of lightweight nodes is indicated by their position between the two governance logics and their orange color. The human actors of governance *over* the infrastructure include: regulatory authorities (domain of compliance with national regulations); developers (domain of the Bitcoin protocol, layers 1 and 2); miners and related actors (domain of transaction processing activity); media and related actors (domain of information and knowledge); and end users. Merchant services and gateways encompass the domains of the usage sphere (financial and real), wallet and payment services, and compliance services.

Let us immediately establish the distinction between the Bitcoin protocol, as a resource system (in stock), and the resource units (in flow): the UCNs and the data processing capacity for transactions that this system enables to administer. Recall that a resource system is often collectively owned, whereas the resource units are individually appropriated, and that it may be composed of resource subsystems (more or less autonomous) articulated among themselves (Hess and Ostrom 2003). Thanks to the seven bundles of major rights of digital commons (Hess and Ostrom 2007, pp. 52–53), it is thus possible to interrogate the rights conferred to groups of actors, notably the singular hierarchy among governance nodes *by* the infrastructure, which translates into differentiated roles/powers, some of which depend on governance *over* the infrastructure:

(i) The right of access to Bitcoin is effective for mining nodes and full nodes, since light nodes here depend on the full node of their service provider, inducing power and risks (cf. Chap. I);

(ii) The right of extraction, or the capacity to obtain a resource unit of Bitcoin, is more complex (the issued UCNs are reserved for mining nodes according to modalities already explained, and actors wishing to use the transactional processing capacities of the system must acquire UCNs to pay usage fees, thereby creating a supply-demand feedback loop);

(iii) The right of management and those derived from contribution and withdrawal, for new digital commons, are interesting in the case of mining communities (the ability to regulate the use of the system and to transform resource units by making modifications appears less as an individual right than as a collective one, pertaining to governance *over* the infrastructure, which alone is capable of modifying protocol codes and seeing a majority of nodes update to change the consensus rules);

(iv) The right of exclusion, which allows determining who has the right of access as well as who has the right to refuse access, is of the same order. Although a mining node or a pool may censor addresses and transactions at will, competition among miners renders such action ineffective at the global level. To modify the protocol mechanics of the transaction fee market (a condition of access/exclusion) and/or effectively exclude addresses or transactions, a protocol modification would be necessary, again depending on governance *over* the infrastructure;

(v) Similarly, the right of alienation—the capacity to sell or transfer the rights of management and exclusion of Bitcoin—has no reality at the system-wide level and empirically only covers actor rights at the level of resource subsystems participating in the infrastructure: wallet clients, more or less centralized services, etc.

Questioning the governance of Bitcoin and Ethereum requires at least a rudimentary mapping of their actors and the relationships among them. This step allows for delineating the contours of the governance of community currencies (CCs), the roles, statuses, and bundles of differentiated rights at play, as well as for understanding the modalities through which actors participate *in*, make use *of*, and govern these new kinds of parallel community currencies.

### A Problematic Categorical Anarchy

CCs and the vast diversity of monetary and financial objects related to them provoke concerns among regulators (central banks, BIS, IMF, etc.)[^92] and governments[^93], prompting reflection on the advisability for central banks to implement new monetary forms, namely Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs), loosely inspired by the architecture of CCs. However, before identifying the risks and opportunities posed by these objects, as well as adapting their reactive functions to the evolutions driven by the ecosystem, it is necessary first to have definitional elements and a precise conceptual framework capable of apprehending such objects. It must be acknowledged that, within the emerging field of CCs, indistinction prevails. The key denominations, concepts, and categories within the CC domain are “*inconsistent*,” “*anarchic*” ([Vergne and Swain, 2017](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WPG7cK)) and blurred. This is true both in the media (Vergne and Swain 2017) and within the indigenous, even grey, literature (Walch 2017), as well as in administrative and legal frameworks that struggle to classify them (cf. section II.1.2). Terms such as “Blockchain,” “cryptocurrencies,” “crypto-assets,” “digital currencies,” “cyber-currencies,” and even “state cryptocurrencies”[^94] are used interchangeably. Some praise a miracle technology which, by itself, would enable perfect traceability of goods, identity and land registry management, governance pathways, democratic voting, improved supply chain management, etc. (Iansiti and Lakhani 2017; Lehr and Lamb 2018). It is difficult to navigate this landscape of yet unstable denominations, each attached to different, sometimes antinomic, concepts and visions depending on the contexts, technologies, and actors involved (the presentation of Altcoins in Chapter I sketched this diversity).

The relative scarcity of academic literature explains the absence of a well-defined conventional conceptual framework (as highlighted by Bano et al., 2017; Bonneau et al., 2015; Walch, 2017; Rauchs et al., 2018). This indeterminacy poses challenges both for researchers and regulatory bodies confronted with the *indigenous* language coined by practitioners. This *emic* language and its categories (such as “decentralization,” “disintermediation,” etc.), which evolve in accordance with the shifting economic and discursive strategies of actors, may unfortunately be adopted uncritically by some academics (Iansiti and Lakhani, 2017, for example), or—more perilously—by regulatory authorities (see [Walch 2017; Walch 2019](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FmxT9y)). Consequently, the construction of an *etic* language^1^, as a rigorous conceptual framework, is necessary in order to identify and analyze cryptocurrencies, crypto-assets, and other digital currencies according to their respective stakes.

Behind the generic designation of *distributed ledger protocols* lie socio-technical objects grounded in different principles, whose implications depend on technological choices and the actors who establish them. For the creator(s) and promoters of Bitcoin, such technologies embody a challenge to the economic and monetary order, capable of calling it into question. However, for others, the mere “*blockchain*” (a misnomer in our view[^95])—liberated from its unnecessary *native unit of account* and from its resource-intensive open consensus mechanisms—would serve as a vector of innovation and optimization for the sectors it was purportedly meant to overturn. As has been suggested, the development of the cryptocurrency ecosystem, which we have characterized as carnivalesque, allows for a diversity of actors with differing perspectives, resulting in socio-technical arrangements that involve sometimes opposing trade-offs, insofar as they reintegrate intermediation.

A distributed ledger protocol refers to a multiparty system operating without a central operator or authority, even in the presence of unreliable or malicious parties (Rauchs et al., 2018). Distributed ledger protocols are formally defined as “consensus machines” that guarantee each participant agreement on a shared dataset and its validity. This entails five simultaneous properties related to the governance of the protocol in question: (i) shared production of the ledger; (ii) multiparty consensus, either open or closed[^96]; (iii) independent validation of the state of its transactions and the integrity of the system; (iv) proof of tampering; and (v) resistance to unilateral alteration/censorship of present and past records (i.e., the transaction history; see *Ibid.*, p. 77). These characteristics allow for an initial characterization of socio-technical objects that are nonetheless very different in form and functioning.

At the protocol level, first, Rauchs et al. (2018, p. 77) demonstrated that while Bitcoin and Ethereum “*satisfy* [these] *five essential properties*,” this is not the case for other protocols such as Ripple, presented in the first chapter: for Ripple’s closed consortium protocol, “*the influence of Ripple Labs on the validating nodes calls into question the properties of multiparty consensus and resistance to tampering*” (*Ibid.*). This problematic influence on the ad hoc establishment of the peer group authorized to produce records recalls classical, centralized consensus protocols, which Nakamoto vehemently opposed. 

To this diversity of distributed ledger protocols is added another: within each considered ledger protocol exist application protocols or Dapps that themselves rely on proprietary native units of account (*tokens*), which may carry monetary or non-monetary uses (cf. the ICOs illustrated in Chapter I). These application protocols and their native units of account again raise the question of their degree of decentralization and the potential influence of a center. Their socio-technical design and parameters may confer more or less power to the development team: influence and control over source codes, network participation, or the price of native units of account, due to the relative share of the total circulating supply they hold, as perfectly exemplified by *Ripple Labs*.

Within this diversity, Bitcoin and Ethereum are taken as referents of the cryptocurrency category because they appear as unprecedented monetary constructions. However, this is not the case for many objects which, like numerous financial innovations, represent only “*old wine in new bottles*” (the title of Mah-Hui Lim’s 2008 article concerning mortgage credits): despite their innovative trappings, a plethora of currencies or digital assets circulating within distributed ledger protocols have issuance and circulation conditions for their native units of account, as well as conditions surrounding the protocol source codes, that reflect centralized logics long known.

### Characterizing the Governance of Cryptocurrencies: A Path Toward Reordering?

The distinctive properties outlined above allow for a clearer understanding of the field of distributed ledger protocols. When complemented by the conceptual tools of monetary institutionalism, they enable, in our view, a reordering within the categorical anarchy that characterizes the monetary domain. The conceptual distinction between the contractual logic of signature inherent to finance and the fiduciary logic of the seal, which lies at the heart of money (Orléan 1998, as revisited by Scialom 2003), permits the examination of governance issues by interrogating the legal status of the issuer, the nature of the signature affixed to its money or security, and the trust/distrust that may develop therein.

As we have seen, although cryptocurrencies bear risks similar to those of second-generation electronic money, they differ fundamentally in lacking both a strictly national dimension and recognized central issuers—features that are central to the legal definition of electronic money. Their primary territorial space is the Internet; they are embedded within a community-based monetary localism (although state or sub-state localisms may be supported through ad hoc socio-technical experiments and arrangements, cf. acceptance of local tax payments, granting their native cryptocurrency unit (NCU) the status of legal and fiat money). Despite design efforts aimed at approximating their coinage to that of metallic currencies, cryptocurrencies are in no way backed by a commodity or a third-party personal liability, unlike Ripple or the USDT stablecoin, which are influenced respectively by the companies Ripple Labs and Tether Limited, for example. These tokens qualify as crypto-assets insofar as they are issued by legally recognized private entities, and the trust placed in them by users—and thus their liquidity—rests on a contractual logic of signature. The same applies to many other crypto-assets which, although not guaranteed by dollars held by Tether (whose reserve claims are regularly scrutinized for credibility), see their protocols or the value of their NCUs depend on the discretionary actions of recognized actors endowed with more or less formal discretionary powers (Ripple is a pertinent example). Trust in this type of private money/securities is fragile because it ultimately relies on the signature of issuers of varying quality, whose credibility and solvency are practically tested, hence the existence of risks that open the possibility of payment defaults.

Conversely, cryptocurrencies (CM) do not appear to be founded on this contractual and private logic, but rather on a fiduciary logic which, although it is not a “seal” in the sense of a formally embodied public authority, nonetheless presupposes trust in a public authority incarnated within an informal community. The issuer of a cryptocurrency is not so much an isolated miner victorious in the race to discover a valid proof-of-work (PoW), but rather the protocol that delegates its functions to a set of actors who ensure its operation. From these considerations arises our rejection of a status as primary financial asset (which reduces to the mere presence of a signature logic). We contest the claim of coiners that cryptocurrencies are autonomous and sovereign technical objects, governed solely by their source codes, independent of human intervention. Nevertheless, cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ethereum do not formally fall under one or more formal entities that would hold exorbitant power over them. Recognizing a fiduciary logic at the heart of cryptocurrencies explains why they so readily fit within the definition of “fiat money” as given by a nominalist (Maurer et al., 2014) and non-statist epistemology: Bitcoin and Ether rely on distributed trust (Mallard et al., 2014), they are bearer social promises, and their purchasing power rests, through self-referentiality, on a set of institutions, actors, relationships, and social representations.  

However, behind this fiduciary logic common to both national currencies and cryptocurrencies lies an important difference. It is indeed in the status of the institutions and actors guaranteeing this purchasing power that cryptocurrencies distinguish themselves from national currencies. Not issued by a public law entity (hence their relegation to private monies, even in the absence of an individual issuer), they raise questions about their capacity to ensure longevity and stability comparable to national currencies. Contrary to the “false information” propagated by coiners regarding the short average lifespan of “fiat monies,” as previously presented (cf. Chapter II, section II.3.2), “fiat monies” are more enduring than private monies. Beyond the identity that embodies the collective (the seal referring more to the collective than to the individual who bears it), their continuity is captured by the formula “*The king is dead, long live the king*”: while an individual may fail and disappear, the same does not hold for a Nation, the embodiment of a political collective that never vanishes all at once, but at best reconstitutes itself. Such temporal durability derives from what cryptocurrencies cannot claim at first glance: a monetary governance that is more broadly articulated with the formal structure of States, their centralization, and their capacity for coercion. The history of the cryptocurrency ecosystem and its significant turnover[^97] demonstrate that cryptocurrencies and their communities can collapse overnight in the absence of actors committed to their development and maintenance. This fragility brings cryptocurrencies closer to private monies and securities governed by signature logic.  

This otherness that cryptocurrencies maintain with respect to the fiduciary logic of the seal and the contractual logic of signature leads us to propose a conclusive hypothesis allowing a reordering of the categorical chaos surrounding cryptocurrencies: they are novel monetary forms founded on a logic that we term (for lack of a better expression) a “*distributed consensus logic*.” This term recaptures the idea of consensus, essential to any currency, while indicating an unprecedented polycephaly pointing to a communal and collective work of co-minting.

More generally, the term “digital asset” is the most encompassing designation, referring not only to distributed ledger technologies but also including all forms of virtual money or securities (consider, for example, in-game currencies or loyalty points, which are assets of this type). “Crypto-assets” correspond to entities whose common feature is that they are issued and circulate within a distributed ledger protocol, and may refer indistinctly to currencies or securities. Within this category, one distinguishes digital currencies as a generic category[^98], whose governance is based on one or more established, recognized centers possessing extraordinary rights. Among these are private digital currencies, which operate according to a contractual signature logic—for example, the stablecoins such as Tether (USDT), whose issuer holds a private legal status. Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) correspond to national currencies operating according to a seal logic, where trust ultimately rests on a public issuing authority. Finally, there is the novel monetary category that we argue is constituted around cryptocurrencies (CM), whose tokens are issued and circulate within their native protocol according to a *distributed consensus logic*, where monetary trust relies on the interweaving of institutions, conventions, actors, and representations participating in a dual and polycentric governance structure, which remains to be studied.

Ultimately, many objects issued and circulating on distributed ledger protocols of cryptocurrencies are not cryptocurrencies themselves but rather fall within well-known logics and categories familiar to regulators, such as those of crypto-assets and digital currencies. In our view, cryptocurrencies distinguish themselves markedly from these categories and simultaneously demonstrate the futility of the currently employed catch-all categories, which, being overly broad and vague, hinder regulatory efforts. In all cases, these distinctions hinge upon the recognition of the type of governance, and thus the underlying logics of trust at work. This necessitates a case-by-case examination of the multiplicity of objects and socio-technical arrangements involved, as well as an evaluation of their properties of “security” and “decentralization,” according to the architectural choices that guided their design (cf. positioning within the Blockchain trilemma, cf. Chap. I, section 3.3), as well as their infrastructural development (presence or absence of a formal organization responsible for development and maintenance, treasury, etc.; cf. Chap. I). Characterizing the presence of a distributed consensus logic or a signature logic involves analyzing elements as diverse as the number and variety of node operators structuring the network (mining pools and their relative shares in transaction processing, number of hashers, etc.), the number of developers involved, their conditions of participation, and the modalities of administration of source code repositories on software forges, since each of these indicates degrees of centralization in decision-making (and thus the rights and duties pertaining to the categories of actors involved). From this perspective, Bitcoin and Ethereum emerge as exemplary case studies, insofar as their hitherto sustainable development and their non-zero exchange value raise questions—beyond the fragility of their “distributed consensus co-currency logic”—about the enduring conditions of their reproduction. We therefore argue that if cryptocurrencies constitute radical innovations in the monetary field, this is due to the nature of their governance. Although broad governance types have already been proposed to characterize the governance of certain cryptocurrencies (anarchic, hierarchical, plutocratic, etc.; [Rauchs, Glidden et al. 2018](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YTrAPO)), empirical work remains to be conducted.

# Conclusion of the Chapter

We conclude a chapter that offers a critical contribution to the controversy surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM). Through this discussion, we have interrogated the two premises of the “liberal-technocratic” syllogism advanced by coiners, which we contest: first, that cryptocurrencies are monies; and second, that they are “better” monies than national currencies due to an alleged apolitical nature stemming from the absence of human governance. Regarding the first premise, we surprise by siding with the coiners, asserting—contrary to the majority of analysts—that cryptocurrencies do indeed function as money. The reader will also be surprised to find no unequivocal response to the second premise. Does this refusal to take a clear stance on the “good” or “bad” qualities of cryptocurrencies circumvent a fundamental controversy? This question arises in any introduction to monetary theory, notably with Gresham’s Law, which states that “*bad money drives out good*.” This absence of positioning results from our theoretical standpoint and the preliminary conclusions drawn from our data. We affirm the monetary nature of Bitcoin and Ethereum, but not according to the liberal-technocratic vision upheld by coiners.

The significance of Gresham’s law lies in its normativity and relativity, which provide a critical perspective on those of the monetary domain. The first section explored the controversies surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies (CM) within the framework of existing economic and monetary theories. We examined the arguments of academic and practitioner detractors of cryptocurrencies, elucidating the theoretical and epistemological foundations of their critiques. Most arguments relegating cryptocurrencies to a category outside of money fall into two broad families: the substantialist, instrumental, and orthodox approach to money, and the competing nominalist corpus, notably in its statist chartalist version. The problem resides in a positioning that relates cryptocurrencies either to the reifying ideality of a capacity to synchronically bear canonical monetary functions, or to national currencies, with their numerous and diversified functions deeply embedded in social life.  

When faced with an attempt to explicate the concept of money, these critiques and their schools of thought, although essential to the economic debate, exhibit a problematic rigidity. We acknowledge the relevance of certain criticisms: undeniably, cryptocurrencies are rarely used to purchase goods and services (including illegal ones); they have a highly volatile market value, which explains the low propensity of coiners to spend them; and for these same reasons, the share of speculative uses is significant, even structuring in terms of *monetization*, as demonstrated in Chapter I. The infrastructural development of Bitcoin has revealed the plurality of *monetization* pathways for cryptocurrencies: speculation has enabled the development of a differentiated and highly liquid market environment where, provided access to the internet, crypto-assets are exchangeable into international currencies at any time, in any place, and every day of the year.  

It is both partial and biased to reduce cryptocurrencies to “valueless” financial assets, equating them with financial bubbles and Tulipomania, without recognizing their other uses as units of account and means of payment, except for illegal ones.

The recognition of the monetary uses and practices of coiners necessitated the development of our own perspective and the conceptual foundations of our usage-sensitive monetary institutionalism. Situated within a non-statist nominalism, we consider that the monetary nature of cryptocurrencies (CM) resides less in their capacity to fully embody the canonical monetary functions than in the manner in which they are perceived and adopted by users. Practices—specifically, uses in accounting and payment, which are central to our definition of money—are irrefutable. Illegal uses, for example, have existed and have also been structuring in terms of monetization, and their marginalization relative to legal activities since 2013 is equally significant (cf. Chap. I.2.1). These transactions, frequently mentioned during our interviews, contribute to the “wow effect” of cryptocurrencies, akin to cross-border payments, which test their monetary value in the eyes of users \[Super Anon, interview\].  

Speculation is not limited to betting on the price of BTC or ETH cryptocurrencies via centralized platforms such as Coinbase (although this is predominantly the case). Ethereum has seen the development of a complex and differentiated monetary and financial infrastructure where, *on chain,* BTC (in the form of WBTC) and ETH provide access to numerous services (decentralized exchanges, lending protocols, collateralized borrowing) that allow for their exchange, lending, or borrowing of other cryptocurrencies or stablecoins. We have observed and participated in these uses and other accounting and payment practices (event-driven, ceremonial, subsistence crypto-finance as described by Allard 2018). Reducing these uses to mere derivative accounting uses, as is often reproached to cryptocurrencies, misses their primary usage. To use Bitcoin or Ethereum, one must possess cryptocurrencies in order to pay usage fees that are exclusively denominated and payable in them.  

As nominalism postulates, money is a debt relation inscribed within the rules established by a superior entity. If the issuer is neither the State nor the central bank, it is also not the individual miner: these cryptocurrencies represent bearer claims on the protocol, which accepts them as payment for the use of its transaction processing capacity and recording space.

In a third section, we emphasized that these usages, this processing capacity, and this recording space are dynamic and evolving rather than static, as they are not irrevocably fixed within their protocol codes. The infrastructural development of Bitcoin, traced in Chapter I, demonstrated that the protocol experienced bugs and evolutions leading to community controversies. The case of the “Scaling Debate” succinctly illustrated how the Bitcoin community split in a Gresham-like conflict: the “good” Bitcoins of the *Small Blockers* are the “bad” ones for the *Big Blockers*, the former privileging the store-of-value function at the expense of the medium-of-exchange function desired by the latter. The majority of Bitcoiners consider increasing the size of the blocks as an unacceptable challenge to Bitcoin’s security and decentralization, which are the sources of its demand as a reserve currency. The victory of the *Small Blockers* pushed those who wished to see bitcoins circulate massively and easily to secede. Usage in account and payment relies on the reproduction of the monetary system, a product of a minting process that denotes “*a collective intentionality aimed at the perpetuation of the currency*” (Théret 2008, pp. 8–9). This highly political process must elicit adherence and consensus among the members of its payment community; otherwise, a monetary crisis ensues. Our conclusive hypothesis in this chapter is that the monetary singularity of cryptocurrencies lies in a dual polycentric governance, placing them in a new category of currency distinct from existing private or public monies, which are always related to a single center. This incongruence with the logic of seal and that of signature leads us to identify for them a distinct logic of distributed consensus.

Embedded in a nominalism grounded in discretion, the quality and viability of a currency are assessed by its capacity to reproduce itself legitimately in the eyes of its actors. For us, governance must at a minimum enable the definition and realization of collective objectives, the management of conflicts, and the control of power relations within the concerned community, all while remaining legitimate in the eyes of all. The sharp conclusions drawn by De Filippi and Loveluck (2016) regarding the *crisis* of Bitcoin governance notably raise questions about the role of Bitcoin’s “core developers” within the “*technocratic power structure*” (p. 19) that would allegedly prevent the resolution of this conflict. The crisis of the “Scaling Debate” “*revealed the difficulty of establishing a governance structure that would properly interface*” between “*two different types of communities*,” namely the user community (the “*active*” and “*passive nodes*”) and “*the developer community, which contributes to the Bitcoin project code*” (p. 10). However, it is difficult to assert that this should be seen as a “*governance crisis and* \[a\] *failure to resolve conflicts*,” given that at the time, the “Scaling Debate” was only emerging as a public problem (for the user community) and had not yet entered its resolution phase. To consider at that time the refusal to increase block size as a failure of Bitcoin governance implies an external judgment beyond the actors themselves, for whom it was a success: *“Bitcoin has governance problems” is like saying “Van Gogh is not photorealistic”—it’s true, but [...] ridiculous. Cypherpunks hate governance. The goal was to block changes. Other projects have different experiences, but do not subject Bitcoin to authoritarian standards [...] The idea that governance systems serve the average user is constantly refuted by history: resistance to change is resistance to capture.”* Nonetheless, these authors open a path by highlighting community heterogeneity, the conflicts it generates, and the dual and polycephalous governance that emerges therein. Hence, it becomes impossible to overlook the diversity of *coiner* communities. Paradoxically, academics and critical practitioners who seek to regulate (or even ban) cryptocurrencies, on the grounds that they presuppose an absence of human governance of cryptocurrencies (a guarantee of their “poor” monetary quality), thereby lose their capacity to effectively regulate. This presumed absence, besides being factually incorrect, leads them to abdicate their ability to discern the relational ensembles at play (the types of human and non-human actors and their dependencies or lack thereof), their supports, and the relevant spaces of their entanglement (*on chain*, *off chain*). Our work claims the opposite. This enigmatic and singular governance must be analyzed. The qualities of the polycentric governance of cryptocurrencies can only be interrogated through empirical analysis of the practices of coiners; without this, it is impossible to apprehend the modalities of the evolution of protocol codes (the actors and processes contributing to their production and maintenance), the desired properties, the meanings they embody, and ultimately, the conditions for maintaining the trust granted to cryptocurrencies to function in a stable and sustainable manner. For us, however, to study the crisis governance of cryptocurrencies requires examining the crisis in its entirety, from *the onset of the crisis* to *the restoration of order*. In the case of the “Scaling” debate, resolution will come through the formation of a clear consensus among the vast majority of community components, via the implementation of a “*decision-making mechanism*” that is not as fragile or centralized as often claimed. This crisis investigation, aimed at deepening reflections on the statuses, roles, and rights of each group of coiners, as well as on the procedures and regulatory/control devices put in place, requires dedicated work, which our third chapter aims to undertake.
