General Introduction

> “The difficulty lies not in understanding new ideas, but in escaping old ideas that have spread their roots into every corner of the minds of those who have received the same education as most of us.”
>
> **“The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money,”  
> John Maynard Keynes, p. 6, preface to the 1st English edition (1936)**

In 2008, under a pseudonym and amidst one of the worst banking and financial crises of the 21st century, a radical monetary challenge was launched: Bitcoin. With it began the phenomenon of *cryptocurrencies* (CCs), whose *open source code*—allowing access to, duplication, modification, and reuse of software code—enabled the creation of a diverse array of unidentified monetary objects, grouped under this neologism. Today, nearly 13,131 CCs are traded on 532 exchanges, with a market capitalization approaching one trillion dollars (peaking above three trillion at the end of 2021)[^1]. Some projects, such as Ethereum, offer complex financial services (deposits and loans, investment vehicles, derivatives of all kinds, etc.). Their ambitions? To open “*a new space of freedom for many years*” [(Nakamoto 2008a)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4mCGVq), to “*democratize money*,” and to restore its dimension as a “*commons*” (Wirdum 2014; Singer 2021).

According to its creator Satoshi Nakamoto [(2008)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CPNkOD), Bitcoin responds to the crisis and its management. His sparse writings[^2], his architectural choices, and the indelible inscription in its genesis data of a *Time* magazine cover story on a bank bailout[^3] all constitute critiques of monetary systems. This is part of a longstanding movement contesting banks and governments, embedded within a history of monetary innovation. This critique does not aim to reform the rules of a system from which Nakamoto expects nothing. Perceived as the product of collusion between political and banking authorities, this reviled system (and the powers it confers) is to be challenged from the flank. Offering a monetary system *alongside* national payment and settlement systems is not sufficient to characterize the innovative aspect of cryptocurrencies. Parallel currencies, notably local and complementary currencies (LCCs), have already done so on a smaller scale (Blanc 1998a; Blanc 1998b; Blanc 2009a). Nakamoto’s critique stands out by its ideological substance and practical form. Local and complementary currencies aim to establish monetary systems that emphasize the human element and social relations, reinstating proximity, trust, shared values, and deliberation in monetary affairs (*Ibid.*). However, our *e-*monetary issuers distinguish themselves by rejecting all of this, at least ostensibly (Dodd 2017, p. 8). LCCs contest the technocratic enclosure of national monetary governance, to which their members claim association. In contrast, *coiners* advocate for its outright abolition: the objective is to substitute governance by technique.

Nakamoto presents a reasoning opposite to that of the MLC: criticizing the governance of money does not imply reinstating the human being at its center. His currency will be "*trustless*"[^4]. The "*fundamental problem of traditional money*" is the trust imposed as "*necessary for it to function*" (Nakamoto 2009). Authorities and banking and financial actors establish themselves as "*trusted third parties*" and "*conflict mediator[s]*," a status that grants them authority and power in the overall governance. This generates costs (Nakamoto 2008), or worse, abuses: "*One must trust the central bank not to debase the currency, but the history of fiat currencies is full of breaches of that trust. One must trust banks to hold our money and transfer it electronically, but they lend it out in waves of credit bubbles with barely a fraction in reserve. We must entrust them with our privacy, trust them not to let identity thieves drain our accounts.*" (Nakamoto 2009)[^5] Change comes through the creation of a monetary system *ex nihilo*, radically opposed and without coercion, in a space where states have little control: the *“cyberspace”*. Monetary rules are defined and enforced within a network of actors following a computer protocol that one joins pseudonymously and voluntarily. No prior approval is required. To the vertical privileges of the hierarchical system correspond the horizontality and universality of a communication protocol and a peer-to-peer (P2P) network open and accessible to all. This protocol would allow undifferentiated machines—called “nodes”—*via* a system of standardized rules and procedures, to agree on the validity of a set of endogenous data: the possession and transfer of native units of account (NUA), the bitcoins of Bitcoin[^6]. There is no credit in exchange for monetary creation, but participation in the processing and securing of the payment system’s transactions: new NUAs are distributed to nodes to incentivize their “*honest*” participation (*Ibid.*). There are no closed and opaque databases to centralize accounts, nor privileges or “backdoors” accessible to a restricted group of actors. Guarantees of individual sovereignty, transparency, and auditability would be total, due to holoptic architectures granting everyone the ability to see the entirety of what transpires therein. Proprietary banking software’s counters and black boxes are replaced by a variety of *open-source software*\*, guaranteeing users the auditability of their holdings. For Nakamoto, the uniqueness of the account ledger does not rely on centralized management. The genius of his *consensus algorithm*\* lies in enabling a multitude of undifferentiated competing nodes to each work independently to produce redundant copies of the accounting ledger, collectively converging on a canonical transactional register (called the *blockchain*\*). Bitcoin addresses the opacity of banking and financial channels by perfect accessibility to all addresses and transactions, both past and pending. The “decentralization” of the network among a multitude of actors would guarantee, by limiting single points of control, the announced properties and overall resilience: given the large number, no one could censor transactions, falsify the endogenous data\* replicated in the ledgers, or modify the protocol software codes across all nodes.

In summary, computer protocols alone would produce monetary and payment systems that resolve the problems of national currencies as well as those of electronic currencies, which are duplicable due to their digital nature (the *double-spending problem*, Nakamoto 2008). There would no longer be breaches of trust through arbitrary acts, but rather automatic rules and procedures established by a protocol that all participants must follow. Currency would be freed from any jurisdictional framework, belonging to no center nor any intermediation, except the (technical) intermediation of the protocol itself. Protocol codes would extricate it from social relations, debates, and conflicts, and monetary policy would no longer be subject to deliberation. This “decentralization” would produce “*neutral*” and “*apolitical*” monetary systems, with “sound” currencies of unparalleled credibility (*Antonopoulos Bitcoin Q&A* 2018): their minting (quantity of units of account, modalities of issuance and circulation) being “immutable,” “objective,” “transparent,” and “automatic” would guarantee temporal coherence beyond reproach. In a competitive space among currencies left to market forces, the exchange value of such cryptocurrencies would derive from these attractive and valorized characteristics guaranteed by their protocol. These currencies would rest *upon* (and compel *to*) a new individual sovereignty, relegating to the past all forms of authority, delegation, and discretionary powers attributed thereto. They would offer an unprecedented choice: that of “*placing* \[one’s\] *money and* \[one’s\] *faith in a mathematical framework free from politics and human error*” (T. Winklevoss, cited in [Mullin 2013](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?n7y4de)), where “*an algorithm replaces the function of government*” (Al Gore, cited in [OConnell 2016](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?js2iEu)).

The discovery of monetary ambitions perceived by some as a “*dream*” and by others as a “*nightmare*” of “*libertarian*” monetary innovation (De Filippi 2013; Karlstrøm 2014) made us smile as much as it made us reflect. Like many, we initially greeted cryptocurrencies (CM) embodied by Bitcoin with disdain. How could one speak of money in the face of assertions so fundamentally opposed to the theoretical and practical essence of money? The reifying and paradoxical claims of an ideology seeking to transform money into a thing—a pure mechanism, insulated from social relations and intermediaries—demanded scrutiny. Through an examination of their design and operation, it is necessary to test the neutrality and technical autonomy purportedly guaranteed by their computer protocols. Their monetary claims raise questions regarding their status and monetization: it is imperative to investigate the capacity of their unique cryptographic numbers (UCN) to be used as units of account and means of payment, as well as the conditions that have allowed mere computer protocols and their Monopoly-like *e-*money to become cryptocurrencies. Furthermore, the presumed absence of any human intermediation and governance must be questioned, particularly with respect to their protocol code, whose proclaimed immutability remains to be confirmed or refuted.

In what follows, we will address the construction of our research object—the governance of cryptocurrencies—and the three interlocking issues it entails (A). Next, we will explicate our theoretical framework, combining an institutionalist monetary approach with an ethnographic methodology drawing on Science and Technology Studies (STS) (B). We will then present the methodological challenges of our multi-level inquiry, the arrangements and strategies for accessing the field sites, as well as the data collected (C). The general economic framework of the thesis will be discussed in the fourth section (D). We will conclude this introduction with a declaration of interests (E).

# A. The Governance of Cryptocurrencies: Constructing Our Research Object

The subject of our research and its associated issues were gradually constructed through discoveries and deepening inquiries. Drawing on the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification, we first explicate the context of our research before addressing our epistemological, theoretical, and methodological positioning [(Avenier and Thomas 2011)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mHiCBC). Reconstructing its development requires revisiting the particular resonance of Bitcoin with our interest in money and questions of its governance. Our acculturation to cryptocurrencies (CM) led to dissatisfaction with: the scarce emerging analyses in the literature (A/1.); the refusal of academics and practitioners to recognize these as money due to theoretical approaches that our institutionalist stance critiques (A/2.); and the technicist liberal pretensions of certain coiners, who revive the monetary controversy opposing the technical “rule” to political “discretion” (A/3).

## 1) An Interest in Money and “Unidentified Monetary Objects”

Let us return to the context of our research. Its object, cryptocurrencies (CM), stems from a longstanding interest in monetary questions. Initiated in October 2018 under the supervision of E. Chiapello, this work was preceded by the beginning of a doctoral thesis under the direction of J. Sapir, inspired by the banking and financial crisis that coincided with the completion of our Master’s degree (late 2009). Economic necessities interrupted our formal research activities; it was during this period of professional activity[^7] and informal research that we encountered the object CM. Our engagement with this field dates back to 2015 and continues our prior reflections on the governance of the monetary system: statuses and roles of monetary authorities, legitimacy of monetary policies, roles of payment community members, institutional arrangements, etc. We had heard of Bitcoin as early as 2011, but our interest was only awakened later by the phenomenon’s survival, the development of its ecosystem, and the gradual increase in its media and academic coverage. A thorough reading of the Bitcoin white paper and our initial “*participant immersions*”[^8] revealed their innovative character. Cryptocurrencies offered the unprecedented opportunity to study a rare phenomenon in the field of money: the *ex nihilo* emergence of “*a new form of money*” (Kavanagh and Miscione 2017, p. 10) which, despite its non-state foundations, was experiencing unparalleled development.

[^7]: [Footnote content as in original]
[^8]: [Footnote content as in original]

The proliferation of academic studies in recent years contrasts with their relative absence at the onset of the phenomenon. Differences exist according to disciplinary fields. The earliest and most numerous research originates from computer science. Bitcoin has been extensively “*studied within the distributed systems community for* [*closed ledger protocol*] *systems* [and] *has revitalized the field and led to a plethora of new designs*” (Bano et al. 2017, p. 1). The “*Nakamoto consensus*” based on *proof of work* (PoW), the first *consensus algorithm*\* enabling open ledger protocol systems, is the “*most debated component*” (Bonneau et al. 2015, p. 104). Although this corpus is important for understanding the “technical” mechanisms and stakes of this field, it is not central to our work, as it is too transversal and distant from our references and expertise.  

The phenomenon of cryptocurrencies (CM) initially received increasing coverage in the media sphere. A bleak portrayal was drawn, emphasizing illegal uses, scams, cyberattacks, and the erratic nature of market prices. Phases of speculative euphoria and panic polarized attention. To the media’s interest was added that of regulators and governments[^9]. Large-scale private enterprise projects, such as Facebook’s now-defunct Libra/Diem, heightened concerns and accelerated work on *Central Bank Digital Currency* (CBDC). Monetary, banking, and financial authorities, as well as tax administrations, engaged early on. A grey literature, emanating from administrations, central banks, and regulatory institutions, developed in the form of reports and information notes (European Central Bank 2012, 2015; Banque de France 2013), but it struggles to establish homogeneous and stabilized classifications.

In the emerging field of cryptocurrencies (CM), there prevailed a marked indistinction between “*inconsistent*” and “*anarchic*” classifications ([Vergne and Swain, 2017](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WPG7cK)), due to a relative lack of academic literature (Bonneau et al. 2015; Bano et al. 2017; Walch 2017; Rauchs et al. 2018). The media (Vergne and Swain 2017), indigenous and grey literature (Walch 2017) indiscriminately employed the terms CM, “Blockchain*,” “crypto-assets*,” “digital currencies,” and “cybermoney”[^10]. This technology was hailed as a miracle offering, beyond “sound” currencies, improved management of supply chains, identity and land registries, democratic voting, and so forth [(Iansiti and Lakhani 2017; Lehr and Lamb 2018)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?3p68vs). Although still nascent, these denominations appeared problematic to us because they referred to systems grounded in different principles, whose implications depended on technological choices as well as the actors establishing them (Rauchs et al. 2018). The generic label of *distributed ledger protocols* concealed diametrically opposed systems (*Ibid.*). Some entities presented as CM were simply digital currencies or crypto-assets, without revolutionizing money as Bitcoin did. Relying on the credibility and solvency of central issuers, they were merely old formulas in new technical guise. The absence of an *etic* language, understood as a rigorous and unified conceptual framework, was problematic for researchers, regulators, and also for citizens, confronted with an *indigenous* language evolving according to the economic and discursive strategies of the actors involved. This context, and the questions raised about the technicist liberal ambitions of CM, convinced us to contribute to this still uncultivated, even neglected, field[^11].

It was predictable that the media would focus on the spectacular surface of these objects. It was understandable that practitioners refused to consider them as money, since they did not correspond to existing regulatory definitions and categories. However, researchers are not necessarily constrained by these definitions. We were therefore surprised by the reactions that CM elicited within the field of money.

## 2) The Controversy over the Monetary Nature of Cryptocurrencies

Not only was there little research (in the humanities and economics), but the existing texts also exhibited a disdain for an object perceived as anything but money (techno-libertarian whim, tulip mania 2.0, scam, Ponzi scheme, etc.). The majority of economists and practitioners condemned Bitcoin without genuinely engaging with it (hence the absence of academic work among the former), criticizing it in newspapers, on social media, or in blogs. Leading “Nobel” laureates in economics (Tirole 2017; Krugman 2018; Shiller 2018) asserted that these entities possessed only monetary pretensions. They judged cryptocurrencies as lacking intrinsic value, technically and economically flawed, and useful solely for illegal or speculative activities. They saw in them nothing but “*Bubble, Fraud, and Turmoil*” (Krugman 2018) with a predictable fate. If cryptocurrencies did not collapse on their own, they argued, they should be banned to protect the “*monetary and financial system*,” society, and the planet from all the dangers they supposedly pose (Stiglitz 2017; Servet and Dufrêne 2021). These objects, superficially apprehended in our view, were condemned by financial professionals who often prophesied their demise[^12].

These discourses were contradicted by stubborn facts. Over the course of a decade, Bitcoin has maintained an availability rate exceeding 99.987%[^13], with a continuously growing outstanding transferred value, number of transactions, and users (see Appendices Nos. 1, 2, and 3). Our initial disdain for these objects, shared with these authors, transformed into piqued interest. It was necessary to test the paradoxical nature of their technicist liberal claims, not by theoretically explaining why these experiments should fail, but by empirically revealing the conditions of their survival and development. Thus, far from the virulent media stances, some academic works did exist. More nuanced, these studies examined Bitcoin’s properties and the determinants of its market price (including potential price manipulations, Halaburda and Gandal 2014; Gandal et al. 2018); the macroeconomic risks and stakes of cryptocurrencies (Brito and Castillo 2013; Aglietta, Ponsot, and Ould-Ahmed 2014; Böhme et al. 2015; Raskin and Yermack 2016), as well as implications for exchange rates and capital control avoidance (Pieters 2016). Finally, several studies focused exclusively on the blockchain and its applications, including by central banks (Danezis and Meiklejohn 2015; Raskin and Yermack 2016; Koning 2016; Pfister 2017).  

In the legal field, a vast segment of the literature has concentrated on the issues surrounding the legal framework of Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, platforms, and practices it has initiated (Walch 2017). Their monetary status, claimed in competition with traditional currencies, remains debated, even contested, although marginally recognized by some studies (Yermack, 2013; Maurer et al., 2013; Ali et al., 2013; Jeong 2013; Böhme et al., 2015; Raskin and Yermack, 2016; Dodd, 2017). Their singular properties and limited uses cause cryptocurrencies to awkwardly fulfill the three canonical monetary functions (unit of account, medium of exchange, and store of value; see Yermack, 2013; Ali et al., 2013). This explains why they can claim such status. Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies would be “*not* [a] *currency*, but* [mere] *disembodied monetary instruments*,” and should not be considered as such (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 11). Although recognized as means of payment, they are relegated, according to monetary and fiscal authorities, to the status of mere financial assets. This is not entirely erroneous—a currency, when taken as a foreign exchange asset, loses its monetary essence by becoming a portfolio asset—but it is reductive and confines them to a secondary status. Cryptocurrencies such as “*Bitcoin* [are] *difficult* to integrate into a political conception of money* [which may be] *perhaps* [...] *incomplete*” (Jeong, 2013, p. 27).

Critiques of Bitcoin are, like all critiques, situated. The criticisms from researchers within the orthodox corpus are unsurprising. In this corpus, money and monetary and financial innovations occupy little space. Conversely, it was more surprising to see these objects relegated outside the field of money by authors of the Francophone monetary institutionalism (FMI) tradition (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet, 2015). During conferences, we occasionally received sharp rebukes: to consider these objects as monies was a fatal error[^14], bordering on excommunication. Paradoxically, the authors whose conceptual framework was essential to us proved to be among the most critical. We acknowledged lacking “*information concerning both its uses and the collective management of the capacities for access and reproduction of this liquidity*” (Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015, p. 4), but this prompted us to undertake exploratory research rather than to formulate definitive conclusions. However, a few rare studies have relied on this institutionalist corpus to question Bitcoin’s claims to be a currency ([C Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015; Rolland and Slim 2017)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OITzJ8): cryptocurrencies correspond to monies conceived as payment systems (Cartelier 1996), founded around organized payment communities (Knapp 1924; André Orléan 2019), whether or not we subscribe to the almost religious representations and beliefs[^15] of these communities. This implies that cryptocurrencies cannot be devoid of a governance structure, even a rudimentary one, which this thesis seeks to help reveal.

## 3) Revealing the “Unspeakable” Governance of Cryptocurrencies Against Liberal Technicist Claims

Our interest in examining the monetary claims of monetary communities (MCs) was ultimately grounded in their resonance within the debate on the “*good*” currency and its governance. This longstanding controversy, structured around the formula “*rules versus discretion*,” interrogates the role of politics in monetary affairs. The central question concerns the neutrality of money, perceived as intrinsically beneficial for the economy (Théret 2008, p. 12). Proponents of the neutrality (or neutralization) of money, who prioritize the stability of the unit of account, advocate for strict rules to limit political interference in the quantity of money in circulation: the “sound management” of money by central banks implies that discretion must be constrained (de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003; Kindelberger 2004; Tutin 2009). Conversely, advocates of discretion argue that the quality of a currency depends on the capacity of monetary authorities to undertake discretionary actions to prevent systemic collapse: money and monetary policy involve relative trade-offs, so the stability of the unit of account should not compromise the viability of the payment system (Kindelberger 2004; de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003). These fault lines are recurrent in the history of monetary thought: present in pre-classical debates, they reemerge in controversies between the Currency School and the Banking School, as well as between monetarists and Keynesians (ibid.; Tutin 2009). Today, financial liberalization policies and the emergence of the New Monetary Consensus, a successor to monetarism, mark the victory of rules and a quantitativist vision (Galbraith 2008). Accompanied by prescriptions regarding independence, transparency, and credibility for monetary policies and responsible authorities (Friedman 2008; King 1999; Feiertag and Margairaz [eds.] 2012), this theoretical and practical victory, although total in the collective mindset[^16], was contradicted by empirical reality. Indeed, the past decades have necessitated intensive recourse to monetary policies that this theoretical corpus condemned, revealing that they were “non-conventional” only in name.

For the coiners, cryptocurrencies succeed where past experiments have failed. Bitcoin would position itself, *ironically* and *paradoxically*, as *absolutely apolitical* and *neutral* (Keir 2022). The paradox lies *in the very existence of bitcoin*: *if the protocol itself is apolitical, this act of creation is profoundly political* (*Ibid.*). The irony refers to the fact that *a world [...] deeply polarized and politicized [...] has led to the creation of an immutable and apolitical system [...] indifferent to any belief or ideology [...] resolutely neutral [...] with regard to race, religion, ethnicity, sex, height, hair color, skin color, eye color, body type, body shape, name, language, location, wealth, or any other myriad of identification and distinction factors* (*Ibid.*; echoed by Antonopoulos 2013). An immutable software framework would guarantee this neutrality, endowing cryptocurrencies with a monetary issuance imbued, to varying degrees, with a *digital metallism* (Maurer et al., 2014, p. 2) and rendering monetary regulation independent from political regulations. By framing the total quantity of monetary units issued (limited to 21 million units for Bitcoin) as well as the modalities of their issuance, distribution, and circulation, the protocol codes would, according to these analyses, preserve the currency’s value from inflation induced by political discretion. From this perspective, these experiments attempt to surpass the most rigorous monetary ambitions, such as those of *Free Banking*, emphasized by numerous authors (De Filippi 2013; Dréan 2013; Karlstrøm 2014; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015). *Free Banking* aims to abolish the monopoly of states and central banks in the issuance and circulation of money, central ambitions of the Austrian School, expressed by Von Mises (1912) or Hayek (1976), and to which certain coiners also lay claim.

This radical positioning of cryptocurrencies (CM), rekindling the “constant battle” between these two schools of thought and the “*conflict that has opposed them under one name or another since the 17th century and probably before*” (Kindelberger, p. 62), partly explains their reception by economists. However, this “*digital metallism*” poorly conceals their nominalist dimension: the purchasing power of cryptocurrencies, without backing by a third party’s asset or liability, constitutes a promise of future acceptance (Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz 2013, p. 14). This self-referential promise is supported by a set of institutions, actors, relationships, and social representations. Thus, cryptocurrencies, like any form of money, are founded on trust and legitimate authority, essential elements in Simmel’s (2009) or Simiand’s (cited by Orléan, 2008) definitions of money.  

The literature on cryptocurrencies and governance issues is sparse and ambivalent. These studies highlight the existence of a social dimension linking Bitcoin’s “*practical materiality*” to the axiological and semiotic dimensions of the community (Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz 2013; Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015), but primarily focus on the “technical” protocol. They emphasize the technological architecture and the relationships within the chain* (which they tend to reify) as the sole modality of governance. In our view, due to a certain bias, these analyses create the illusion that all institutions and sociability relations at the core of money are grounded in technology. It is necessary to accept that “*Bitcoin provides a useful reflection on the sociality of money, although it integrates this sociality of trust into its very code*” (Maurer, Nelms, and Swartz 2013, p. 3). Trust would crystallize entirely in the code, or even solely in the “*cryptographic algorithm*” (*Ibid.*, p. 4; echoed by Ponsot 2021)[^17]. Bitcoiners would hold an absolute “deference to codes,” encapsulated in the slogan “Code is Law,” reducible to individual trust guaranteed by the technical nature of their “*algorithmic authority*” (Lustig & Nardi 2015).  

Social relations, software maintenance, and internal political debates—occurring off-chain* and without which cryptocurrencies would be nothing—are left in a blind spot, or even explicitly denied [(Dupré, Ponsot, and Servet 2015)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HZ3H1U). These texts call for moving beyond this perspective, emphasizing that “*the reliability of money is based on shared trust among individuals and supported by multiple institutions and technical infrastructures*” (Mallard, Méadel, and Musiani 2014, p. 3). “*Behind the code, is there not a designer* [...] *a community? Beyond the P2P network constituted by the nodes, how is this community organized? Through which concrete channels does it work toward legitimizing its currency? It would then be appropriate to examine such an organization more closely, to understand its functioning and specify the roles, statuses, and positions of the various actors composing it: miners, promoters, official site administrators, users, etc. This more empirical approach should be the subject of further research*” (Desmedt and Lakomski-Laguerre 2015, p. 15).

Although the ethnographic approach values the voices of the actors, this does not imply taking them at face value. Technicist liberal representations cannot claim unanimous support within heterogeneous communities. The varied and competing visions and narratives of the different *coiners* factions, sometimes incompatible and always evolving (Carter and Hasufly 2018), hardly conceal the fact that the theoretical and rhetorical ambitions to have detached money from politics and conflict stem from a paradoxical and counterfactual fetishism (Dodd 2017). The technical infrastructure and its components are fundamental to understanding cryptocurrencies (CM), which has attracted the attention of numerous scholars. However, limiting oneself to this is unsatisfactory and neglects many dimensions. Cryptocurrencies are subject to dual governance (De Filippi and Loveluck, 2016; Rolland and Slim, 2017). First, there is governance *by* the infrastructure, which frames interactions and resolves conflicts over the transaction history *via* the *consensus algorithm*\*, as with the “Nakamoto consensus” for Bitcoin (cf. Chap. I). However, this governance leads to another, more social and political: governance *over* the infrastructure. Creating money involves hybrid problems and solutions concerning the maintenance or modification of protocol codes, conditions of access and circulation of crypto-assets, legitimate uses, and so forth.

The problems and solutions that governance over infrastructure must address lie beyond the realm of the purely “technical,” situated instead within socially constructed spaces of debate and decision-making that are exogenous to the protocol itself. Debates and conflicts arise among actors with diverse positions, interests, and representations. Technical discussions often conceal political and normative considerations regarding what constitutes “good” money. Disintermediation is illusory (De Filippi & Loveluck, 2016; Dodd, 2017; Rolland & Slim, 2017; Hsieh, Vergne, and Wang, 2018). Certain sectors and groups of actors are crucial “at the helm” of governance *over* the infrastructure, which is neither centralized nor “headless” (Favier and Takkal Bataille, 2017), but rather polycentric. Some actors create, maintain, correct, and continuously modify the software codes essential to cryptocurrencies (CM). Others serve as financial intermediaries (gateway services* such as exchanges, third-party managed wallets, etc.), enabling UCNs to have both a sphere of use and an exchange value. Still others produce and disseminate information and knowledge. They provide measurement tools, opinions, and models (data processing *within the chain*; evaluation of transaction fees; price assessment models, etc.), as well as narratives that help forge shared beliefs, representations, and meanings. These investments in form [(Thévenot, 1986)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QzVoYm), *off-chain*, are essential for users and actors seeking to develop services around these technologies. The internalization of knowledge, conventions, habits, and even meaning is necessary to stabilize, render effective, and coordinate their commitments.

Cryptocurrencies, with their singular dual and polycentric governance, more or less formally define the role and status of *stakeholders*, raising several questions: how can we account for their institutionalization and the role of technical mediations? What arrangements, devices, and instruments are mobilized? Which representations, principles of justification, and arenas of debate structure this institutionalization? What are the foundations of conflicts, their modes of expression, and resolution? What types of rules and control mechanisms (if any) define the spaces and times of governance within these decentralized communities? Addressing these questions, without succumbing to the technicism prevalent in the literature, requires an adequate theoretical and conceptual framework. We will now present the framework adopted.

# B. The Governance of Cryptocurrencies Revealed Through Their Crises: A Monetary Institutionalism Supported by a Sociology of Science and Technology

This section and the following one will situate our type of research—its “design” ([Avenier and Thomas 2011, p. 1](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GrGAUk))—as well as the empirical materials employed[^18]. We have already addressed the context of our research. The explication of our epistemological and theoretical positioning will reconstruct the process that led us to develop our approach, combining monetary institutionalism and economic sociology, infused with science and technology studies (STS). This assemblage, which has become indispensable, requires that its internal and external coherence be made explicit and critically examined[^19], as well as our interest in crisis, a conceptual crossroads for these two bodies of literature.

## 1) Monetary Institutionalism to Characterize Cryptocurrencies as Money

We were not devoid of concepts and theories at the outset of this work. A Master’s program (M1 and M2) at Nanterre in the “Economics of Institutions” curriculum had trained us in multidisciplinarity and economic institutionalism. For us, economics followed the substantive definition[^20] of K. Polanyi rather than Robbins’s formal *economic sophistry*, which *“retrospectively projects representations derived from market economics onto all of human history”* (Chavance 2011, p. xi)[^21]. In the tradition of institutionalism since German historicism, we rejected the vision of the market as an *ahistorical* and *asocial* mechanism[^22]. We preferred induction and abduction to deductive inferences and axioms of standard economics: the observation of real facts and the analysis of relative contexts constituted our necessary starting point, leading to hypotheses confirmed or refuted by deductive inference. Understanding the object of cryptocurrencies (CM) required prior knowledge of money. Our exploratory readings for the first thesis, drawing extensively on the heterodox corpus [(Polanyi 1944; Polanyi 2011; Cartelier 1996; Aglietta and Orléan 2002; Orléan & Aglietta 1982; Simmel 2009)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?odSlHb), had prepared us. For cryptocurrencies, this corpus seemed from the outset more fruitful than the orthodox neoclassical corpus.

### Our Dissatisfaction with the Dominant Monetary Theory

An utilitarian approach combined with methodological individualism underpins the standard monetary theory. It mobilizes a representative agent who maximizes utility, the *homo œconomicus* characterized by instrumental, parametric, and solipsistic rationality (Cartelier 1996)[^23]. A substantive theory of utility/rarity value supports the theoretical edifice. Any currency, past or present, whether commodity-based or fiat, is conceived as possessing intrinsic value, which determines its utility and thus its demand. Following Aristotle, commodity-money is viewed through the functions of unit of account, medium of exchange, and store of value, but its “origin” is inferred from an instituted collective, convention, and authority. Marginalist economics rejects this holistic inference as the foundation of money’s existence. For Jevons (1876) and [Menger (1892)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QGgTuY), only the individual interest underlying exchanges should explain it, forging the “myth of barter” whereby money is merely a facilitator of exchanges that could occur without it [(Orléan 1992; Servet 2001, among others)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OQ1tVC). The approach is dichotomous, separating “real” phenomena (pertaining to market economy and relative prices) from “monetary” phenomena (nominal prices). The question of money’s role in price formation (real or nominal variations) is coupled with its influence on the real economy (Tutin 2009). This results in a representation where money is merely a neutral “veil” covering exchanges determined independently of it, accompanied by a formalization in terms of the quantity theory of money (cf. Box 1, Chap. II.1.1). The determination of price and money quantities must conform to a market logic. In an economy viewed as a set of interdependent markets, money is just the nth good [(Plihon 2008)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1qjSMI), whose profit incentive drives the market towards equilibrium. Exchange is reduced to contractual interindividual relations and constrained arbitrage, naturally producing a social optimum where individual plans are coherent and each agent’s utility is maximized. If the real and the nominal diverge, money—or more precisely those responsible for governing it—have failed in their stabilization role. Hence the question of its neutralization and the focus on regulating its issuance so as to ensure the provision of the “right” quantity of money.

Neoclassical economics accords a limited role to the monetary object, which serves to count but does not count (Zelizer 2005). Its “*existence*” remains “*the most serious challenge posed* [...] *to the theorist*” since “*the best-developed model of economics (the Arrow-Debreu version of Walrasian general equilibrium) cannot* [find] *the slightest place for it*” (F. Hahn [1982], cited in Aglietta 1988, p. 1). Its internal coherence encounters an external incoherence[^24]. The advances made within the *extended standard* economy[^25] raise more questions than they resolve[^26]. The *economistic sophistry* at work grounds the definition of money on its modern definition. Money and monetary media must perfectly fulfill the canonical monetary functions within a unitary and exclusive economic space. This occurs despite an ancient, but also recent, history (Polanyi 2011; Cohen 1998; Gilbert and Helleiner 1999; V.A. Zelizer 2005) in which money is never as unitary as it appears (Zelizer 1989): yesterday as today, no instrument “*can alone cover the full range of possible uses*” (Blanc 2009b). The difficulties of this corpus in grasping money are compounded when faced with more composite objects. The history of the monetary and financial field abounds with innovations, evolutionary variables that this corpus struggles to integrate[^27]. Monetary innovations constitute “*tests of explicitation*” that force academics and practitioners to clarify the definition of money, just as financial innovations challenge the definition of the market (Muniesa, 2017, p. 3). J. Law’s innovation—the paper credit monies detached from precious metals—elicited both enthusiasm for their innovative financing and horror from proponents of metallic monies, vigorously reopening the debate on the status and management of money by banks and central banks, as evidenced by the dispute between the *Banking School* and the *Currency School* (de Boyer des Roches and Rosales 2003; Kindelberger 2004, pp. 62 ff.; Tutin 2009, pp. 14–15). Similar theoretical problems arise for parallel currencies. Economic theory “*rarely takes into account*” the specific units of account used in payment and accounting alongside legal tender money, and “*when it does, it is with difficulty (currency substitutions) or in connection with very specific phenomena (hyperinflation) that only represent a portion of the phenomenon*” (Blanc 1998a, p. 6). Does the analysis of complementary currencies suffer from the same aporias?

Ultimately, the issues raised by these classical approaches remain unsatisfactory. Is money the object of an individual market choice? Does it merely simplify exchanges that would occur in its absence? Or does it refer to a socio-political framework—one that methodological individualism cannot grasp—a prerequisite for the market that it is incapable of establishing?

### Analyzing the Monetary Phenomenon Without Diminishing It: A Foundational Institutionalism

The preceding *aprioristic*, *ahistorical*, and *asocial* approaches neglect the transition from the individual to the collective(s). This relationship is central to money according to heterodox schools of thought, for whom money is a debt linked to radical uncertainty, of a social nature, and subject to authorities (Wray 2010, p. 37). Francophone monetary institutionalism (FMI) aligns with these perspectives.

For FMI, money is a fundamental social relation between economic agents. Within this corpus, there is no value preexisting exchanges: money is consubstantial with both parties. Contrary to the economic myth of barter, no society was ever “*completely devoid*” of money, which is “*a social fact*”: it is “*an institution, a faith*” under consideration, “*by no means a material and physical fact* [...] *its value is that of its purchasing power, and the measure of the confidence placed in it*” (Mauss 1914, pp. 3–4). Money as an institution is conceived here as a payment system, a set of fundamental rules for coordination. Its primary function is as a unit of account, a prerequisite measure without which exchanges would lack terms to be honored: it makes exchanges possible before facilitating them. Its metrological language of economic value structures the relations of debts and credits among actors in the division of labor (Cartelier 1996; Aglietta and Cartelier 1998). Money raises questions regarding the quality of claims and uncertainties (default risk) and their institutional remedies of trust, hierarchy, and authority (which can become power[^28]). Money is a group agreement: each participant sees in it the legitimate and consensual expression of value. Historically, it is linked to sovereign powers imposing their currency and demanding taxes in it. Authority and power are not to be conflated with their modern state forms. FMI adopts a nominalist approach that is not “*statist*,” unlike chartalist or neo-chartalist theories (Orléan 1998; Desmedt and Piégay 2007). The role of the state and administrations in the “*acceptance, issuance, and regulation of modern money*” is essential but insufficient, since the legitimacy of money must be experienced.

The IMF attempts to reconcile a transhistorical theoretical analysis of money with "*a historical approach* \[to\] *observable monetary practices*" (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, p. 5). First, universal theoretical invariants are defined, serving as heuristic fixed points. Subsequently, these generic invariants enable the comparative analysis of practical variations, both synchronic and diachronic. The monetary universals are: debt, trust, sovereignty. The issue of debt reveals various claims from different issuers, raising questions about the quality of signatures and defaults related to their repudiation. The fiduciary logic of the seal, at the core of money, contrasts with private titles and currencies governed by a contractual logic of signature. This interrogates a phenomenon of trust that articulates three dimensions: methodical, hierarchical, and ethical (Aglietta and Orléan 2002; Théret 2008). Since money constitutes a set of rules, it is necessary to examine the authorities that formulate, enforce, and sanction them. The link between money, sovereignty, power, and authority is essential but is not limited to the state and its formal rules, which raise reflections in terms of legitimacy. Every embodied sovereignty faces "*in one way or another* \[the\] *sovereignty of the users of money*" (Courbis, Froment, and Servet 1990, citing the concept of J. Rueff, p. 23). This theoretical framework has produced a substantial corpus of studies addressing various monetary phenomena, notably innovations and experiments.

This conceptual framework appeared to us as too abstract and theoretical to adequately address the governance of community currencies (CCs); therefore, we will draw upon the Institutional Analysis and Development / Social-Ecological Systems (IAD/SES)[^29] analytical and institutional development framework developed in the works of Ostrom and the Bloomington School. The latter have elaborated a conceptual apparatus that has the advantage of explicitly addressing governance. The Ostromian framework, developed around common-pool resources (CPRs)—whether physical or immaterial—is entirely oriented towards analyzing the diversity of governance modes exercised by “self-managed” and “polycentric” communities[^30]. Designed to decipher institutional diversity and its modes of evolution, this framework appears particularly suitable. Comprising a collection of disparate empirical analyses covering various institutional arrangements, it does not presuppose any hierarchy among (reified) types of coordination or property regimes. It constitutes a *non-normative* and *agnostic* framework, insofar as governance is conceived therein without presupposing the forms and functions it ought to assume. Governance refers to a contextually situated configuration of a set of rules and interactions—and the associated “bundles of rights.” The malleability of this toolbox corresponds to its systematic nature: regardless of the degree of decentralization of the governance system under study, it is possible to analyze the interaction framework and to define who holds which rights and under what terms. This is a rigorous, systematic institutionalist framework, open to empirical investigation and qualitative data (Chanteau and Labrousse 2013; Allaire 2013). Finally, it is a recognized framework.

To identify the common denominators of currency, the IMF draws upon interdisciplinarity. Thus, they introduced us to approaches from ethnography, economic sociology, and the sociology of science and technology (STS), which constitute the second theoretical pillar of this thesis.

## 2) Governance of Community Currencies: Sociological Perspectives

Our grounding in economic institutionalism led us to engage with significant monetary texts outside the economic field. The work *Philosophy of Money* (Simmel 2009) occupies a special place. This text initiated a programmatic renewal that triggered a disciplinary reconfiguration challenging the “*nested double division*” of the social sciences, which prevailed and within which money was situated[^31] (Polanyi 2011; Viviana A. Zelizer 1989; Dufy and Weber 2007). In France, this renewal concerning monetary issues includes researchers from the IMF (Alary et al. 2016) and, in other countries, authors positioned at the intersection of ethnography, anthropology, and economic sociology[^32].

### Anthropology, Ethnography, and Sociology of Money: The Empirics of Usage

[^29]: Elinor Ostrom and colleagues developed the IAD/SES framework to analyze institutional arrangements and social-ecological systems, focusing on collective action and governance.
[^30]: Polycentric governance refers to multiple overlapping centers of decision-making authority, often characteristic of self-managed communities.
[^31]: The “nested double division” refers to the traditional disciplinary boundaries in social sciences that separated economics from sociology and anthropology, marginalizing the study of money.
[^32]: This interdisciplinary approach combines ethnographic fieldwork, anthropological insights, and economic sociological analysis to study monetary phenomena in practice.

Despite their differences, these approaches renew the monetary question, critique orthodox economic views, and share a common methodology. Contrary to instrumental approaches, they start from practices and usages rather than from disembodied functions. Polanyi (1944, 2011) challenges the hypostatization of money as always perfectly fulfilling the canonical monetary functions: empirical evidence demonstrates that monetary usages were distributed across a multiplicity of objects and uses with differentiated functions. “*Primitive and archaic*” monetary systems differed from “modern” systems by their plural and compartmentalized economic spaces, employing “*Special Purpose Money*” (SPM) with clearly distinct functions: the separation between “treasure” and “basic commodity” reveals different monetary channels depending on the media circulating, the uses they fulfill, and the persons who have access to them (Polanyi 2011). Modern monies, following the “great transformation” of the industrial revolution, are said to have replaced these costly and inefficient SPMs with unitary and integrated “*All Purpose Money*” (APM), fully fulfilling the aforementioned monetary functions. The distinction between SPM and APM thus reveals, through the sophistic anachronism of APMs, the partial and situated character of the definition of money within the orthodox corpus, given the historical dominance of SPM systems. Zelizer (1989), a pioneer of this renewal, goes further. She criticizes Polanyi’s APM as a reifying hypostasis of modern monies, which are neither “all-purpose” nor perfectly homogeneous.

Zelizer (1989) opposes a systematic approach to the social construction of money to hegemonic visions of “market monies”[^33]. This approach enables the conceptualization of monetary alterity and “imperfections” through various conversions and *markings*, alterity understood as more or less subjective differentiations that money objectively undergoes through its usages[^34] (Zelizer 1989; Zelizer 2005; Blanc 2009). This renewed approach no longer opposes SPM to APM, since the modern monetary system, though apparently unitary, is shaped by its users beyond the rules and monetary forms instituted by sovereign power.

[^33]: See Zelizer, Viviana A. (1989). *The Social Meaning of Money*. Basic Books.
[^34]: For a detailed discussion of monetary markings and conversions, see Zelizer (2005) and Blanc (2009).

These approaches share the perspective of conceiving money as the product of three elements: accounting systems that establish value and equivalences (various rules and mechanisms such as accounting and management, taxation, etc.), monetary instruments (different objects), and the social relations in which they are used and transformed. There is no money without its uses as such. Any monetary analysis must begin with uses, their material context, and their indigenous meanings, since it is impossible to dissociate what actors do from their intentions and identities (Zelizer 1989; Blanc 2009). Attached to these uses are cognitive frameworks, as well as the values and representations of co-exchangers, groups, and, more broadly, the society under consideration, which it is crucial to analyze (Dufy and Weber 2007). These frameworks are constituted by a language and qualification procedures, whose rules and evolutions must be carefully observed. For Zelizer and the IMF, money is a social signifier that attests to the foundations of a political community that it helps to construct. No research can dispense with an analysis of the uses, representations, and institutional arrangements that interlock to produce money. However, to understand the arrangements and mechanisms of digital currencies (DCs) and to respond to the technicist claims that accompany them, a final conceptual string has been added to our theoretical bow.

### Beyond the “Black Boxes”: DCs as “Seamless” Infrastructures

Despite their apparent opposition, critics and proponents of cryptocurrencies share a reifying vision of money that borders on fetishism. The claims of the former and the reductionism of the latter rest on a technicist bias that regards cryptocurrencies as “black boxes,” in Latour’s sense: the illusion of a purely autonomous and stabilized technique, maintaining itself “*by itself as a discrete and determined reality, independent of any social or political standpoint*” (Quéré 1989, p. 102). Science and Technology Studies (STS) emerged in opposition to this “*technologism*”: technology and society are “*two fabricated objects*” (Latour 2006, p. 4) whose separation into “*two worlds*,” even conceptually, is sterile [(Akrich, 1989, pp. 31–32)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4b1b1h). We must abandon this arbitrary dichotomy, as well as many categories “*that lead us to describe a world already made*” (Callon 2006, p. 3): ends and means (Latour 2000), *on-chain* and *off-chain*... It is impossible to endorse the idea of a technology governed solely by its own neutral rationality. To overcome this “*technologism*,” as well as to avoid the pitfalls of the opposing vision of “sociologism”[^35], technology is conceived as “*a seamless fabric*,” a socio-technical object (Latour 2006; Akrich 1989). Our *e*-money creators, like Edison, navigate “*in troubled waters between the social, the technical, the economic, etc., negotiating the very contents of their innovations with the actors they seek to enroll, incorporating the results of various trials of all kinds that they impose on themselves*” (Akrich 1989, citing Hughes, p. 32). The metaphor of the absence of seams emphasizes that a socio-technical object always intertwines, from its inception through the threads of its developments, problematizations, argumentative grammars, and heterogeneous references that are not purely technical.

As a composite entity, a socio-technical object incorporates within its design a scenario that reflects a “*description of the social world in which*” it is intended to operate. The innovator’s designs embody their *intentions*, hence an impossible neutrality that has two implications: the resources and constraints embedded in technical devices influence the social world, and actors within the social world, in turn, may divert these devices from the original scenario. Technical devices and arrangements are powerful instruments for partitioning the physical and social world; they act, cause action, and their configurations positively assign roles to certain actors—human or non-human—while relegating others, as well as determining (or prohibiting) certain modes of relations (*Ibid.*; Akrich 2010). Although they “*constitute active elements in organizing relations among humans and between humans and their environment*,” nothing guarantees their proper functioning, nor that the “*projected user*” embedded in the original intentions corresponds to the “*actual user*”: every technical object encounters gaps “*between design and use, from which one can highlight the mechanisms of mutual adjustment between the technical object and its environment*” (Akrich, 2010, pp. 205 and 208). In our field, these deviations from the scenario take the form of vulnerabilities, bugs, cyberattacks, or additions and evolutions of the socio-technical devices at the core of the blockchain community (BC). These situations—whether they involve uses governed by codes that diverge from their expected outcomes or an explicit demand from certain actors to evolve the scenario (e.g., optimization or radical modification of the protocol)—illustrate the always (re)negotiated and composite nature of a BC. In such cases, technology, incapable of self-correction, requires the work of organized human allies. Governance *over* the infrastructure reemerges, where a “conflict/debate” is not only technical but also socio-economic [(De Filippi and Loveluck, 2016; Rolland and Slim, 2017)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FdGlFa). Protocol evolutions are exemplary. Evolving a BC means redefining means and ends. This mobilizes actors, their perceptions of desirable ends and means, and induces trade-offs that reveal situated interests, constraints, and opportunities.

More than mere socio-technical objects, cryptocurrencies correspond to the definition and properties of an infrastructure—whether physical or digital[^36]—according to Science and Technology Studies (STS) and *Infrastructural Studies* (Star 1999; Bowker 1996; Edwards et al. 2009, cf. Chap. I). [De Filippi and Loveluck (2016)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?u9lygj) and Kavanagh and Miscione (2017) have emphasized this point with regard to Bitcoin. An infrastructure is relational and ecological, appearing “*as a relational property, rather than as a thing devoid of use*” (Star, 1999, p. 377). Inscribed “*within a balance between action, tools, and the built environment*,” an infrastructure only truly becomes such “*in relation to the organized practice*” of disparate actors. Due to its scale, different perspectives on it coexist depending on the positions of the actors and groups discussing it. Their analysis requires an “*infrastructural inversion*,” which foregrounds the underlying elements of the practices related to it and that give it substance (Bowker, 1994, cited in Star, 1999, p. 380). Within this corpus, an infrastructure is not constructed but rather emerges, grows, and develops through processes and stages (such as the creation of standards and gateways), thanks to the work of a myriad of human actors (Edwards et al. 2009). It is therefore crucial to focus on the routine activities indispensable to their functioning, as well as on the hands that perform them. As with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) or economic sociology, it is necessary to analyze the complex interplay of representations, material structures, and relational networks in order to understand an infrastructure.

It is significant that the studies most closely related to our object and research questions come from scholars situated within this field (Mallard, Méadel, and Musiani 2014; Karlstrøm 2014; De Filippi and Loveluck 2016; DuPont 2018; Musiani, Mallard, and Méadel 2018). These works highlight the essential roles of diverse technical arrangements, at the heart of which lie forms of cooperation and the construction of shared values. Thus, rather than starting from a fixed, *a priori* and reifying definition of governance (with its attendant formal prescriptions), our approach apprehends it more positively as a set of dynamic, indeterminate, and informal processes, referring to singular situations (our crisis field sites) and the interactions that unfold therein. Governance is conceived here as a collection of rules, logics, frameworks, and structures of collective action, beyond their merely formal institutional aspects.

## 3) The Governance of Bitcoin and Ethereum Revealed through Their Crises

To address the governance of cryptocurrencies, we have chosen to examine crisis phenomena. Although their forms and stakes vary, crises are privileged objects of study, both for the IMF, which uses them to study money, and for STS, where a specific corpus has developed.

### The Heuristic and Hermeneutic Functions of “Crises”

In the monetary field, crises are often regarded as privileged moments of study during which money is “unveiled,” revealing its historical, social, and political dimensions (Théret, 2007). Crises interrupt the *“routine functioning of money,”* compelling observers to question past stabilities. They disclose the social and political substratum of the monetary systems they affect, in both their material and ideological dimensions (*Ibid.*, Simmel 2009). The functions, instruments, uses, and actors of money appear more fragmented and dissociated in these moments.

In STS as well, crisis—and technological controversies more broadly—occupies a prominent place. As a “*point of fusion where technology takes shape*,” crises reveal the “*numerous negotiations that precede and delimit technical choices*”: the socio-technical object, however reified it may be, is partially unveiled (Callon 2006, p. 3). Two properties commonly attributed to infrastructure pertain here: an infrastructure “*becomes visible during breakdowns*” and it is “*fixed by modular increments, not all at once or globally*” (Star 1999, p. 382). Being large-scale, layered, and complex, infrastructure signifies different things locally and does not evolve top-down, since any change takes time and requires negotiations with all involved actors (*Ibid.*). To denaturalize crisis demands “*abandoning the search for causes and relinquishing the distinction between the routine state of the world and the critical phenomenon*” (Aguiton, Cabane, and Cornilleau 2019, p. 11). Arbitrary categories opposing routine to dysfunction, bug to attack, normal to exceptional are thus interrogated. Crisis reveals a hiatus between the designer’s projected scenario and the one actually enacted through use. It informs us about the state of the social world considered “*normal*” and that which is not. Crisis is not given but manufactured. The STS perspective compels us to study “*the politics of crisis*” and “*its governance*,” from end to end—from the moment of crisis onset to the restoration of order—in order to expose the mechanisms, stakes, and actors involved in this enterprise (*Ibid.*). Within crisis, the socio-technical object demonstrates that it is always in the process of becoming and unbecoming, contingent upon the strategies of the actors involved. It is therefore necessary to interrogate the conditions of its “onset,” “normalization,” “aggravation,” or “containment,” up to its “resolution.” At the heart of crisis governance are human actors for whom crisis is nothing exceptional, since they work with it daily. They produce diagnoses of it and take control through *ad hoc* tools, devices, and procedures that they have helped implement. Crisis lays bare the arrangements in place aimed at anticipating it, preventing it, or, if it occurs, containing and correcting it. Crisis governance represents “*a site of demarcation between the visible and the invisible*” (*Ibid.*, p. 18). Therein is revealed the irreducible normativity of labels, stakeholder statuses (victims, responsible parties, etc.), definitions of tolerability, and degrees of severity, priority, and urgency.

### On the Scope of Our Fields: Two Distinct Crises Affecting Bitcoin and Ethereum

The confirmation that cryptocurrencies (CM) function as money implies another hypothesis: their monetary singularity lies in the presence (and not the absence) of polycentric governance (cf. Chapter II). This study will analyze this specificity through the examination of two crises, one concerning Bitcoin, the other Ethereum. Let us consider the choices that led us to this focus.

Selecting a field of study is never easy, and in this domain, one difficulty stemmed from its vastness. When we first encountered cryptocurrencies, there were already hundreds, if not thousands, of them, and they have continued to evolve both extensively[^37] and intensively (within a single cryptocurrency, technologies and usages have expanded). We have become aware of and/or attended numerous events, more or less significant, that have affected the cryptocurrency ecosystem over these years, and it is clear that no cryptocurrency is ever fixed in its form or content. Negotiated, cryptocurrencies undergo technical, economic, institutional, and relational evolutions. From this arose practical constraints, already highlighted: we faced considerable masses of data and potentially processable documents (Berry, 2012). The object is also geographically vast. These monetary systems imply a community fragmentation, a multiplicity of arrangements that are more or less local/global, depending on the relational networks of their members. We therefore narrowed our field following an exploratory phase (cf. section C below), specifically choosing Bitcoin and Ethereum, the two most capitalized cryptocurrencies, as well as the most dynamic in terms of development and communities. They are, in our view, ideal-typical of what the concept of cryptocurrency encompasses: sharing the expected common characteristics of a cryptocurrency—formal openness to “all,” *censorship resistance*, multipartite consensus based on *PoW*[^38], etc.—they differ in terms of their architecture and governance, fueling controversies within their communities. To further restrict our field, we chose to analyze, for each, a particular moment of “crisis”: the CVE 2018 #17144 bug for Bitcoin and the crisis triggered by the “The DAO” attack for Ethereum (cf. Chapter III).

[^37]: See the extensive evolution in the number and diversity of cryptocurrencies over time.  
[^38]: Proof of Work (PoW) consensus mechanism.

These two crises will prove equally emblematic of two distinct types of crises and two differentiated forms of governance. The first type is what we shall call a *“vulnerability crisis”*, and the other an *“evolution crisis.”* Each type is defined relative to the situation conceived as normal: what is permitted by the protocol codes—the “letter of the code”—is consistent with what is expected and considered legitimate by social consensus—the “spirit of the code.” However, two forms of disjunction may arise between the letter and the spirit of the code. This gives rise to *vulnerability crises*, linked to a flaw arising in a situation where the codes produce outcomes that, for the actors involved, are in explicit contradiction with what is expected. For example, if the letter of the code allows the same UTXOs (Unspent Transaction Outputs) to be spent twice (the explicit case of *double spending*\*) and thereby creates bitcoins outside the canonical minting rules, the contradiction with social consensus is glaring. This is what occurred, at least theoretically, in the Bitcoin CVE 2018 #17144 crisis. *Evolution crises*, by contrast, reveal themselves as different in their processes of crisis emergence and restoration of order. They correspond to a situation where the code does not permit what actors implicitly wish it to do. Although it performs as expected, this code may be contested by some who desire its evolution—for example, by requesting a modification of the Ethereum protocol to freeze the funds of an address following a theft. This was the issue at stake in the “The DAO” crisis. To these two types of crises correspond two particular and mutually exclusive forms of governance presiding over their resolution: a form we term *“behind closed doors”*, operative in our Bitcoin fieldwork, which contrasts with another *“public and open”* form, deployed in our Ethereum fieldwork. Crises that are *“behind closed doors”* or *“public and open”* reveal the two opposing faces of the same coin: the governance of crypto-monies (CM). Each face refers to the establishment of differentiated diagnoses and solutions, pertaining to procedural and relational frameworks specific to each (applicable rules and procedures, actors and mechanisms involved).

# C. An Ethnographic Approach for a Multi-Level Field Investigation

We have presented the context in which our research object was constructed, as well as its epistemological and theoretical framework. Let us now specify the empirical materials of this thesis. Since every investigation is “*dependent on the conditions of entry into the field*,” which are always singular and relate to the “*characteristics of the field and the respondents, those of the researcher, and the interactions established between them*” (Bué 2010, p. 78), we will first elucidate the context in which this material was collected. Our research, based on a qualitative approach and data, is idiographic. This choice became increasingly affirmed as our work progressed. Faced with an emerging field, it was pertinent to employ an approach validated in contexts of discovery [(Avenier and Thomas 2011)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ni2Y6E). Our methodological reflections intertwined our research object, our fields of inquiry, our research perspectives, as well as the resulting constraints and questions.

## Investigating the Governance of CM: Constraints and Stakes

Clarifying our materials requires, beforehand, “*to state the particular context of* [our] *field and to explain, in connection with its specificity, the strategies and methods adopted to conduct the investigation*” [(Pastinelli 2011, p. 38)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qTAKxO). Initially, as a complete outsider to computer science, gaining access proved complex. Beyond having to delineate our object, it was simultaneously necessary to identify the actors involved in order to establish contact. To the apprehension of strangeness was added the fear related to the difficulties we anticipated encountering during initial contacts[^39].

### Methodological Stakes

The first challenge lies in defining the object of cryptocurrency markets (CM) as an infrastructure in the sense of Star (1999). Its broad scope refers to a plurality of uses and disparate practices. The infrastructural turn demands “*going backstage*” in order to “*bring to the surface the invisible work*” that takes place there and all the elements related to it (*Ibid.*, p. 385). Yet, one must first manage to situate, even if only roughly, the backstage scene. While at its inception, any research does not strictly speaking have an object, choosing to “*study the unstudied*” and “*boring things*” (Star 1999, p. 382) in a context unfamiliar to us made the task even more arduous. Grasping indigenous meanings and the cognitive frameworks of the protagonists required mastery of a specific language (hence the necessity to compile a glossary, see *Glossary*). This jargon, a mixture of computer engineering, cryptography, and finance language, *destabilized* preexisting definitions and categories: the *vocabulary of money* was *“transformed by that of machines”* (free adaptation of Muniesa 2017, p. 1). Added to this was the question, inherent to infrastructures, of moving “*to a scale larger than the spaces traditionally studied by ethnography*,” since “*groups are geographically and temporally distributed, and may involve hundreds of people and terminals*,” imposing “*the collection and processing of a large amount of data (transaction logs, etc.) in order to understand the reciprocal interplay of online and offline actions*” (Star 1999, p. 382). CMs owe their material reality both to the Internet and to their *stakeholders*\*, whose identities and statuses are not necessarily present. Their *topos* evolves dynamically, following technological and community boundaries rather than geographical ones. Like physical infrastructures that did not emerge all at once, CMs emerge “*first at smaller scales of time, space, and service, as second-order systems built on and around the Internet and other existing information frameworks*” (Edwards et al. 2009, p. 366). Beyond *on-chain* transactional relations, human and non-human actors coexist in arenas of debate and discussion that unfold *off-chain*, both in “cyberspace” and in the “real world.” The former refers to numerous communication channels, more or less public, across different types of platforms (social networks, *software forges\**, IRC channels[^40], mailing lists, etc.). In the real world, following dispersed communities and through the work of certain actors (individuals or organizations) who organize meetings, conferences, presentations, etc., again more or less open and targeting different types of audiences (developers, investors, etc.).

The methodological stakes are underpinned by research perspectives, and the electronic space does not hold the same status depending on the viewpoint of the researcher producing it (Pastinelli 2011, p. 40). For the interactions mediated by open distributed ledger protocols* that we study, the online communities (OCs) indeed encompassed “*practices or phenomena that exist only online, that were born from electronic communications*” [(Ibid.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Dsfvlf). Although this immediately imposed cyberspace as the field of investigation, we did not limit ourselves to conducting only a “netnography”[^41]. Of course, to observe the communities of *coiners* and the relationships woven therein, their modalities and meanings, the Internet became for us a *field site*—a *place, a space, or a tool* (Ibid.)—privileged for inquiry. OCs certainly involve online relationships that can only be observed where they exist, but to what extent was observing this visible facet sufficient? From an infrastructural logic, the digital space is not autonomous; simultaneously “virtual” and “real,” its material reality synchronically rests upon practices related to it. Delimiting the context in which practices and discourses gain meaning leads to questioning how *coiners* extend and articulate their online and offline lives. More than an ethnography of the virtual, it was necessary to “*put an end to*” it, to break away from the “*reification of an electronic space* [which] *abstracts from the wide diversity of practices, contexts, and phenomena involved* [...] *in the process of taking shape and* [always] *subject to transformation*” (Ibid., p. 39).*  

### The Investigator–Investigated Relationship

It is time to revisit *the*, or rather *the multiple* (Bué 2010), investigator-investigated relationships developed during our fieldwork. Ethnographic research leads the researcher, just as much as the subjects, to become actively involved. This mutual involvement partly determines the form and content of the relationships and influences the results the researcher derives from them (Duclos 2014). Based solely on its narrative construction, the governance of Bitcoin and Ethereum should have little in common with that of the local coalition of political parties analyzed by Bué (2010); yet, our field was likewise “*multiplied into several social spaces, simultaneously nested, interdependent, and competing*,” and our investigation resembled “*a dynamic among social actors with distinct expectations and interests*” of which we were a part: situated “*at the intersection of the cleavages, interdependencies, and other competitions that characterize the studied milieu*” (pp. 77–78). Consequently, “*self-presentation or distance from the investigated, the management of the plurality of investigative relationships*” were particularly salient challenges for us (*Ibid.*, p. 78). This was true both upon our initial entry into the field and subsequently, as our research unfolded. Our field experience—indeed, *our* multiple fields—witnessed our investigator-investigated relationships evolve dynamically, becoming plural and ambivalent. The original forms of reciprocal mistrust, even distrust, gradually gave way, through encounters and exchanges, to more “egalitarian” or “symmetrical” relationships, and for a small number of these, to more affective ones.

On our side, the initial distrust can be explained by our point of entry: documentary research *via* grey literature and our initial form of netnography. In 2015, the resources available on Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies were overwhelmingly Anglo-Saxon, and even predominantly American. To the primary monetary ethos evident in their design, which was in direct opposition to ours, was added an ideological layer situated at the “*American far right*” permeating many resources (Golumbia 2015, pp. 119–120). The repulsion we felt toward this “*cyber-libertarianism*,” mixing “*anti-government*” and “*anti-central bank and/or commercial bank*” rhetoric, was gradually overcome through our rapprochement with French actors. This allowed us to build a certain distance during our initial approaches to actors. In light of our first contacts, this proved judicious: it was erroneous “*to homogenize Bitcoin in political terms* [as Golumbia does], because ‘*this is an unnecessarily one-sided perspective*’” (Dodd 2017, pp. 6–7) that overlooks the essential element: the heterogeneous values of coiner communities and the conflicts this induces. We were not alone in harboring *a priori* judgments: our status as doctoral students in the humanities placed us among academics, and thereby on the opposing side. We experienced firsthand the discord existing between monetary experts and *coiners* (cf. Chap. II). Although initially we bore the brunt of this[^42], once prior suspicions were dispelled, our presence and status evolved positively.

The most commonly invoked reproach by *coiners* against their critics seemed understandable to us: it indeed refers to the “*constitutive asymmetry of the investigator-investigated relationship, which tends to make the former the subject of the research and the latter its object* [...] : *who were we* [...] *to claim to be those who, first, would know how to ask the right questions*?” (Duclos 2014). Faced with an emerging field articulating disparate objects belonging to specialized disciplines far removed from our initial competencies, adopting the “*overarching gaze of the external observer*” (*Ibid.*) was scientifically problematic for us. In order “*that actors not be amputated of a part of themselves*,” it was necessary to overcome this “*asymmetry*,” this “*privilege*” “*granted to the social sciences*” (Callon 1986, pp. 171–172) and to avoid its pitfalls[^43]. Moreover, we also had to dispel a pervasive suspicion of incompetence—both among our actors and within ourselves—when numerous widely publicized harsh critiques were disqualified by their lack of solid empirical knowledge and technical mastery. Although the following excerpts address specific authors—who are far from the most blameworthy—their content is symptomatic of the reproaches voiced: one would hear “*talking about people like Primavera De Filippi or Emin, I don’t know who, the guy from Cornell* [Emin Gün Sirer, ed.], *and I find that they say quite a lot of nonsense actually* [...] *they express opinions on the characteristics of networks, their decentralization and all that, which are unfortunately false.* [...] *they mix everything up* [...] *without having the fundamentals to talk about it*.” [P. Noizat, Interview no. 24]

Faced with the *“technical governance”* of cryptocurrencies, researchers in the humanities found themselves “*in the domain of engineers*,” raising a methodological problem to be approached with humility[^44]: “*like a clockwork mechanism* [which is] *appreciable only if you are a craftsman* [...] *One must be humble.* [To be able] *to speak about technology, one really has to undertake the process of coding.* [...] *If you take Claude Lévi-Strauss,* [...] *he was the first to emphasize the importance of primitive languages to understand peoples. I think it is the same for computers: if you do not learn their language, you are a visitor, you know. You are not scientific in your approach at all.*” [P. Noizat, Interview no. 24]

Thus, our “interview” with Mr. Falke [Non-Interview no. 27], which he had asked us to conduct on a public forum, was cut short. Our questionnaire did not speak the right language, went “against almost” all the tacit conventions of the forum, where we had naively stopped at our interpretation of the formal rules of use. While most of our questions seemed to concern the Bitcoin client and “applicable events,” our questionnaire was removed less than 10 minutes after publication for being “*off-topic*” and “*not adapted to the format*” of a forum (personal questions, for example) “*built around obtaining answers to objective technical questions*,” aiming to discourage those “*subjective or opinion-based, or that can only be answered by specific persons*” (*Ibid.*, see appendix no. IV.4). Whereas our work shows that governance *over* the infrastructure precisely relies on specific, non-interchangeable individuals in practice. What could not be explained by incompetence could be explained by malice, for example when critics were accused of relaying “*propaganda, even* [*disinformation*]” [P. Noizat, Interview no. 24], referring to the ideological conflict opposing them to traditional monetary and financial systems and their organized interests. Similarly, the legal qualification of the actions of our protagonists was surrounded by uncertainty, which weighed on the freedom of their speech: the case of “The DAO,” with its reputational stakes, violent conflicts, and threats of litigation, is exemplary in this regard. Our initial attempts at contact showed that obtaining interviews would not be an easy task: many actors contacted never responded, while others simply refused, citing apprehension about the risks involved (we think of the CEO of Slock.it and some of its employees who took part in the rescue operations, for example).

If we did not attain the coding competencies expected by the interviewee cited above, our initial apprehensions instilled in us a meticulous desire to master the subject. It was only after a prolonged period of maturation, within the asynchronous comfort of passive observation of social networks, blog posts, and forums, that we began our participant observations—and “humbly” at first. This time was necessary because, although the novelty of the beginning made it easy to identify salient elements, it was still essential to develop the appropriate vocabulary, distinguish the important from the anecdotal, and maintain a critical distance from the indigenous language (Amoto 2008). Refining our understanding of these objects as referential frameworks for their community, we gradually found *our place* within the community: beyond our increasingly recurrent presence at events, the establishment and continuation at EHESS of one of the first seminars dedicated to community managers (CM), as well as our public interventions (whether media-related or not)[^45], ultimately situated us within the field. In its second year, the seminar welcomed community actors, including “Bitcoin.fr,” a specialized Francophone media outlet that ensured publicity and occasionally video broadcasts[^46]. Gradually, both our knowledge of the subject and our problematizations evolved, and in doing so, so did our relationships within the community. Some began to recognize our “technical” knowledge and to envy our mastery of academic codes and references—something they sometimes lacked when facing *clients* or *institutional actors*. The investigator/investigated relationships established during initial contacts transformed into professional, even friendly, relationships[^47]. With the inner circle, our exchanges became more informal, the topics addressed less limited, and, as with Duclos (2014, p. 10), “*this was our way of achieving a dialogue where, without ever merging, we were able to come closer; where, without the ethnographer ceasing to have the last word, his voice* [could] *articulate with those of the people encountered to jointly trace the path of knowledge*.” It was this progression—in knowledge and relationships—that removed our status as “visitors,” particularly regarding governance and crises in Bitcoin and Ethereum. This relative position taken within the field, facilitated by the mediation of a first circle, eased contact with more distant actors[^48]. From then on, in these domains, we participated in indigenous controversies (though were they still indigenous?), without hesitation—something we had refrained from doing initially—sometimes even finding the support of influential actors[^49]. As critical as our positions were, they were considered “more nuanced” and less “detached from reality.” We thus became—somewhat despite ourselves—one of the “*rare economist friends*” with whom, despite known oppositions, it was *pleasant* to discuss[^50]. To the internal guarantees of reputation was added an external guarantee, namely a particular vigilance regarding anonymity and confidentiality issues. Preserving the anonymity of our informants was, from the outset and according to the referential frameworks of this field, an important concern. This also relates to a researcher’s ethics, which is now regulated (Cefai 2009). In ethnography, “*confidentiality and anonymity are* (...) *two sides of the same problem, that of guaranteeing to informants a dissociation between their speech—sometimes also their actions—and their identity, either with respect to those who know them, other informants or close contacts (confidentiality), or with respect to the anonymous mass of potential readers (anonymity)* [and] *this interweaving of anonymity and confidentiality concerns all researchers working in environments of inter-knowledge*” (Zolesio 2011, p. 179, citing Béliard and Eideliman). Thus, the possibility of interviews under strict anonymity was always offered *a priori*, while actors agreeing to respond under their own names retained the option to require that certain statements be kept “*off the record*,” hence the creation of a fictitious actor [“SuperAnon”], which allowed us to incorporate these materials while protecting our sources (cf. Appendix IV.4).

Ultimately, however singular this field may be, it shares similarities with any other. A *software forge*\* is not the board of directors of a central bank, just as “*the forum is not a municipal council*,” yet the principles of inquiry remain unchanged, and no method is rendered obsolete: it is always a matter of analyzing content and relationships, whether they exist in the “virtual” or the “real” (Pastinelli 2011, p. 37). The specificities of the phenomenon we observe do not in any way compel us to abandon the tools available to anthropology or ethnography, whose contributions to the study of these phenomena lie less in the “specificity” of their method than in “*the nature of the questions researchers ask and the framework within which these questions are posed*” (*Ibid.*, p. 47). It was necessary to adhere to Maussian triple questioning of *“who are these people, what do they do, and what do they think about it”* while reflecting *“on the relationship to the technique or the device itself, but always by putting into perspective what *[one]* can observe online with the way the same ties manifest and are experienced in other contexts, since it is these ties that are the starting point and the core of the research and not the technical device*” (*Ibid*., pp. 45 and 47–48). The particularities of these new kinds of infrastructures lead our work to take the form of “*a combination of historical and literary analysis, traditional tools such as interviews and observations, systems analysis, and usability studies*” (Star 1999, p. 382). This combination of heterogeneous devices remains to be presented.

### The “Online” Aspect of Community Managers: From Documentary Research to the Leap into “Participant Immersion”

#### “Digging Deep Down the Rabbit Hole”[^51]: Documentary Research as an Essential Source for Understanding This Field

Exploratory research was initially conducted through documentary research mobilizing heterogeneous sources and materials: various types of literature (academic, grey, and indigenous), as well as different types of documents and data accessible online (on-chain data, forum debates, exchanges on software repositories related to optimizations, etc.).

At the level of academic literature, although studies originate from various disciplines, we prioritized texts situated within economics and sociology, without excluding other disciplines (for the counterpoints they provide). Most of the works in this corpus have already been mentioned previously. This was supplemented by grey literature, reports, notes, or position papers published by institutions such as central banks or government agencies. Within one category or another, we included hybrid expert statements (“Nobel Prize” economists, ministers, central bank governors, whose contributions do not directly pertain to their own disciplinary field but rather to the media sphere, such as articles and opinion pieces in newspapers—this status being indicated by the absence of pagination, which is reserved for citations drawn from formal works; see writing precautions and preliminary information).

Upon entering the field, while the first two corpora were sparse, the corpus constituted by the literature and indigenous materials was abundant. A large portion of the collected material is drawn from this corpus, which comprises diverse objects originating from various actors. It derives from sources hosted on the Internet, often produced by the actors themselves: transactional registers, blog posts, forum discussions, media of varying degrees of public reach, etc. From these, we extracted both qualitative and quantitative data. The qualitative data are distinguished according to the actors who provide them (developers, journalists, blog posts by more or less recognized personalities, etc.), the information conveyed (primary or secondary sources, level of technicality, language, etc.), the targeted audience, as well as the channels/networks mobilized in their publication. Some materials are technical in nature and correspond directly to traces of discussions and technical proposals made by developers *via* the repository of their *software forge*, which is the tool for the public production of software code. These discussions aim at evaluating the problems posed, producing patches (know-how), but also disseminating and translating this information for the entire community (making known). Others correspond to materials with a more general purpose and a broader audience, such as online specialized journal articles, blog posts, vulnerability disclosure reports disseminated by the Bitcoin Core team, and community exchanges on various social networks. Three sets of quantitative data were constituted: one relating to the ecosystem as a whole (Appendix I) and two others dedicated to Bitcoin (Appendix II) and Ethereum (Appendix III). These data, serving the three chapters of the thesis, are referred to in the Appendices to avoid redundancy and overload, and to facilitate access (the glossary and appendices are designed as a detachable booklet for this purpose).

Faced with this volume of information, it was necessary to perform a selection so as not to be *drowned under the mass* (Berry 2012, p. 53). Our capacity for access was a straightforward criterion, and our search for resources and data was limited to those accessible to us. The role of documentary materials will evolve, but they will remain essential throughout our investigation: from a primary vector for the general apprehension of our object, they transform into a vector of continuous information and more focused research. These documentary inquiries allowed us to better understand Bitcoin and Ethereum theoretically and to envisage confronting them more practically.

#### Approaching Non-Human Actors: Numerous and “Fruitful” Participant Immersions

This initial phase of documentary research prompted us to delve deeper. Following an initial encounter with cryptocurrencies in 2015, we began, together with a member of the CEMI laboratory (Assen Slim), drafting a preparatory paper. Our understanding of our object of study was then based solely on our preliminary readings and our knowledge of monetary theory. This was pointed out by a discussant during the presentation of the aforementioned paper, who questioned our potential use of Bitcoin. Our negative response revealed a significant issue: we were commenting on a monetary object with an innovative and sophisticated architecture without having made the practical effort to experiment with its use. That very evening, we empirically engaged with this new medium, inaugurating a new era of monetary socialization.

The entry point of our immersive experiences (a non-exhaustive list of our participatory immersions can be found in Appendix IV.1) was the acquisition and use of our first BTC UCNs. Given that we were still reluctant to purchase bitcoin with euros *via* an exchange platform, these initial UCNs were earned through our “work,” *via* faucet websites, in exchange for completing micro-tasks (viewing advertisements, playing various games, etc.). Interested in the subject of Bitcoin, we were immersed, *via* these third-party services, in a universe composed of a plurality of cryptocurrencies and socio-technical arrangements enabling both access to and use of them. As this tedious work allowed us to build a first, more than modest, wallet, our initial reluctance faded. Going further into the experience led us to adopt the roles of *trader*, *investor*, and *hasher*, and to acquire cryptocurrencies against euros *via* various gateway services. Whether offering “rental” of dedicated computing power enabling the *processing of transactions* (and the receipt of related rewards) or facilitating *trading* on exchange platforms, these services involved *off-chain*\* actions, outside the formal boundaries of the *distributed ledger protocol\**. The growing importance of these financial intermediaries, known as *gateways\*,* quasi-mandatory passageways for any user (both entering and exiting), became practically evident. Navigating this procedural multiplicity and its complexities led us to disparate activities and practices. We had to turn to specific sites—both of production and discussion—of these new knowledges and internalize their norms and values. On this occasion, we joined social networks for the first time (Twitter, Reddit, etc.) and discovered the particular modes of communication prevailing there. We were able to witness, and even take part in, numerous events and controversies: the *scaling debate* on Bitcoin unfolded noisily before our eyes in 2017; the deployment of Ethereum, which we had joined in its early days; the attack on the “The DAO” project and the resounding *hard fork* it led to—taken as a case study in this thesis—while we ourselves had participated in its record-breaking fundraising campaign; the first NFTs on Bitcoin (participation in the *Rare Pepe Card* community); the exuberant phenomenon of public sales known as *Initial Coin Offerings* (ICOs), which peaked in 2017 and attracted the attention of investors, media, and regulators alike; the emergence of DEFI on Ethereum, including “farming,” “airdrops,” and so forth. From the outset, it was inconceivable for us that a monetary system, however innovative, could be devoid of governance spaces, trust, and thus structures of power and conflict; our practical experiences tended to confirm these intuitions.

Methodologically, if it is the “*duplicity*” of our fields—both online and offline—that led us to conduct “*participant immersions*” (Amato, 2008), this approach carries ethical implications (disclosure policies regarding holdings, conflicts of interest, etc.) that remain contentious (DuPont, 2021). For some, not owning any crypto-assets (UCN) guarantees objectivity[^52], and discussing this field necessarily implies an external standpoint. It is true that indirect access to practical knowledge was possible through participant observation. Limiting ourselves to accompanying and dialoguing with users might have seemed sufficient. However, as others[^53] have argued, it appeared necessary for us to know our field practically, while simultaneously engaging in reflexivity. Since, beyond the primary research purposes, our acquisitions also served other, more personal aims (as 52% of respondents in Dupont 2021 declared having “*purchased tokens for purposes other than research*”), we will disclose our potential conflicts of interest at the conclusion of this introduction. For us, following Amato’s (2008) example—who was interested in online multiplayer games—only active practice allowed us to discover and internalize the “*involved competencies*”[^54] inherent to our field. This arrangement proved effective in acquiring and mastering a foundational set of knowledge, skills, and communicative abilities. Moreover, this immersion enabled us not to disrupt the “*initiatory moment*” of the “*first plunge*,” that initial conditioning and the “*original fascination, this grip*” described by the actors (*Ibid.*). Although these experiences do not claim to be ludic in the manner of video games, they are partly so, and it is undeniable that ours took on this dimension[^55]. The fascination elicited by the first BTC transaction sent, as described to us, was real for us as well. It combined anxiety and satisfaction: anxiety about having made an error in the procedure, thereby irreversibly losing the UCN sent; satisfaction in being able to execute a peer-to-peer transaction without a bank or hierarchical payment system and to monitor its processing in real time—the successive *confirmations* up to the *payment finality*—via an online-accessible *ledger explorer*. Thus, the choice of immersion was “*not merely a stance* [...] *a methodological or epistemological choice, but an inevitable condition of access to the field*” (Berry, 2012).

This initial phase of investigation, consisting of our documentary research and preliminary “participant immersions,” provided us with a representation of the relational networks at play, their supports, and the relevant spaces of their intertwinements. Our experiments confirmed the structuring of a field around a plurality of actors, activities, and developing economic sectors; socio-technical arrangements combining *off-protocol* and *within-protocol* logics; as well as constraints inherent to usage (downloading a *wallet* and securing the *cryptographic authentication* key, etc.). This phase enabled us to master more than just the practices and conventions prevailing *within the protocol* and allowed us to more confidently envisage conducting participant observations and interviews in the real world.

### Its “offline” counterpart: engaging with actors through observations and interviews

#### Participant observations of offline events: initial face-to-face contacts with human actors

The term “*participant immersion*,” in addition to sharing with “participant observation” the explicit emphasis on the interactive dimension of the method, allows us to delineate what pertains, for the former, to practices situated in “cyberspace” and, for the latter, to practices situated in the “real world” (Amato, 2008). In parallel with immersions in these new digital worlds—mediated simply by our computer connected to the Internet—we approached in the “real world” the individuals and organizations based in Paris (associations, groups organizing *meetup*-style events, etc.) involved in this field.

In the “real world,” we conducted 27 observations (see the inventory in Appendix No. 2), with an average duration of 4 hours and 30 minutes each. All observations pertain to collective community events (17 involving the Bitcoin community, 10 the Ethereum community). These observations encompass: so-called “social” meetings (“*Social meetup Bitcoin*”)—recurring events that allow participants to engage in informal exchanges; project or book presentation conferences, where interactions are more formal and the scope more limited (technical, economic, and financial issues, etc.); practical workshops (“create my first *smart contract* on Ethereum,” organized by Asseth); and international conferences (such as the “Breaking Bitcoin” conferences in Paris and Amsterdam, or ETHCC in Paris). We prioritized geographically proximate events due to access constraints. We attended the monthly *Meetups* of the Bitcoin community, registered as members of the Ethereum France association (formerly “Asseth”) from its inception, as well as members of the “Cercle du Coin.” We also took advantage of our seminar at the EHESS to invite practitioners from the community. These observations enabled us to meet numerous leading actors on the French cryptocurrency scene and, more generally, to engage with a diversity of technologies, uses, and communities. This integration into the Parisian “crypto network” led us to attend one or more international public events on Bitcoin and Ethereum, as well as to establish contacts for our interviews.

# D. Objectives, Contributions, and General Organization of the Thesis

In response to the question of whether cryptocurrencies (CM) are currencies that have succeeded in autonomizing themselves from the political sphere, we argue that, although they are currencies, the ideology surrounding them is counterfactual: cryptocurrencies, notably Bitcoin, are, like any currency, conflictual socio-political artefacts which, beyond pure technical governance (the realm of rules), pertain to political compromises that are always under (re)construction (the realm of discretion).

This overarching thesis is structured around specific problematics that respond to the technicist liberal claims of the coiners, recalled in the introduction to our discussion. These claims take the form of a syllogism stating that (i) since technology is autonomous and neutral with respect to the social world, and (ii) cryptocurrencies (CM) are purely technical currencies, then (iii) they are immune to human governance and its socio-political interests, which makes them (iv) “better” currencies than previous monetary forms, in particular national *fiat* currencies. Each premise of this syllogism encompasses singular problematics requiring specific deconstructive work. We counter the first two by arguing that technical neutrality is but an illusion, due to the normativity inherent in every socio-technical object. With a cryptocurrency, the architecture and protocol code are not neutral, not only because they incorporate the socio-political views of their designers, but also because these ideal views do not constitute a material content (protocol specifications and client software), which depends on hybrid and evolving compromises. A bare protocol does not make money; this requires an infrastructure: usages, other socio-technical arrangements to connect to it, maintenance, and evolutions that renegotiate its forms, contents, and normativity. This first step in demystifying the technical neutrality of cryptocurrencies will allow us to address the remaining premises, which concern their specifically monetary claims.  

We argue that cryptocurrencies do constitute money, according to our monetary institutionalism as well as their use in accounts and payments: parallel currencies to community localisms. From our perspective, it is impossible to assert from a vantage point the superiority of one currency over another, since their quality as “good” or “bad” depends on the judgment of the members of their payment communities and is constantly renegotiated in the course of controversies that are irreducibly political. At the level of actors, these controversies and judgments expressed about the desired properties of their cryptocurrency delineate a governance structure, not its absence. To interrogate this, we start from the crises of cryptocurrencies, or rather from their fabrication by the *coiners*. The labels, categories, regulatory and control devices for protocol modifications, means of containment, arenas of debate, and actors revealed are the hallmarks of a two-faced crisis governance, distinct yet complementary: a routine form of *“closed-door”* governance, where a local consensus among highly technical actors predominates *a priori*, as well as an absence of global dissensus; and a more exceptional *“public and open”* form, where the consensus sought is from the outset global due to the existence of real or supposed dissensus.  

Although the routine face appears technocratic, the maintenance activity of protocol codes by a college of specialists is highly regulated, which ensures responsibility, control, and the expression of dissensus. This dissensus leads governance to adopt an opposite form, open and public, characterized by a broad mobilization of community stakeholders. These stakeholders (whose scope is subject to debate) must participate in debates and decisions in pursuit of community legitimacy, which relies on diverse discussion arenas and devices for measuring consensus.

This dissertation aims to make two types of contributions. The first is empirical, through documentary research, participant immersion, surveys, and participant observation, enabling the documentation and mapping of the governance of the studied cryptocurrencies (CM). This approach allows for the preliminary formulation of categories and concepts within an academic context where data remain fragmentary. The second contribution is more theoretical, as this work also seeks to provide elements for characterizing cryptocurrencies and the actors involved in their governance. In particular, it intends to emphasize that the existence of these new forms of money cannot leave dominant monetary representations unaffected—representations that are partly shared by both *coiners* and their critics. Our work primarily addresses academic actors interested in the monetary field and all practitioners dealing with issues related to cryptocurrencies, digital currencies, and crypto-assets: those working in public or international administrations (tax authorities, ministries, central banks, etc.) or in the private sector (banking, finance, insurance). Finally, civil society actors (NGOs, associations, etc.), as well as anyone curious about the economic, social, and political stakes of these technologies, will find material for reflection.

This dissertation documents, maps, and analyzes the ecosystem and governance of Bitcoin and Ethereum from their origins until 2020.

The first chapter introduces Bitcoin and Ethereum by questioning the allegedly “technical” neutrality and autonomy of their protocols. Contrary to the “technologism” found in part of the literature, which focuses solely on their protocols, we situate them within their socio-historical context. We demonstrate that cryptocurrencies, as objects and even socio-technical infrastructures, are not purely technical, neutral, autonomous, and stabilized achievements, but rather negotiated and hybrid infrastructures: technology and the social world mutually influence each other through a political back-and-forth (Akrich 1989; Star 1999). These protocols incorporate social *a priori* composed of heterogeneous theoretical and practical inspirations. Moreover, a protocol alone does not constitute money: this status depends on its interaction with users, *via* infrastructural development, primarily off-chain, which enables the creation of circuits and connections, as well as the establishment of conversion spaces (wallets and gateways). This process is complex, multi-actor, and multi-level, allowing cryptocurrencies to evolve, adapt, extend their uses, and integrate into the monetary and financial system, thereby conferring upon them, through monetization, an exchange value.

We will thus examine the design and socio-technical arrangements of Bitcoin, emphasizing that each of the architectural choices made (in particular the PoW-based consensus) is irreducibly hybrid, negotiated, reflecting the inspirations and constraints of Nakamoto’s project, notably decentralization. By shifting the analysis towards users and usages, Bitcoin will appear as a “seamless” infrastructure, reducible neither to the intentions of its designer nor to its protocol boundaries, since it is continuously renegotiated by actors (reintermediation of certain processes and activities, disputes over protocol evolutions that eliminate previously possible uses deemed illegitimate, etc.). In the same vein, the presentation of Ethereum will highlight its normative aspects and its own infrastructural development, influenced by Bitcoin but enriched by critiques of what is perceived as its protocol and infrastructural rigidities. This aspiration to emancipation explains more radically differentiated socio-technical arrangements, demonstrating that cryptocurrencies cannot claim any form of technical neutrality.

The second chapter will present our contribution to the controversy surrounding the monetary status of cryptocurrencies. We will examine existing critiques of cryptocurrencies by situating them within their theoretical and epistemological context, emphasizing that they represent a test of the explicitation of money for the dominant monetary approaches underpinning these critiques: cryptocurrencies, like other monetary innovations before them, challenge the definition of money held by their detractors. Contrary to the opinion of the majority of economists and practitioners in this field (bankers and central bankers), we will affirm the monetary character of cryptocurrencies by adopting an institutionalist and ethnographic approach. We will show that, in their uses, cryptocurrencies possess the minimal characteristics of any money (they relate to debt, trust, and sovereignty), and integrate readily into the monetary field as parallel community monies, of which they represent a novel form.

We will offer a review of the various critiques leveled against Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies (CM), distinguishing two families of distinct theoretical foundations: the instrumental approach and the nominalist/chartalist approach. We will demonstrate that, from the perspective of a non-state monetary institutionalism concerned with usage, neither the canonical functions of money nor state exclusivity can exclude cryptocurrencies from the monetary domain: their use in accounting and payment is undeniable, especially since their unit of account (UCN) is the only one capable of expressing and settling the usage fees of their protocols. However, if Bitcoin and Ethereum qualify as money for us, this is neither in the sense adopted by the majority of economists and practitioners, nor in that of the *coiners*: they are monies not as replacements for national currencies, but as complements to them, with niche uses *despite* and even *because* of the prohibitive imperfections highlighted by critics (high volatility, use in illegal activities). Ultimately, contrary to the views of both *coiners* and their detractors, the singularity of cryptocurrencies within the monetary field does not lie in the absence of human governance, but rather in its presence: a dual and polycentric governance emerges, placing them in a new category of money distinct from existing private or public monies. Grounded in a nominalism maintained with discretion, it will be impossible for us to conclude on the “good” or “bad” qualities of Bitcoin or Ethereum, since these are assessed by their capacity to reproduce themselves legitimately in the eyes of actors, which depends on the quality of this polycentric governance that must ensure the definition and realization of collective objectives, as well as the management of conflicts introduced in this chapter.

The third chapter will analyze the governance of Bitcoin and Ethereum through the lens of their crises: the “Bitcoin Core CVE 2018 #17144” vulnerability for Bitcoin, and the Ethereum *hard fork* following the “The DAO” attack for Ethereum. The crisis inquiry (from *crisis onset* to *restoration of order*) will reveal both the normative and political dimensions of governance *by* the infrastructure, as well as governance *over* the infrastructure, along with its polycentric nature. Different types of crises challenge the normal functioning of the payment system, reflecting the existence of a crisis policy whose markers take the form of nomenclature, severity scales, discovery and disclosure procedures, modes of response, involved actors and procedures, arenas of action, discussions, and mechanisms for measuring community consensus.  

We will identify two families of crises based on the alignment between what the code permits and what the community expects from it — *vulnerability crises* and *evolution crises* — as well as two polar forms that governance can assume: “*closed-door*” crisis governance, which appears restricted to a body of technical experts, and “*public and open*” governance, inherently conflictual, mobilizing all community stakeholders. Regardless of the governance mode, the protocol consensus ultimately appears subject to a community consensus on its desired properties, associating more or less directly the community members and their diverse interests.

Starting from the Bitcoin CVE 2018 crisis, one infers that a governance structure *over* the infrastructure takes over when governance *by* it fails: it is primarily the production and behind-the-scenes, the routine face of a “*closed-door*” governance, that we have been able to document and analyze. In its “*closed-door*” form, governance *over* the protocol illuminates the “infra-political” dimensions, based on trust and delegation, of maintaining the codes of the hegemonic implementation “Bitcoin Core,” and the centrality assumed by the restricted group of “core developers” (so-called “core devs”), without, however, being technocratic. Although the reordering was carried out secretly and without publicity, according to a local consensus among networked actors (the “core devs” and “mining pool” operators), community regulations and controls do exist. Due to the critical nature of the activities surrounding the protocol codes and the powers wielded over them by certain community members (the “core developers”), governance offers users certain guarantees against discretionary, non-consensual rule changes.  

Analyzing governance *over* the infrastructure of a cryptocurrency must not stop at the routine face of crisis governance since, absent clear dissent, the consensus surrounding code modifications is primarily local, tacit, and unequivocal. This is not the case for *evolutionary* crises, such as the *hard fork* following the “The DAO” attack in the Ethereum community, which inevitably lead to debates, or even conflicts, resolved on a “grand stage,” publicly and with great noise. It is during the rare and intermittent expression of this second face of governance that all community components (end users, exchanges, service operators, etc.) don the roles of counter-power, and all community mechanisms aimed at ensuring the production of consensus (and the expression of dissent) are mobilized.
